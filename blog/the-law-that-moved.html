The Law You Cited May Not Be the Law Any More

The ImpactMojo Team August 26, 2026 7 min read
law governance research

A judgment that stopped existing

In May 2025 the Supreme Court struck down the two instruments that let a project built without environmental clearance be regularised afterwards. If you were advising a community against a factory that had already been put up, that judgment was the strongest card you had.

In November 2025 it was recalled on review, two judges to one. It stopped existing. On 29 July 2026 the Court reheard the matter and split the difference: the 2017 Notification is valid, the 2021 Office Memorandum is quashed prospectively, clearances already granted stand unless someone challenges them one by one, and no fresh applications will be entertained.

Count the months. For six of them the May 2025 judgment was the law, and citing it was right. For the eight after that it did not exist, and citing it was wrong — anyone who briefed a community, drafted an objection or planned a campaign in that window was working from a document that had been withdrawn. Nothing told them. Judgments do not send notifications, and the older, stronger-sounding ruling stays exactly where it was: in the PDF you downloaded, in the training deck, in the annexure to the proposal.

The Vanashakti litigation, both ends of which are now in the Docket — each pointing at the other.

This is ordinary, not exceptional

Take Pune Municipal Corporation (2014). It was the authority on which a great many old land acquisitions were reopened and land returned to owners, and for six years it was cited that way with confidence. In March 2020 a larger Bench in Indore Development Authority overruled it and narrowed the rule sharply.

Advice given in 2019 and never revisited is wrong advice today. It is not obviously wrong. It reads exactly as it did when it was right.

Why our sector gets caught by this specifically

Three things, and none of them is carelessness.

The documents that carry law in development work are long-lived. A safeguarding policy, an MoU, a community rights training deck, the legal annexure of a grant application. They are written once, approved, and copied forward for years. Nobody re-reads the citations at renewal, because renewal is about budgets.

Nobody in the room is a lawyer. The person who put the citation in three years ago has moved on. What survives is a case name and a sentence summarising it, both of which look permanent.

Checking used to cost money. The commercial legal databases charge for access, courts published inconsistently, and a search by party name would return a confident, wrong answer. That last part is worth spelling out: searching for the well-known Thalappalam right-to-information case by party name returns, as its top result, a different Kerala High Court matter entirely. Not an error message. A plausible decoy.

The third reason has stopped being true

Over the past year the primary sources have become genuinely open, and it is worth knowing what is now free.

The Supreme Court's own judgments are published as open data. The signed PDFs sit in a public bucket alongside an index that carries, for every judgment, its neutral citation, its CNR number, the decision date, the presiding judge and the disposal. You can read it anonymously, with no key and no account. That index is what lets you confirm a case's identity rather than trust a search result.

India Open Law, released under the Apache licence by vaquill-AI, publishes 12,848,644 judgments from the Supreme Court and 25 High Courts covering 1950 to 2025, together with 813,168 tribunal matters across fifteen forums — the National Green Tribunal, NCLT, CAT, CESTAT, ITAT, DRT, SAT, CCI, APTEL, TDSAT, RERA, IBBI, the GST advance ruling authorities and ATFP — and 22,265 enactments. For anyone working on environment, land, labour or company law, the tribunal set matters more than the Supreme Court one: that is where most of these matters actually live.

District court decisions are published too, on the eCourts services, court by court. They are simply not aggregated the way the higher courts now are. We should say plainly that we got this wrong on this site: the Docket previously stated that district-level decisions are not published. They are. We filed that against ourselves as issue #1023 and corrected it.

The two questions that get collapsed into one

When people do check, they usually check one thing: is this still good law? That is the right question. It is not the only one.

A separate question is whether the court is still doing anything in the matter. A judgment can be perfectly good law and the case still open, with the same court issuing fresh directions year after year. The right-to-food litigation works like this. So does the migrant-workers matter, and the forest-governance case.

The Docket keeps these two as separate fields, because collapsing them is how a live matter gets cited as a closed one.

The cost of collapsing them is concrete. Cite the original manual-scavenging judgment and you have the principle. The compliance order of January 2026 is where the ₹30 lakh figure for a sewer death is actually being claimed, case by case, with a district survey still unfinished and a court that has not let go. If your submission stops at the 2023 judgment, you are asking for less than the law now gives you.

Four things worth checking, before you cite

We are not lawyers and this is not legal advice. It is the checklist we ended up using ourselves.

The neutral citation. 2024 INSC 866 is an address. A case name is a description, and descriptions collide — several unrelated matters share a party name. If a source gives you a case name and no citation, you cannot yet confirm you are looking at the right document.

Whether the holding still stands. Overruled, modified, partly overruled, under reference to a larger Bench, recalled. Any of these can have happened since your document was written.

Whether the court is still seized of the matter. If it is, the current obligations are in the latest order, not the original judgment.

The date you last checked. Write it next to the citation. It converts “we cited a case” into “we cited a case, as it stood on this date” — which is a true statement that stays true.

What we built, and what it is not

The Development Law Docket holds 109 Indian judgments that changed what development organisations, state programmes and rights-holders can actually do. Every entry carries both fields above, and both are filterable, so you can pull up only the matters a court is still running, or only the holdings that have been narrowed since they were decided. Each entry's case name and decision year were checked against the judgment text; the most recent six link to the Supreme Court's own signed PDF and carry its neutral citation, CNR, date and presiding judge as printed in the document.

Three honest limits. Undecided matters are not in any judgments index, because there is no judgment yet — the petitions on drug pricing, on wildlife protection and on gig-work social security are live and consequential and invisible to this method. High Court and tribunal matters need different handling from Supreme Court ones, and the Docket is thin on both. And 109 judgments out of nearly thirteen million is a reading list, not a database. Where the two disagree, the linked judgment is authoritative and we are not.

The point of the Docket is narrower than it might look. It is not there to tell you what the law is. It is there to stop a judgment sitting in your files looking current when it has not been current for a year.
