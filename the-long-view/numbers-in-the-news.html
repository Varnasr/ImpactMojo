Numbers in the News

A reader's field guide to the statistics that pass for facts. Eight ways numbers mislead — each with a small interactive example you can poke at. Learn to spot them, and you read the news more honestly.

How to read this

Most misleading numbers aren't lies

They are true numbers framed to point the wrong way. A real count, a real percentage, a real chart — arranged so the obvious reading is the wrong one. You don't need statistics to defend yourself; you need a handful of questions. Here are the eight that catch most of it. All figures below are illustrative, chosen to make the mechanism visible.

The missing denominator

A big number out of what?

"State A had 5,000 road deaths last year; State B had 1,200." So State A is more dangerous? Only if the two states are the same size. A count means nothing without the population it came from. Switch the view.

Ask: out of how many? A count tells you size; a rate tells you risk. The news loves counts because they sound bigger.

Percent vs percentage points

"Risk doubled" — from what to what?

A drug that takes your risk from 2% to 4% has doubled your risk (+100%, relative) and raised it by 2 percentage points (absolute). Both are true. One sells papers. Enter any two rates and see both framings.

from% to%

Ask: percent of what base? A scary relative rise on a tiny base is a tiny absolute change.

Base rates & false positives

A "99% accurate" test that's usually wrong

A test for a rare condition is 99% accurate. You test positive. Your chance of actually having it can still be under 1-in-2 — because when the condition is rare, false positives outnumber true ones. Drag the prevalence and watch.

How common the condition is: 1 in 1,000 · test sensitivity & specificity fixed at 99%

Ask: how common is it to begin with? Accuracy without the base rate is half the story — this is Bayes' theorem in everyday clothes.

The truncated axis

A cliff that's really a gentle slope

The same four numbers — 48, 49, 50, 52 — can look like a dramatic surge or a near-flat line, depending only on where the y-axis starts. Toggle the baseline.

Ask: where does the axis start? A bar chart should almost always start at zero. When it doesn't, someone is amplifying a small difference.

The cherry-picked baseline

"Since 20XX, things have..." — pick the X

Give me the freedom to choose the starting year and I can make this wobbly series look like steady progress or steady decline — without changing a single data point. Slide the start year.

Start the story in: 2009

Ask: why that start year? An honest trend shows the whole series, not the slice that flatters the argument.

Sample size & margin of error

A "2-point lead" that's really a tie

A poll says 51% to 49%. With a small sample, the margin of error swamps the gap — the "lead" is noise. Grow the sample and watch the uncertainty shrink.

People surveyed: 400

Ask: how many people, and what's the margin of error? If the gap is smaller than the margin, there is no gap.

Correlation isn't causation

The third thing nobody mentioned

"Children who own more books score higher" — so buy books? Maybe. But richer, more-educated households have both more books and higher scores. The books may be a marker, not a cause. The hidden third variable — the confounder — is where most "X causes Y" headlines quietly fail.

Ask: what else could explain both? A genuine cause survives the question "what's the confounder?" — see ImpactMojo's work on causal inference.

Survivorship & selection

Counting only the winners

"Most successful founders dropped out of college." But you only hear about the founders who succeeded — the far larger number of dropouts who failed never make the article. When the sample is "those who survived to be counted," the lesson is usually wrong. The planes that came back from war were the ones that could.

Ask: who's missing from this data? The failures, the dropouts, the silent — selection decides the story before the numbers do.

The pocket checklist

Eight questions for any number

  • Out of how many? — a count needs a denominator to mean anything.
  • Percent of what base? — relative vs absolute change are different stories.
  • How common is it to begin with? — accuracy without the base rate misleads.
  • Where does the axis start? — a truncated axis amplifies small gaps.
  • Why that start year? — a cherry-picked baseline manufactures trends.
  • How big is the sample? — if the gap is under the margin of error, it's noise.
  • What's the confounder? — correlation rarely proves causation.
  • Who's missing from the data? — selection and survivorship hide the failures.
