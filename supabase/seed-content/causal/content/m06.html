Module 6: Instrumental Variables

Matching assumes you measured every confounder. Instrumental variables abandons that hope. Instead it finds a source of variation in treatment that is as good as random — a nudge that moves take-up but touches the outcome only through it. IV can identify a causal effect even with unmeasured confounding, at the price of strong, mostly untestable assumptions and a narrower estimand.

What an Instrument Must Satisfy

An instrument \(Z\) for the effect of \(D\) on \(Y\) must clear three bars (four, with heterogeneous effects):

  • 1Relevance. \(Z\) actually shifts treatment: \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z,D)\neq 0\). This one is testable — it is the first stage.
  • 2Independence. \(Z\) is as good as randomly assigned (conditional on covariates): unrelated to the potential outcomes and confounders.
  • 3Exclusion. \(Z\) affects \(Y\) only through \(D\) — no direct path. Untestable, and where most IV claims live or die.
  • 4Monotonicity. \(Z\) moves everyone toward treatment in the same direction (no “defiers”). Needed to interpret the estimand.
The exclusion restriction is the missing arrow: there is no direct \(Z \to Y\) path, and no \(Z \leftrightarrow U\) link. \(U\) confounds \(D\) and \(Y\) freely — IV survives that.

The Estimator and What It Estimates

With a single binary instrument, the IV (Wald) estimator is the ratio of reduced form to first stage:

\[ \widehat{\beta}_{\text{IV}} \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Z,Y)}{\operatorname{Cov}(Z,D)} \;=\; \frac{E[Y\mid Z{=}1]-E[Y\mid Z{=}0]}{E[D\mid Z{=}1]-E[D\mid Z{=}0]} \]
Numerator: how much the instrument moved the outcome. Denominator: how much it moved take-up. Two-stage least squares generalises this to covariates and multiple instruments.
It is a LATE, not the ATE

Imbens & Angrist (1994): under the four conditions, IV identifies the Local Average Treatment Effect — the effect on compliers, the units whose treatment the instrument actually moved. Never-takers and always-takers contribute nothing. A different instrument can give a different (equally valid) number, because it recruits different compliers.

Worked example — an encouragement design

You cannot force people to open a bank account, so you randomly send encouragement (\(Z\): a fee waiver + assistance). Assignment is random (independence holds by design); it plausibly affects savings only by raising account take-up (exclusion).

QuantityValue
Reduced form: savings gap, encouraged − not (ITT)+₹320
First stage: account take-up gap+0.40
IV / LATE = 320 / 0.40+₹800

The ₹800 is the effect of having an account for the 40% of people the encouragement moved — not for everyone, and not for those who would have opened one anyway.

Weak Instruments

If the first stage is weak (\(Z\) barely moves \(D\)), the denominator is near zero, 2SLS is badly biased toward OLS, and confidence intervals are unreliable. Report the first-stage F-statistic; the old “F > 10” rule is now known to be too lenient (Lee, McCrary, Moreira & Porter 2022), so prefer weak-instrument-robust inference (e.g. Anderson–Rubin) when F is modest.

## R — 2SLS with a first-stage diagnostic (fixest)
library(fixest)
fit <- feols(savings ~ 1 | account ~ encouraged, data = d)
summary(fit, stage = 1)                # first stage + F-stat
fitstat(fit, "ivf")                    # weak-instrument F
* Stata — 2SLS and weak-IV checks
ivregress 2sls savings (account = encouraged), first
estat firststage          // Cragg-Donald / effective F

Pitfalls. Exclusion is an assumption, not a finding — “rainfall” or “distance” instruments often fail it (rain affects income through many channels, not just the treatment). A “clever” instrument with a strong first stage but a leaky exclusion is worse than honest OLS, because it hides the bias behind machinery.

Practice
  1. To estimate the effect of irrigation on yields, a study uses a village's distance to the nearest river as an instrument for irrigation. State the exclusion restriction and give one realistic way it could fail.
    Solution
    Exclusion: river distance affects yields only through irrigation. It can fail because riverside land is also more fertile, flood-prone, better connected to markets, or differently settled — all direct channels to yields that bypass irrigation. Then distance is not a valid instrument and IV is biased.
  2. Two valid instruments for schooling give LATEs of 6% and 10% return. A referee calls this a contradiction. Are they right?
    Solution
    Not necessarily. Each instrument identifies the effect on its own compliers. If returns are heterogeneous, different instruments move different people and yield different (both valid) LATEs. The “contradiction” is information about heterogeneity, not a failure — provided each instrument's assumptions hold.
  3. Your first-stage F is 4. Why should you not simply report the 2SLS point estimate and its usual standard error?
    Solution
    A weak first stage makes 2SLS biased toward OLS and its normal-approximation confidence interval severely under-covers. Report weak-instrument-robust inference (e.g. Anderson–Rubin confidence sets), reconsider the instrument, or treat the design as uninformative about the effect.
