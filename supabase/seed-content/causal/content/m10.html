Module 10: Causal Machine Learning

Machine learning is superb at prediction: fit flexible functions of many covariates. Causal questions are different — they ask about an intervention, not a pattern. Causal ML borrows ML's flexibility for the parts of a causal problem that are really prediction (modelling confounding, modelling heterogeneity) while protecting the causal estimate from the bias that naive ML would introduce. It changes how we estimate; it does not change what we must assume.

ML does not solve identification

A random forest predicting \(Y\) from \(D\) and \(X\) gives an association, however accurate. Without a design (randomisation, unconfoundedness, an instrument, a discontinuity) it is not a causal effect. Causal ML still needs the assumptions of Modules 2–9 — it makes the estimation under those assumptions more flexible and less hand-tuned.

Double / Debiased Machine Learning

Suppose unconfoundedness holds given a high-dimensional \(X\). Plugging an ML estimate of \(E[Y\mid X]\) straight into an effect estimate transmits the ML model's regularisation bias. Chernozhukov et al. (2018) fix this with two devices. First, Neyman-orthogonal (partialling-out) moments: residualise both the outcome and the treatment on \(X\), then relate the residuals.

\[ \tilde{Y}_i = Y_i - \hat{\ell}(X_i),\quad \tilde{D}_i = D_i - \hat{m}(X_i),\qquad \widehat{\theta} = \frac{\sum_i \tilde{D}_i\,\tilde{Y}_i}{\sum_i \tilde{D}_i^{\,2}} \]
\(\hat\ell\) and \(\hat m\) are ML predictions of the outcome and the propensity. Orthogonality makes \(\widehat\theta\) insensitive to small errors in them. Second, cross-fitting: estimate the nuisance functions on one fold and the effect on another, so the model never “sees” the points it scores — removing overfitting bias.

Heterogeneous Effects: Who Benefits?

A single ATE hides variation. The conditional average treatment effect asks how the effect depends on characteristics:

\[ \tau(x) \;=\; E[\,Y(1) - Y(0)\mid X = x\,] \]
Causal forests (Wager & Athey 2018; Athey, Tibshirani & Wager 2019) estimate \(\tau(x)\) with honest, cross-fit trees — useful for targeting: directing a scarce programme to the households the evidence says gain most.

Discipline for heterogeneity. Subgroup hunting invites false discoveries. Causal forests guard against this with sample-splitting and honesty, but you should still pre-specify the question, validate discovered subgroups out-of-sample, and treat \(\tau(x)\) estimates as hypotheses for targeting, not proven facts about every cell.

Worked example — targeting a livelihood grant

A grant raises incomes by ₹1,500/month on average. A causal forest on a randomised pilot estimates \(\tau(x)\) and finds the gain concentrated among households with some prior business experience and low baseline assets; for asset-rich households the effect is near zero. Targeting the grant by these features could raise total impact at the same budget — a hypothesis to confirm in the scale-up, not a guarantee.

dml$fit(); dml$summary()                 # cross-fit, orthogonal ATE

library(grf)
cf  <- causal_forest(X, Y, W)            # W = treatment
average_treatment_effect(cf)            # ATE with valid SE
tau <- predict(cf)$predictions           # CATE per unit, for targeting

Practice
  1. A colleague trains a gradient-boosted model that predicts programme outcomes with 92% accuracy and concludes the programme “causes” the outcome. What is the error?
    Solution
    Predictive accuracy is not identification. The model captures associations among \(D\), \(X\) and \(Y\); without a design ruling out confounding, the “effect of \(D\)” it reports is biased by the same unobservables as OLS. ML improves the fit, not the causal claim.
  2. What two ingredients make double ML “debiased,” and what does each fix?
    Solution
    (1) Neyman-orthogonal (partialling-out) moments make the effect estimate first-order insensitive to errors in the ML nuisance functions, neutralising regularisation bias. (2) Cross-fitting estimates nuisances and the effect on separate folds, removing overfitting bias from reusing the same data. Together they restore valid inference with ML components.
  3. A causal forest reports a large effect for one narrow subgroup not specified in advance. How should you treat it?
    Solution
    As a hypothesis. Post-hoc subgroups risk false positives; honesty/sample-splitting reduces but does not eliminate the risk. Validate on held-out data or a new sample, check that the subgroup is policy-meaningful and stable, and adjust for multiple comparisons before acting on it.
