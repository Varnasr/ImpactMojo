Module 2: Selection Bias and the Identifying Assumption

Module 1 showed that the naive comparison equals the effect plus selection bias. Every method in this course is a strategy for setting that bias term to zero. The first strategy is the most demanding: assume that, once you control for the right observed characteristics, who got treated is as good as random. This module states that assumption exactly, shows what it buys, and shows why it is so hard to defend.

The Identifying Assumption

Adjustment methods rest on one condition, written several ways — conditional independence, unconfoundedness, ignorability, or “selection on observables”. All say the same thing: within cells defined by observed covariates \(X\), treatment is unrelated to the potential outcomes.

\[ \{\,Y(0),\,Y(1)\,\} \;\perp\; D \;\mid\; X \]
Conditional Independence Assumption (CIA). Among units with the same \(X\), the treated and untreated are comparable: their untreated outcomes would have had the same distribution.

A second, easily forgotten, condition is needed for the comparison to exist at all:

\[ 0 \;<\; \Pr(D=1 \mid X) \;<\; 1 \quad\text{for all }X \]
Overlap (common support). At every value of \(X\) you must see both treated and untreated units. Without it there is no one to compare, and the model silently extrapolates.
What it buys you
Identification of the ATT
Under CIA + overlap, \( \text{ATT} = E_X\!\big[\,E[Y\mid D{=}1,X] - E[Y\mid D{=}0,X]\,\big] \). Match like with like on \(X\), difference within cells, then average.

Selection on Observables vs Unobservables

The DAG makes the bet visible. If the only thing driving both treatment and outcome is observed (\(X\)), conditioning on it closes the backdoor path and the bias disappears. If anything unobserved (\(U\)) does so, no amount of controlling for \(X\) helps.

Left: the confounder is observed, so adjustment works. Right: the confounder is unobserved (motivation, ability, household connections), so “controlling for what we have” leaves bias behind.

How Big Is the Bias? The Omitted-Variable Formula

Regress \(Y\) on \(D\) leaving out a confounder \(U\). The short-regression coefficient is the long-regression coefficient plus a bias term:

\[ \widehat{\beta}_{\text{short}} \;\xrightarrow{p}\; \beta_{\text{true}} \;+\; \underbrace{\delta_{U}\,\cdot\,\gamma_{U\to D}}_{\text{omitted-variable bias}} \]
Bias = (effect of \(U\) on \(Y\)) × (association of \(U\) with \(D\)). It vanishes only if \(U\) does not affect the outcome, or is unrelated to treatment. “Controlling for more” helps only to the extent the added variable stands in for \(U\).
Worked example — a skilling programme

A youth skilling programme reports a ₹1,800/month earnings premium for graduates over non-graduates. Graduates are younger, better-educated, and urban. Adjusting for these observed differences shrinks the estimate; what remains still rests on assuming nothing unobserved (drive, family contacts) separates the groups.

SpecificationEstimated premium (₹/month)Identifying bet
Raw difference in means+1,800none — pure selection
+ age, education, urban, sector+700CIA on these covariates
+ baseline earnings (pre-programme)+450CIA incl. prior trajectory

The estimate falls by three-quarters as controls accumulate. The honest report states the last number and the assumption it requires — not the first.

In Code

## R — adjustment by regression, and a balance check
m_raw  <- lm(earnings ~ trained, data = d)
m_adj  <- lm(earnings ~ trained + age + educ + urban + base_earn, data = d)
summary(m_adj)$coef["trained", ]
## overlap: propensity distribution should overlap across groups
ps <- glm(trained ~ age + educ + urban + base_earn, family = binomial, data = d)$fitted
tapply(ps, d$trained, summary)   # check common support before trusting the estimate
* Stata — same adjustment and an overlap check
regress earnings i.trained
regress earnings i.trained age educ i.urban base_earn
logit trained age educ i.urban base_earn
predict ps, pr
by trained, sort: summarize ps      // do the supports overlap?

Pitfalls. CIA is an untestable assumption — balance on observables cannot prove balance on unobservables. Never control for a variable affected by treatment (a mediator or post-treatment control); Module 3 shows it can create bias rather than remove it. And a precise estimate off no overlap is precise extrapolation, not evidence.

Practice
  1. State the CIA in words for a programme that gives interest-free loans to women who apply. Name one plausible unobserved \(U\) that would break it.
    Solution
    CIA: among applicants with the same observed \(X\) (age, assets, prior income), getting the loan is unrelated to what their business outcomes would have been either way. A natural breaker is entrepreneurial drive or risk tolerance — unobserved, it raises both the chance of applying/qualifying and later profits, so it sits in \(U\) and the adjusted estimate stays biased upward.
  2. Using the omitted-variable formula, explain why adding household wealth as a control could either raise or lower the estimated treatment effect.
    Solution
    The bias term is \(\delta_U\gamma_{U\to D}\). If wealthier households are likelier to take up (\(\gamma>0\)) and wealth raises the outcome (\(\delta>0\)), omitting wealth biases the estimate up, so adding it lowers the coefficient. If the poor are targeted (\(\gamma<0\)) while wealth still raises the outcome, the omitted bias is negative and adding wealth raises the coefficient. Sign depends on both directions.
  3. Why is overlap as important as CIA? Sketch what an estimator does when \(\Pr(D{=}1\mid X)\approx 1\) for some \(X\).
    Solution
    With no untreated units at that \(X\), there is no empirical counterfactual; a regression supplies one by extrapolating its functional form into a region with no data. The “estimate” there is an artefact of the model, not a comparison. Good practice trims to the region of common support and reports the estimand for that region.
