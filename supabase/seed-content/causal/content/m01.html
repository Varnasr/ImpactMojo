Module 1: The Counterfactual Problem

Almost every claim in development practice is causal. A transfer cut stunting. A bridge raised attendance. A guarantee lifted rural wages. The difficulty is that the comparison which would settle such a claim cannot be run, and the comparison that can be run usually answers a different question. This module makes that problem precise, so that every later design can be read as one more attempt to solve it.

The Comparison You Cannot Run

Take one household that worked on an MGNREGA site this year. Call its consumption Y. To know whether the work caused that consumption, you would need to see the same household, in the same year, having not worked. That world does not exist. You observe one outcome, never both.

The fundamental problem

A causal effect is a comparison between two outcomes, only one of which you ever observe. This is not a small-sample problem that more data fixes; it is a missing-data problem built into the definition. Holland (1986) named it the fundamental problem of causal inference.

The Potential-Outcomes Model

Fix a unit i and a binary treatment Di ∈ {0,1}. Define two potential outcomes: \(Y_i(1)\), the outcome if i is treated, and \(Y_i(0)\), the outcome if it is not. Each unit has both; reality reveals only one. This notation is due to Splawa-Neyman (1923) for experiments and Rubin (1974) for observational settings.

\[ Y_i \;=\; D_i\,Y_i(1) \;+\; (1-D_i)\,Y_i(0) \]
The observed outcome is a switch. For a treated unit the second term vanishes; for an untreated unit the first does. The other half is the counterfactual you must reconstruct.

The individual treatment effect is the difference between a unit's two potential outcomes,

\[ \tau_i \;=\; Y_i(1) - Y_i(0), \]
which is never observed for anyone, because only one potential outcome is ever realised. Causal inference is therefore unavoidably about averages, not individuals.

Three Estimands — Name Yours First

Since individual effects are out of reach, we target averages. Three appear constantly, and they are not the same number:

Estimands
ATE — average treatment effect
\( \text{ATE} = E[\,Y(1)-Y(0)\,] \), the effect averaged over everyone.
ATT — effect on the treated
\( \text{ATT} = E[\,Y(1)-Y(0)\mid D=1\,] \), averaged over those who actually took the treatment.
ATU — effect on the untreated
\( \text{ATU} = E[\,Y(1)-Y(0)\mid D=0\,] \), the effect the non-participants would have had.

“Did MGNREGA help the people who used it?” is an ATT question. “Should we extend the guarantee to new districts?” is closer to ATU or ATE. A design that recovers the ATT says little about the second question if effects differ across groups. Naming the estimand before choosing a method is the discipline this course keeps.

Why the Naive Comparison Misleads

Compare the average outcome of the treated with that of the untreated and call the gap the effect. Adding and subtracting \(E[Y(0)\mid D=1]\) — the counterfactual mean for the treated — following Angrist & Pischke (2009, ch. 2):

\[ \underbrace{E[Y\mid D=1]-E[Y\mid D=0]}_{\text{naive gap}} \;=\; \underbrace{E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid D=1]}_{\text{ATT}} \;+\; \underbrace{E[Y(0)\mid D=1]-E[Y(0)\mid D=0]}_{\text{selection bias}} \]
The braced selection-bias term asks: how would the treated have done untreated, compared with how the untreated actually did? It is rarely zero, and you cannot sign it in advance.
Selection as confounding. An unobserved U (here, how poor a household is) drives both whether it works on a site (D) and its consumption (Y). The dashed node is unobserved. Module 3 makes this language exact.

SUTVA: The Assumption Hiding in the Notation

Writing \(Y_i(1)\) and \(Y_i(0)\) already assumes that unit i's outcome depends only on its own treatment, and that “treatment” means one well-defined thing. That is the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (Rubin 1980).

No interference. Imbert & Papp (2015) show MGNREGA raised private rural wages even for households that never worked a single day on a site. The treatment spilled through the labour market onto the “untreated”, contaminating any control group built from them.

No hidden variations of treatment. A scheme paid promptly is not the same treatment as one paid late with leakage. If take-up bundles different doses, \(\tau_i\) is not well defined.

Worked example — selection can flip the sign

Suppose the true ATT of an MGNREGA spell is +400 rupees of monthly consumption. But participants are poorer: even untreated they would have consumed 600 less than non-participants. The naive gap mixes the two.

QuantityValue (₹/month)
True effect on the treated (ATT)+400
Selection bias \(\;E[Y(0)\mid D{=}1]-E[Y(0)\mid D{=}0]\)−600
Naive participant − non-participant gap−200

The naive number says the programme hurt people. It helped. Selection ran the other way and swamped the effect — the default whenever take-up is voluntary and correlated with the outcome.

Seeing It in Code

Simulate a programme where poorer households are likelier to take up, then watch the naive estimate diverge from the true ATT of 400.

## R — selection bias in a simulated programme
set.seed(2026)
n        <- 20000
baseline <- rnorm(n, 3000, 800)               # latent consumption
p_take   <- plogis(-(baseline - 3000) / 400)  # poorer => likelier to work
D        <- rbinom(n, 1, p_take)
Y        <- baseline + 400 * D                 # true ATT = 400
naive    <- mean(Y[D == 1]) - mean(Y[D == 0])
cat(sprintf("Naive gap: %.0f   |   True ATT: 400\n", naive))
# Naive gap is strongly negative — selection, not the programme.
* Stata — same experiment
set seed 2026
set obs 20000
gen baseline = rnormal(3000, 800)
gen p_take   = invlogit(-(baseline - 3000)/400)
gen D        = runiform() < p_take
gen Y        = baseline + 400*D          // true ATT = 400
mean Y if D==1
mean Y if D==0                            // compare the two means

Common pitfalls. (1) Reporting a before-vs-after change as “impact” — that compares \(Y\) over time, not \(Y(1)\) against \(Y(0)\). (2) Assuming a large sample removes bias; it only sharpens a biased number. (3) Quoting an ATT and answering an ATE question, or the reverse.

Practice — work these before Module 2
  1. A district reports that anganwadi children who attended a nutrition camp grew 0.3 cm more last year than those who did not. Write this claim in potential-outcomes notation and state the one quantity you would need but cannot observe.
    Solution
    The claim compares observed \(E[Y\mid D{=}1]\) and \(E[Y\mid D{=}0]\). The effect on attenders is \(E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid D{=}1]\) (the ATT). The missing quantity is \(E[Y(0)\mid D{=}1]\): how the attenders would have grown had they not attended. Parents who send children to camps differ, so non-attenders are a poor stand-in for it.
  2. In the decomposition above, give one realistic story for an MGNREGA evaluation in which selection bias is positive (the naive gap overstates the ATT).
    Solution
    If sites open mainly in better-connected villages, participants may have higher untreated consumption than non-participants, so \(E[Y(0)\mid D{=}1] > E[Y(0)\mid D{=}0]\) and the bias is positive. The sign of selection is an empirical question about who takes up, not a constant.
  3. Give a concrete SUTVA violation for a sanitation programme that builds toilets in half the households of a village, and say which potential outcome it makes ill-defined.
    Solution
    Disease transmission is communal: a treated household's diarrhoea outcome depends on whether neighbours also got toilets (a spillover / herd effect). Then \(Y_i(0)\) is not unique — it depends on others' treatment — so the “no interference” part of SUTVA fails and the unit-level effect is ill-defined.
Varna
When a funder asks “did it work?”, they almost always mean the ATT — but they write indicators as if they mean the ATE. In coaching we pin the estimand to the decision the evidence has to serve, before a single indicator is drafted. It saves a great deal of arguing at the review meeting.
