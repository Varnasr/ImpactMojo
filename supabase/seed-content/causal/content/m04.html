Module 4: Randomisation and the RCT

The first three modules built a problem and an assumption that is hard to defend. Randomisation defends it by construction: if a coin decides who is treated, the treated and untreated are comparable in everything — observed and unobserved alike. That is the whole power of the randomised controlled trial, and also the source of its limits.

Why Randomisation Works

Assign \(D\) by a fair lottery and treatment is, by design, independent of the potential outcomes — not just within \(X\)-cells, but unconditionally:

\[ \{\,Y(0),\,Y(1)\,\} \;\perp\; D \]
The selection-bias term from Module 1 is zero in expectation, because \(E[Y(0)\mid D{=}1]=E[Y(0)\mid D{=}0]\). The control group's average outcome is the treated group's missing counterfactual.

It follows that the simplest possible estimator is unbiased for the ATE:

\[ \widehat{\text{ATE}} \;=\; \bar{Y}_{\,\text{treated}} - \bar{Y}_{\,\text{control}} \]
A difference in means. No modelling of the outcome, no assumption about functional form, no list of controls to defend. This is why the RCT is the reference design.

What Randomisation Does and Does Not Buy

Design choices that matter
Balance, not proof
Randomisation balances groups in expectation. Report a baseline balance table; a few imbalances by chance are normal. Covariates can be added to the regression to raise precision, but they are not needed to remove bias.
Stratification / blocking
Randomise within strata (e.g. by district and baseline score) to guarantee balance on the variables that matter most and to cut variance.
Clustering
If you randomise whole villages or schools, units within a cluster are correlated. Use cluster-robust standard errors and power the study on the number of clusters, not individuals.

Intention-to-Treat vs the Treated

People assigned to treatment do not always take it. Two estimands diverge:

\[ \text{LATE} \;=\; \frac{\text{ITT effect on the outcome}}{\text{effect of assignment on take-up}} \;=\; \frac{\bar{Y}_{Z=1}-\bar{Y}_{Z=0}}{\bar{D}_{Z=1}-\bar{D}_{Z=0}} \]
The intention-to-treat (ITT) compares as assigned — the policy-relevant number when take-up is itself part of the programme. Dividing by the take-up gap rescales it to the effect on compliers (the LATE of Module 6). Never compare only those who actually complied: that re-introduces selection.
Worked example — incentives for immunisation (Rajasthan)

Banerjee, Duflo, Glennerster & Kothari (2010) randomised villages in Udaipur to reliable monthly immunisation camps, with or without a small incentive (a kilo of lentils per shot). Full immunisation of children rose dramatically — and the incentive arm did better despite “paying” people, because it offset the cost of repeat visits.

Each number is a difference in means against the randomised control — no modelling required — and the design answers a policy question (does a small incentive beat exhortation?) that no observational comparison could settle credibly.

library(estimatr)
fit <- lm_robust(immunised ~ incentive,
                 fixed_effects = ~ strata,
                 clusters = village, data = d)
tidy(fit)["incentive", c("estimate","std.error","conf.low","conf.high")]
# Minimum detectable effect at 80% power for a two-arm cluster design:

Threats even in an RCT. Differential attrition (treatment changes who you can find at endline) re-opens selection. Spillovers from treated to control violate SUTVA. And an RCT identifies the effect for the studied population — external validity (will it travel?) is a separate, judgement-heavy question taken up in Module 11.

Practice
  1. A reviewer says “your treatment and control differ on baseline literacy, so the randomisation failed.” When is this objection wrong, and how do you answer it?
    Solution
    With many baseline variables, a few significant differences are expected by chance even under valid randomisation. The objection is wrong if the imbalance is within what chance predicts and the assignment was genuinely random. Answer by reporting the full balance table, noting the expected number of “significant” differences, and showing the estimate is robust to controlling for the imbalanced covariate (which should barely move it).
  2. Only 60% of those offered a job-training place attend; the rest do not. You find attendees out-earn non-attendees by ₹2,000. Why is this not the causal effect, and what should you report?
    Solution
    Comparing attendees with non-attendees compares self-selected groups — pure selection. Report the ITT (offered vs not offered, regardless of attendance). To recover the effect on those induced to attend, rescale: \(\text{LATE} = \text{ITT}/0.60\) (assuming no always-/never-taker contamination), exactly the IV logic of Module 6.
  3. You randomise 40 villages (20/20) rather than 4,000 individuals. Why might the study be underpowered even with 4,000 people in it?
    Solution
    Outcomes within a village are correlated (intra-cluster correlation \(\rho\)). Effective sample size is closer to the number of clusters than the number of people; the design effect \(1+(m-1)\rho\) inflates the variance. With only 40 clusters and non-trivial \(\rho\), the minimum detectable effect can be large despite 4,000 individuals. Power on clusters.
Vandana
The hardest part of a real trial is rarely the statistics — it is keeping the control group truly untreated and tracking everyone to endline. In design clinics we spend most of our time on take-up, attrition and spillover logistics, because that is where good RCTs are won or lost.
