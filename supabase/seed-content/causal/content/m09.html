Module 9: Synthetic Control

Sometimes the treated unit is singular — one state bans liquor, one city prices congestion, one country reforms. There is no natural control group and DiD's “pick a comparison” feels arbitrary. The synthetic control method builds the comparison instead: a weighted blend of untreated units chosen to track the treated unit's history closely, then reads the effect as the gap that opens after treatment.

The Idea

With one treated unit and \(J\) potential controls (the “donor pool”), construct weights \(w_j \ge 0\) summing to one so that the weighted donors reproduce the treated unit's pre-treatment outcomes and predictors. That weighted average is the synthetic control.

\[ \widehat{\tau}_{1t} \;=\; Y_{1t} \;-\; \sum_{j=2}^{J+1} w_j^{\,*}\,Y_{jt}, \qquad w_j^{\,*}\ge 0,\ \ \sum_j w_j^{\,*}=1 \]
After treatment, the gap between the treated unit \(Y_{1t}\) and its synthetic counterpart is the estimated effect. The non-negativity and sum-to-one constraints keep the synthetic unit inside the data (no extrapolation) and make the weights transparent.
What it needs
A long pre-period
Enough pre-treatment observations to fit the treated unit's trajectory well — a good pre-fit is the method's credibility.
A clean donor pool
Untreated units not themselves affected by the treatment (no spillover) and plausibly driven by the same forces.
No anticipation
The treated unit doesn't change behaviour before the official start date.

Inference by Placebo

With one treated unit, classical standard errors do not apply. Abadie, Diamond & Hainmueller (2010) use permutation: apply the method to every donor as if it were treated, building a distribution of placebo gaps. If the real treated unit's post-treatment gap is extreme relative to the placebos, the effect is unlikely to be chance. Complement with in-time placebos (a fake earlier date) and leave-one-out checks on the weights.

A close pre-treatment match (the two lines overlap before the cutoff) is what licenses reading the post-treatment gap as the effect.
Worked example — a single-state prohibition

One state introduces prohibition in a given year. To estimate the effect on, say, reported road-traffic deaths, build a synthetic “state” from a weighted mix of non-prohibition states that matches the treated state's pre-ban death trajectory and covariates (income, vehicles, urbanisation).

Donor stateWeight \(w_j^*\)
State A0.41
State B0.33
State C0.26
All others0.00

The post-ban gap between the real state and this synthetic blend — tested against placebo gaps from every donor — is the estimate. The transparent weights show exactly which states are doing the comparison.

In Code

## R — synthetic control with a placebo distribution (tidysynth)
library(tidysynth)
sc <- panel %>%
  synthetic_control(outcome = deaths, unit = state, time = year,
                    i_unit = "TreatedState", i_time = 2016) %>%
  generate_predictor(time_window = 2005:2015,
                     income = mean(income), vehicles = mean(vehicles)) %>%
  generate_weights() %>% generate_control()
sc %>% plot_trends();  sc %>% plot_placebos();  sc %>% grab_sign. .  # gaps & weights

synth deaths income vehicles urban deaths(2005) deaths(2010) deaths(2015), ///
      trunit(11) trperiod(2016) fig
* loop over donors as placebos to build the permutation distribution

Pitfalls. A poor pre-treatment fit invalidates the comparison — do not interpret the gap. Beware donors contaminated by the same policy (spillover), over-fitting with too many predictors, and reading a gap smaller than typical placebo gaps as a “real” effect.

Practice
  1. When is synthetic control preferable to a standard DiD with several comparison states?
    Solution
    When the treated unit is a poor match for any single comparison and for the unweighted average of comparisons — SC finds a data-driven weighted blend that tracks the pre-period, reducing the arbitrariness of choosing controls, and it makes the comparison transparent via the weights. It shines with one (or few) treated units and a long pre-period.
  2. Your synthetic control matches the treated unit poorly in the pre-period (visible gaps before treatment). What can you conclude about the post-treatment effect?
    Solution
    Little. If the synthetic unit cannot reproduce history, its post-treatment path is not a credible counterfactual, so the gap conflates effect with mis-fit. Report the poor fit honestly; consider a different donor pool, more pre-periods, or another design rather than presenting a number.
  3. Why is permutation (placebo) inference used here instead of conventional standard errors?
    Solution
    With a single treated unit there is no sampling distribution of treated units to form a standard error. Permutation treats each donor as a placebo “treatment,” generating a reference distribution of gaps under no effect; the treated unit's rank in that distribution is the basis for inference.
