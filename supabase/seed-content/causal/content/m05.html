Module 5: Matching and Propensity Scores

When you cannot randomise but believe selection is on observables, matching builds a comparison group that looks like the treated on everything you measured. Its honesty lives entirely in that belief.

The Idea

For each treated unit, find untreated units with the same covariates and compare outcomes. With many covariates, exact matches run out. Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983) showed you can match on a single number instead, the propensity score, Pr(D=1 | X), and still balance X.

Methods and Diagnostics
Propensity-score matching
Estimate the score, then match treated to control units with nearby scores (nearest neighbour, caliper, kernel).
Coarsened exact matching
Iacus, King & Porro (2012). Coarsen each covariate into bins, match exactly within bins. Transparent and bounds imbalance directly.
Common support
Drop treated units with no comparable control and vice versa. You can only compare where both groups exist.
Balance check
After matching, covariate means should be close across groups. Standardised differences under about 0.1 are the usual target. Balance, not the regression output, is the test of success.

A frequent application: comparing facilities that adopted a quality-certification programme with similar uncertified facilities, matched on size, caseload, location, and prior performance, then comparing outcomes. Done to lending-industry standards, a careful matched comparison can persuade a risk committee even when an RCT was never possible.

Key Idea

Matching does not solve selection on unobservables. It makes selection on observables transparent and checkable. If the thing that drove participation is unmeasured, a perfectly balanced match is still biased. Dehejia and Wahba (2002) showed matching can recover experimental benchmarks, but only when the right covariates are observed.

Balance is the deliverable, not the coefficient. Report covariate balance before and after matching. A reviewer who sees only the treatment effect, and no balance table, should not believe the number.

Varna
I treat matching as a descriptive comparison dressed in careful clothing, not as identification. It is often the most honest thing available, and I say so to the funder in those words. Overclaiming it as causal is how matched studies lose credibility.

## R: propensity-score matching with balance check
library(MatchIt)
m <- matchit(treat ~ size + caseload + region + prior_perf,
             data = df, method = "nearest", caliper = 0.1)
summary(m)                       # check standardised mean differences
att <- lm(outcome ~ treat, data = match.data(m))
