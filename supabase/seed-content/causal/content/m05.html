Module 5: Matching and Propensity Scores

When you cannot randomise but believe selection is on observables (Module 2's CIA), matching builds a comparison group that looks like the treated on those observables. The propensity score makes this practical in many dimensions. None of it rescues you from unobserved confounding — matching is an estimator for the CIA world, not an escape from it.

The Propensity Score

Conditioning on many covariates at once is infeasible — cells empty out. Rosenbaum & Rubin (1983) showed you can condition on a single number, the probability of treatment given \(X\):

\[ e(X) \;=\; \Pr(D = 1 \mid X) \]
The propensity score. Their theorem: if CIA holds given \(X\), it also holds given \(e(X)\). A high-dimensional balancing problem collapses to one dimension.
Three ways to use it
Matching
Pair each treated unit with control(s) of similar \(e(X)\) (nearest-neighbour, within a caliper). Difference outcomes within pairs, then average for the ATT.
Stratification
Split into bins of \(e(X)\), estimate the within-bin difference, and average across bins.
Inverse-probability weighting (IPW)
Reweight so treated and controls represent the same population.
\[ \widehat{\text{ATE}}_{\text{IPW}} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{D_i\,Y_i}{\hat{e}(X_i)} \;-\; \frac{(1-D_i)\,Y_i}{1-\hat{e}(X_i)}\right] \]
Up-weights treated units that “looked” like controls and vice versa. Extreme scores near 0 or 1 produce enormous weights — a signal that overlap is failing.

Balance and Overlap Are the Whole Game

Matching is only as good as the balance it achieves. After matching, the treated and control groups should be near-identical on every covariate; check standardised mean differences (a common rule of thumb: keep them below 0.1). And matching can only compare over the region of common support — treated units with no comparable control must be dropped, which changes the estimand to “the ATT on the matchable.”

The LaLonde cautionary tale. LaLonde (1986) showed observational estimators often failed to reproduce an experimental benchmark for a training programme. Dehejia & Wahba (1999) recovered it with careful propensity matching on rich covariates including pre-treatment earnings — but Smith & Todd (2005) showed the success was fragile to specification. The lesson: matching can work, but only with strong covariates and honest sensitivity checks. It never removes bias from what you did not measure.

Worked example — SHG membership and savings

Suppose you evaluate whether joining a self-help group raises household savings. Members differ from non-members on assets, education and prior income. Matching shrinks the raw gap toward the part attributable to membership — conditional on having measured the right covariates.

Practice
  1. A colleague matches on the propensity score and reports near-perfect balance, concluding the estimate is “as good as an RCT.” What single assumption are they still relying on, and how would you probe it?
    Solution
    They still rely on CIA — selection only on the observed covariates in the score. Balance on observables says nothing about unobservables. Probe it with a Rosenbaum-bounds / sensitivity analysis (how large an unobserved confounder would overturn the result?) and by checking robustness to richer covariates, especially pre-treatment outcomes.
  2. Why does an IPW estimate become unstable when some estimated propensity scores are very close to 1?
    Solution
    A control-group weight is \(1/(1-\hat e)\); as \(\hat e \to 1\) this explodes, so a handful of units dominate the estimate and the variance balloons. It signals an overlap failure: at those \(X\) there are almost no controls. Remedies include trimming extreme scores or stabilised/winsorised weights — and honesty that the estimand now excludes that region.
  3. You match on the propensity score but include “current employment” (measured after the programme) among the covariates. What goes wrong?
    Solution
    Current employment is post-treatment — plausibly a mediator or collider (Module 3). Matching on it conditions on a consequence of treatment, biasing the estimate. Only pre-treatment covariates belong in the score.
