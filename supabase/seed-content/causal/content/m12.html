Module 12: When There Is No Counterfactual

Much of development work cannot be evaluated with a control group. Advocacy, institutional reform, network building, and one-off national programmes have no comparison units and no clean before-and-after. Refusing to evaluate these is not an option. This module is the credible toolkit for the no-counterfactual world.

Theory-Based Evaluation

When you cannot estimate a counterfactual, you test the causal story instead. Lay out the theory of change as a chain of claims, then gather evidence for each link and, harder, against plausible rival explanations. The credibility comes from the rigour of the testing, not from a comparison group.

Four Credible Approaches
Contribution analysis
Mayne (2008). Build the results chain, assemble evidence for each link, and seriously assess other factors that could explain the outcome. The deliverable is a contribution story that survives scrutiny, not an effect size.
Process tracing
Collier (2011). Use within-case evidence as diagnostic tests. A hoop test must be passed for a claim to survive; a smoking-gun test, if passed, strongly confirms it. Bayesian process tracing updates belief on each piece of evidence.
Realist evaluation
Pawson & Tilley (1997). Ask what works, for whom, in what circumstances, through the context-mechanism-outcome configuration. The question is the mechanism, not the average.
Qualitative comparative analysis
Ragin (1987). Across a medium number of cases, identify which combinations of conditions are necessary or sufficient for the outcome, using set theory rather than correlation.
Key Idea

No counterfactual is not the same as no rigour. A well-executed contribution analysis or process trace can be more credible than a badly identified quasi-experiment. The standard is the same: state the claim, state what would disconfirm it, and go looking for the disconfirming evidence honestly.

The misuse to avoid. These methods are sometimes chosen not because a counterfactual is impossible but because it is inconvenient. That is a problem. Reserve theory-based designs for cases where a counterfactual genuinely cannot exist, and say plainly when you are using them as a fallback rather than a first choice.

When a funder wants impact numbers for an advocacy programme, I do not invent a counterfactual that cannot exist. I offer a contribution analysis with explicit rival explanations and a process trace of the key decisions. Most funders respect the honesty once they see the alternative is a fabricated comparison.

These approaches connect back to the MEL flagship’s distinction between attribution and contribution. Modules 1 to 10 are about attribution, isolating a programme’s effect against a counterfactual. This module is about contribution, defensible reasoning when isolation is impossible.
