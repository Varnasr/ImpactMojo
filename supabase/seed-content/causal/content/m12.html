Module 12: When There Is No Counterfactual

Some of the most important questions in development resist every design in this course. What did an advocacy coalition contribute to a new law? Did a systems-strengthening programme change how a department works? You cannot randomise a constitutional amendment or find a control group for a one-off reform. Abandoning rigour is not the answer; counterfactual logic is. This module covers the theory-based methods that establish causation by ruling out rival explanations rather than by estimating a number.

Rigour without a control group

When you cannot construct \(Y(0)\), credibility comes from a well-specified theory of change plus evidence that (a) the predicted causal steps actually occurred, and (b) the plausible alternative explanations did not. That is a different epistemology from estimation — closer to a detective's case than a regression — but it can be disciplined and transparent.

A Toolkit of Theory-Based Methods

Match the method to the claim
Contribution analysis (Mayne)
Build a contribution story, gather evidence for each link, confront it with rival explanations, and assess whether the story holds. Verdict is about contribution, not sole attribution.
Process tracing (Beach & Pedersen)
Test the causal mechanism within a single case using diagnostic evidence: hoop tests (must pass to stay plausible), smoking-gun tests (passing strongly confirms). Often framed as Bayesian updating on hypotheses.
Realist evaluation (Pawson & Tilley)
Ask “what works, for whom, in what circumstances?” via Context–Mechanism–Outcome configurations — valuable for complex, context-dependent interventions.
QCA (Ragin)
Across a medium number of cases, identify combinations of conditions that are necessary or sufficient for the outcome — useful when causation is conjunctural (several things must co-occur).
Outcome harvesting / Most Significant Change
Work backwards from observed changes to whether and how the intervention contributed — suited to emergent, unpredictable outcomes (advocacy, networks).

The Logic Is Still Counterfactual

Even here, the question “would this have happened anyway?” never disappears — you answer it with evidence about timing, mechanism and alternatives rather than a comparison group. Process tracing makes this explicit through Bayesian reasoning: a piece of evidence is persuasive to the extent it is likely if your hypothesis is true and unlikely if a rival is. Strong tests are those a rival explanation would probably fail.

Worked example — did the campaign move the law?

A coalition claims its advocacy helped pass a state nutrition law. There is no control state. A contribution analysis would assemble: a dated theory of change; evidence the coalition's briefs entered the drafting (committee records citing them); testimony from officials on what shifted their position; and, crucially, a serious examination of rivals — a change of minister, central scheme guidelines, media pressure. The verdict is calibrated: “the coalition made a substantial contribution alongside a favourable political window,” not “the coalition caused the law” and not a fabricated percentage.

Pitfalls. The temptation is to confuse a vivid story with evidence. Discipline it: specify the theory of change before collecting evidence, actively seek disconfirming evidence and rival explanations, triangulate sources, and report your confidence honestly. A theory-based evaluation that never looked for a rival is advocacy, not evaluation.

Practice
  1. You must evaluate a three-year governance-reform programme in a single department. Which approach fits, and what would count as strong evidence of contribution?
    Solution
    Contribution analysis and/or process tracing suit a single complex case. Strong evidence: a pre-specified theory of change whose intermediate steps are documented (training led to new procedures led to faster service), official testimony on why practice changed, timing that aligns with programme inputs, and a credible examination ruling out rivals (a leadership change, a court order, a parallel reform). Calibrate the conclusion to how well rivals were excluded.
  2. Distinguish a “hoop” test from a “smoking-gun” test in process tracing, with an example.
    Solution
    A hoop test is necessary but not sufficient: failing it largely rules the hypothesis out, passing it only keeps it alive (e.g. “was the coalition's brief physically in the drafting file?”). A smoking-gun test is sufficient but not necessary: passing strongly confirms, failing does not rule out (e.g. a minister's memo explicitly crediting the coalition's argument). Strong inference combines both.
  3. Why is “contribution” often the honest causal claim for advocacy, rather than “attribution”?
    Solution
    Policy change is over-determined — many actors and a political window usually co-produce it. No counterfactual can isolate one actor's share. “Contribution” states that the intervention was part of a sufficient causal package without claiming it was the sole or decisive cause, which is what the evidence can actually support.
