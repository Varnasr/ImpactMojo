Module 11: Reading and Interrogating an Evaluation

Most practitioners will commission and read far more impact studies than they will ever run. This module is about the harder skill: holding a finished evaluation up to the light and deciding how much weight it can bear.

The Reading Order

Read an impact evaluation in a deliberate sequence, not front to back. First the estimand: what effect, on whom. Then the identification strategy and its assumption, stated in one sentence. Then the threats specific to that design, from the relevant module. Only then the results table, and last the abstract, which is where authors are most generous to themselves.

A Standing Checklist
Identification
Which design, which assumption, and is the assumption argued or merely asserted.
The specific threat
RCT: compliance, attrition, spillovers. IV: exclusion, first-stage strength. RD: manipulation. DiD: pre-trends and staggered timing.
Specification stability
Does the result survive sensible alternative specifications, or only one. A specification curve (Simonsohn, Simmons & Nelson 2020) shows the whole distribution rather than the author’s favourite point.
Generalisation
To what population and setting does the number plausibly travel, and on what argument.

The Credibility Debate, Without the False Balance

The randomised-evaluation movement, associated with Banerjee and Duflo (2011) and recognised by the 2019 Nobel, sharpened development economics by insisting on credible identification. Deaton (2010, JEL) and Pritchett and Sandefur (2015) pressed the obvious limit: an internally valid estimate from one trial does not tell you what the programme will do elsewhere, and mechanically transporting RCT results across contexts can mislead more than a context-specific observational study.

My read

The critics are right about external validity and wrong if taken to dismiss the method. The discipline of stating an identifying assumption is a permanent gain, and the field is better for it. The honest position is narrow internal claims plus an explicit, falsifiable argument about transport, rather than either RCT triumphalism or a retreat to unidentified storytelling.

Publication bias is structural, not occasional. Significant, surprising results are likelier to be published (Andrews & Kasy 2019). A single striking finding, especially an early one, is weaker than it looks until it replicates. Weight a literature, not a paper.

Varna
When I review an evaluation for a board, I write the identifying assumption in one sentence at the top of my note, then ask whether the paper earns it. If the assumption needs a paragraph of hedging, that is the finding. Most of my value to a funder is killing the over-claim before it reaches a decision.
