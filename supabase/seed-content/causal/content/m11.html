Module 11: Reading and Interrogating an Evaluation

Most practitioners will commission and read far more evaluations than they run. The skill that matters most is therefore judgement: given a study, what claim can it actually bear? This module turns the whole course into a reading protocol — a sequence of questions that separates a result you can act on from a number dressed up as one.

The Seven Questions

  1. 1What is the estimand? ATE, ATT, LATE, or an effect at a cutoff? For whom? A LATE on compliers answers a narrower question than a headline implies (Modules 1, 6, 7).
  2. 2What is the identification strategy, and its one key assumption? Every design rests on something untestable — CIA, exclusion, parallel trends, continuity, pre-fit. Name it.
  3. 3Is that assumption defended? Look for the matching diagnostic: balance table (RCT/matching), first-stage F (IV), pre-trends/event study (DiD), density & covariate-smoothness tests (RD), pre-period fit and placebos (SC).
  4. 4Internal vs external validity. Is the effect credibly estimated here (internal), and would it travel to your context (external)? They are different questions; a clean RCT can have weak external validity.
  5. 5Magnitude, precision, and the null. Read the confidence interval, not just the star. Is the effect economically meaningful? A “significant” 0.02 SD effect may not be worth the budget; a wide interval around a large estimate may be uninformative.
  6. 6Researcher degrees of freedom. Was it pre-registered? How many outcomes/subgroups were tested (multiple-comparison risk)? Are results robust across specifications, or only the headline one?
  7. 7Cost and the decision. Effect per rupee, against alternatives. Evidence serves a decision; an effect with no cost context rarely settles one.

External Validity: Will It Travel?

An effect estimated in Rajasthan need not hold in Jharkhand. Generalisation is not automatic; it is an argument about mechanisms and context. Ask what made the programme work, and whether those conditions (markets, capacity, norms, the counterfactual services people already had) are present where you intend to act. Vivalt (2020) documents that effect sizes vary substantially across replications, so a single study's number is a draw from a distribution, not a constant.

Structured speculation. Rather than “it worked there, so it will work here,” map the causal chain, list the contextual conditions each link needs, and check them against your setting. A systematic review or meta-analysis (where one exists) beats any single study for what to expect on average.

Worked example — interrogating a brief

A vendor's one-pager claims: “Our app increased learning by 0.6 SD (p<0.05).” Run the protocol: Estimand? — unstated; turns out to be the effect on app-completers (selection). Design? — completers vs non-completers (no counterfactual). Defended? — no balance, no randomisation. Precision? — n=80, CI runs 0.05–1.15. Pre-registered? — no; one of many outcomes. Verdict: a selected-sample correlation with a huge, imprecise estimate — not evidence the app raises learning. Two questions dismantled the headline.

Practice
  1. A study reports “no significant effect (p=0.21)” and concludes the programme “does not work.” The 95% CI is −0.1 to +0.5 SD. What is the correct reading?
    Solution
    Absence of significance is not evidence of absence. The interval is consistent with effects up to +0.5 SD — the study is simply underpowered/imprecise, not a demonstration of no effect. Correct statement: “inconclusive; cannot rule out a meaningful positive effect.”
  2. An RCT in one district finds a strong effect of a teacher-incentive scheme. Your state wants to adopt it. List three context conditions you would check before assuming it travels.
    Solution
    For example: (a) the baseline level of teacher absence/monitoring (the mechanism the incentive acts on); (b) administrative capacity to measure and pay on the incentivised metric without gaming (Goodhart); (c) the counterfactual services and teacher labour market, which shape both take-up and the room to improve. If these differ, the effect may not transfer.
  3. A report tests 20 outcomes and highlights the 2 that are significant at 5%. Why is caution warranted, and what would strengthen the claim?
    Solution
    At a 5% threshold, about one in twenty tests is “significant” by chance, so ~1–2 false positives are expected among 20 even with no true effects. Strengthen with a pre-registered primary outcome, multiple-comparison adjustment (e.g. family-wise or false-discovery control), and replication. Treat the highlighted two as exploratory.
Vandana
When a vendor or partner sends “proof,” we read it together against these seven questions before any money moves. Most over-claims fall at question 1 (what is the estimand?) or question 3 (is the assumption defended?). Learning to ask them is the highest-return hour a programme team can spend.
