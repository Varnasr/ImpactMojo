Module 7: Regression Discontinuity

Many programmes assign benefits by a rule: a poverty score below a line, a test mark above a cutoff, a population threshold that triggers a grant. Just on either side of that line, units are almost identical — but one side gets treated and the other does not. Regression discontinuity turns the administrative rule into a near-experiment at the boundary.

The Design

Let \(X\) be a running (forcing) variable and \(c\) a known cutoff. In a sharp RDD, treatment is a deterministic step:

\[ D_i = \mathbf{1}\{X_i \ge c\} \]
Everyone above the cutoff is treated, everyone below is not. In a fuzzy RDD the probability of treatment merely jumps at \(c\); the jump becomes an instrument (Module 6 logic) for treatment at the boundary.

The identifying assumption is continuity: absent the programme, average potential outcomes would vary smoothly through \(c\). Any jump in \(Y\) at \(c\) is then attributable to treatment.

\[ \tau_{\text{RD}} \;=\; \lim_{x \downarrow c} E[\,Y \mid X = x\,] \;-\; \lim_{x \uparrow c} E[\,Y \mid X = x\,] \]
The estimand is the effect at the cutoff — for units near the margin. Local by construction: it says nothing about people far from \(c\).
The jump at \(c\) between the two local fits is the RD estimate. The slopes capture the smooth relationship between the running variable and the outcome; the gap is the effect.

Estimation: Local, Not Global

Fit the outcome–running-variable relationship separately on each side using local linear regression within a bandwidth \(h\) around \(c\), and take the gap at \(c\). Choose \(h\) by a data-driven rule (Calonico, Cattaneo & Titiunik 2014) with bias-corrected, robust confidence intervals. Avoid high-order global polynomials — Gelman & Imbens (2019) show they put absurd weight on far-away points and invent discontinuities.

Worked example — a merit-cum-means scholarship

A scholarship is awarded to students scoring at or above 60 on a qualifying exam. Compare college enrolment just above and just below 60. Students at 59 and 61 are essentially indistinguishable except for the award, so the jump in enrolment at 60 estimates the scholarship's effect at the margin.

ApproachEstimated jump in enrolment at cutoff
Naive: all scholars vs all non-scholars+31 pp (confounded by ability)
Local linear RD, optimal bandwidth (±~6 marks)+9 pp

The naive gap conflates the award with the ability that earned it; the RD isolates the award's effect for students near 60. It does not tell you the effect for a student scoring 90.

Validity Checks — Earn the Continuity Assumption

  • ANo manipulation. Units must not precisely sort across the cutoff (e.g. graders nudging 59s to 60). Test for bunching in the running-variable density (McCrary / Cattaneo–Jansson–Ma density test).
  • BCovariate smoothness. Pre-determined characteristics should not jump at \(c\). Run the RD on baseline covariates as a placebo — a jump there is a red flag.
  • CPlacebo cutoffs. Fake cutoffs away from \(c\) should show no effect.
## R — robust RD estimate, density test, bandwidth (rdrobust)
library(rdrobust); library(rddensity)
rd <- rdrobust(y = enrolled, x = score, c = 60)        # bias-corrected CIs
summary(rd)
summary(rddensity(X = score, c = 60))                  # manipulation test
rdplot(y = enrolled, x = score, c = 60)                # see the jump
* Stata — same workflow
rdrobust enrolled score, c(60)
rddensity score, c(60)
rdplot   enrolled score, c(60)
Practice
  1. A loan-waiver scheme covers farms below 2 hectares. You find a sharp spike in the number of farms reported at exactly 1.9 ha. What does this threaten, and which test detects it?
    Solution
    It threatens the no-manipulation assumption: farmers (or officials) sort land records just under the threshold to qualify. The continuity of potential outcomes likely fails, so the RD estimate is biased. A density/bunching test (McCrary or Cattaneo–Jansson–Ma) at the cutoff detects the excess mass just below 2 ha.
  2. An RD on a poverty score finds a large enrolment jump, but baseline household assets also jump at the cutoff. Interpret.
    Solution
    Pre-determined covariates should be continuous at \(c\). A jump in assets signals either manipulation or that the score is not the only thing changing at the cutoff — the continuity assumption is in doubt, and the outcome jump may reflect the covariate jump rather than the programme. The design is not credible as-is.
  3. Why is an RD estimate “local,” and how should that shape the policy claim you make from it?
    Solution
    It identifies the effect only for units near the cutoff (the marginally eligible). Extrapolating to people far from \(c\) requires extra assumptions about how effects vary with the running variable. Frame the claim as “for households near the eligibility line,” not “for all beneficiaries.”
