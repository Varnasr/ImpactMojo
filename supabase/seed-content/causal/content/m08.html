Module 8: Difference-in-Differences

When a policy arrives at different times or places, you can compare the change in a treated group to the change in a comparison group. The design rests on one assumption that has been a moving target in the literature since 2018.

The Canonical 2x2

Two groups, two periods. The treated group is exposed between periods; the comparison group is not. Subtract each group’s before-after change, then subtract the two differences. Whatever common shock hit both groups cancels. Card and Krueger (1994) made the design famous with the New Jersey minimum-wage study.

DiD = ( Ytreated,after − Ytreated,before ) − ( Ycontrol,after − Ycontrol,before )
The first difference removes fixed differences between units; the second removes the common time trend. What remains is the effect, if the trends would have moved together absent treatment.
The Identifying Assumption
Parallel trends
Absent the treatment, treated and comparison groups would have followed the same trend. This is the whole ballgame, and it is not testable directly.
Pre-trends
Plot the groups before treatment. Divergence before the policy is evidence against parallel trends. Similar pre-trends are supportive, not proof (Roth 2022 warns against reading too much into pre-trend tests).

The Staggered-Adoption Problem

Most real policies in India arrive state by state, year by year. The reflex is a two-way fixed effects regression with unit and time dummies. Since 2018 we know this can fail badly. When treatment timing varies, two-way fixed effects implicitly uses already-treated units as controls for newly-treated ones, and can put negative weights on some effects. Goodman-Bacon (2021) decomposed exactly where the bias comes from.

Key Idea

For staggered rollouts, do not trust a plain two-way fixed effects coefficient. Use an estimator built for the problem: Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021), Sun and Abraham (2021), or de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille (2020). They estimate clean group-time effects and aggregate them with honest weights.

Report an event study, not a single number. Plot effects by time relative to treatment. Flat pre-period coefficients support parallel trends; a clear post-period jump shows the effect emerging. A lone DiD coefficient hides both the pre-trend and the dynamics.
