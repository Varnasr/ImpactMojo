Module 8: Restorative Practices

The paradigm becomes practice through a family of structured encounters. They range from a thirty-second "affective statement" to a formal, scripted conference — a continuum, not a single method.

The core models

  • Victim–Offender Mediation / Dialogue (VOM). A prepared, facilitated meeting between the person harmed and the person responsible — the earliest Western RJ model (Kitchener, Ontario, 1974).
  • Family Group Conferencing (FGC). Originating in Aotearoa New Zealand's Children, Young Persons and Their Families Act 1989, shaped by Māori practice — the extended family (whānau) is convened, given private time to devise a plan, and central to the outcome.
  • Circles (peacemaking / talking circles / sentencing circles). Rooted in First Nations practice in Canada and the US; a keeper holds the space and a talking piece passes so each voice is heard in turn. Documented by Kay Pranis.
  • Restorative Conferences (the "Wagga Wagga" / scripted model). A trained facilitator follows a script built on affective questions ("What happened? Who's been affected? What's needed to make things right?").

The restorative continuum

Ted Wachtel and the IIRP frame practices as a continuum of increasing formality and participation — most restorative work is the informal end, done daily, not the dramatic conference:

Design principle

The heart of every restorative practice is the same: "with," not "to" or "for." High expectations and high support, together — not control (to), not permissiveness (for), and not neglect (neither).

The shape of the circle

Almost every model shares one deceptively simple geometry: participants sit in an unbroken circle, no table, no rows, no bench — everyone faces the centre and everyone can see everyone else. The circle is not decoration; it is the method's statement that no one here is a spectator and no one presides. A facilitator (keeper) sits in the ring, not above it, and a talking piece moves round so that speaking is regulated by an object rather than by status or volume.

In a circle or conference no one sits at the head of the table: harmed, responsible, their supporters and the wider community all face a shared centre, and the talking piece — not the facilitator — governs who speaks.

Three encounters, often confused

Practitioners routinely blur mediation, conferencing and circles. They share a restorative spirit but differ in who is in the room, who holds authority and what the process is for.

Mediation

Typically two parties in dispute and an impartial mediator. The mediator is neutral about the outcome and helps the parties reach their own settlement. Fault need not be established; a good mediation can end with "we simply see this differently." Best where harm is mutual or contested.

Conference

A wider, scripted encounter that assumes a person has caused harm and (usually) accepts responsibility. It deliberately includes supporters on both sides and follows affective questions toward an agreement to repair. The facilitator is not neutral about the harm — only about the people.

Circle

The most communal form. A keeper and a talking piece structure the space; it can hold many voices and need not centre on a single incident — circles also build community, make decisions, or heal collectively. Everyone speaks in turn; the process is the point as much as any outcome.

Talking piece
A held object (a stone, a shell, a length of cloth — often something of meaning to the community) that passes round the circle. Only the person holding it may speak; everyone else listens. It slows the exchange, protects the quietest voice from the loudest, and removes the facilitator's power to allocate the floor. The object, not a person, keeps order.
Pre-conference preparation

The encounter you see is the smallest part of the work. Before any circle or conference, the facilitator meets each participant separately to prepare them, and this preparation is what makes the room safe:

  • Consent and readiness. No one is compelled. The person harmed must genuinely want to take part — never pressured "for the offender's sake" — and the person responsible must have moved past denial to some acceptance of what they did.
  • Managing expectations. The facilitator walks each person through what will happen, what will be asked, and what will not (no ambush, no cross-examination, no guaranteed apology).
  • Safety and power. Screening for intimidation, ongoing risk or gross power imbalance — some cases (e.g. certain domestic-abuse situations) are unsuitable for a face-to-face meeting, and a skilled practitioner will say so.
  • Choosing supporters. Helping each side identify the people whose presence will steady rather than inflame them.
Reintegrative vs stigmatising shame
John Braithwaite (Crime, Shame and Reintegration, 1989) distinguishes shame that brands the person as bad — pushing them out, toward a criminal identity — from shame that disapproves of the act while affirming the person's worth and welcoming them back. A well-run conference is engineered for the second: the harm is named plainly, but supporters in the room signal that the person still belongs. Get this wrong and a conference simply humiliates.
Case — a school conference after a serious fight

At a secondary school, Rohan (15) punched Imran during a break, splitting his lip and frightening the students who watched. The old response would be a three-day suspension: Rohan misses lessons, learns that he is "a violent boy," and Imran — the person actually harmed — is never asked what he needs. The school runs a restorative conference instead.

Preparation. The facilitator meets Rohan, Imran and their parents separately. She confirms Imran wants to take part and is not being pressured; she checks that Rohan has moved past "he started it" to some ownership; she agrees who will attend. A bystander who was shaken and Rohan's form teacher are invited as affected community.

Opening. Everyone sits in a circle — no desk between Rohan and Imran. The facilitator states the purpose in one breath: "We are here because of what happened on Tuesday, to understand the harm and work out how to put things right. We are not here to decide punishment." She names the ground rules and, in a circle format, introduces the talking piece.

Each voice, in turn. She asks Rohan the affective questions first: what happened, what were you thinking at the time, who has been affected? Then Imran describes the fear and the humiliation of being hit in front of his friends — the part Rohan had not considered. The bystander speaks to how unsafe the break felt. The parents speak. The facilitator does almost nothing but hold the sequence and let silence do its work; she resists the urge to lecture.

Reaching agreement. Only after the harm is fully in the room does she ask: what is needed to make things right? The plan is written by the participants, not imposed — Rohan apologises in his own words, agrees to help reset the sports store he damaged, and commits to a fortnightly check-in; the school agrees to a period without a suspension mark so Rohan is not branded.

Follow-up. The facilitator schedules the check-in and closes by noting what she heard each person offer. Two weeks later she confirms the apology landed for Imran and the agreement is holding — the accountability was facing the harm and repairing it, not being sent away from it.

Two settings that matter for practitioners

  • Schools. Restorative practices replace suspension-and-expulsion cultures with circles, restorative conversations and repair. Strong evidence for reduced exclusions and improved climate; weaker where bolted on without whole-school buy-in. Directly relevant to South Asian education programming and the anti-corporal-punishment agenda.
  • Criminal justice. Used as diversion (pre-charge), pre-sentence, in prisons, and at re-entry. Meta-analyses (e.g. Sherman & Strang) find RJ conferencing tends to reduce repeat offending and markedly improves victim satisfaction and post-traumatic recovery compared with court.

Reflect. Think of a harm you have handled at work or in your community. Which encounter did it really call for — a mediation, a conference or a circle — and did the process you actually used match that? Hardest of all: were you honest about whether the person harmed genuinely wanted to be in the room, or was the meeting quietly arranged for the sake of the person who caused the harm?
