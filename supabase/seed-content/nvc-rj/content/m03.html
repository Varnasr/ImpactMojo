Module 3: The Four Components

The visible technique of NVC is a four-part form — Observation, Feeling, Need, Request (OFNR). It is a scaffold, not a script: the aim is the consciousness behind it, not the phrasing.

OFNR, precisely

ComponentIsIs not (the common error)
ObservationWhat a camera would record — specific, time- and context-bound factsEvaluation ("you're always late") — mixing observation with judgment invites the other to hear criticism
FeelingAn emotion or body sensation (anxious, relieved, tired)A thought disguised as a feeling: "I feel ignored / manipulated / let down" — these are interpretations of what others did
NeedThe universal need alive under the feeling (to be heard, for reliability, for rest)A strategy aimed at one person ("I need you to call me") — that belongs in the request
RequestA present, positive, concrete, doable action, offered as genuinely open to "no"A demand — where a "no" will be met with blame, guilt, or punishment
Observing without evaluating is the highest form of human intelligence. J. Krishnamurti, quoted approvingly by Rosenberg
OFNR is a loop, not a ladder: the response to a request — including a "no" — feeds back as fresh observation, so connection keeps circulating rather than closing.

Request or demand? The test is in the response to "no"

The same sentence — "Would you be willing to take the bins out tonight?" — is a request or a demand depending entirely on what happens when the answer is no. If the "no" is treated as information about the other's needs (and you look for a strategy that works for both), it was a request. If the "no" brings coldness, guilt-tripping or retaliation, it was a demand wearing polite clothing. NVC insists that we cannot make anyone do anything; we can only create the quality of connection in which people give willingly.

Worked example

Jackal: "You never listen to me in these meetings."
Giraffe (OFNR): "In the last two meetings, when I finished a point you moved straight to the agenda (observation). I felt discouraged (feeling), because I really want my contributions to land (need). Would you be willing to tell me back what you heard before we move on? (request)"

Need vs strategy
A need is universal and impersonal — for rest, for reliability, for respect, for ease — and can be met in many ways. A strategy is one particular person doing one particular thing at one particular time. Conflict almost never lives at the level of needs (everyone wants reliability); it lives at the level of competing strategies. Naming the need first widens the field of workable strategies, so a "no" to your strategy is not a "no" to your need.
Case — the Sunday phone call

Meera, in Bengaluru, wants her adult son Arjun, newly moved to Pune for work, to call every Sunday evening. Two Sundays pass with no call. Under her irritation is a need for connection and reassurance that he is well.

Approach A — the demand in polite clothing. "A good son would call his mother. If you can't manage one call a week, don't bother." The words request nothing; the tone promises punishment for a "no". When Arjun explains that Sunday evenings are when his shift ends late, Meera hears an excuse, goes quiet, and lets the guilt do its work. He may start calling — resentfully, watching the clock — or stop altogether. Either way the connection she actually wants has been spent to buy the behaviour.

Approach B — the genuine request, and a real "no". "The last two Sundays went by without a call (observation). I noticed I felt a bit anxious (feeling), because staying connected with you matters to me and I like knowing you're settling in (need). Would you be willing to call this Sunday? (present, positive, concrete request)" Arjun answers: "Sundays are impossible — that's when my shift runs over." Because Meera made a request, not a demand, the "no" is information, not injury. She stays with the need, which was never "Sunday" but connection: "Ah — so Sunday doesn't work. What if we found any evening that does, even a five-minute message when you can?" They settle on Wednesdays. The request/demand test passes precisely because the "no" changed the strategy without wounding the relationship.

Practise this
  • Rosenberg — NVC: A Language of Life, chs. 3–6 (one chapter per component)
  • CNVC "feelings & needs inventories" (Center for Nonviolent Communication)

Reflect. Recall a recent "request" you made that was really a demand. Restate it as full OFNR, then name the underlying need beneath your preferred strategy. Now sit with the hardest part: if that person said a clear "no", could you still stay in connection — or would some part of you reach for guilt, distance or punishment?
