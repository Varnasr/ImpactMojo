Module 7: The Restorative Paradigm

Restorative Justice (RJ) asks a different question of wrongdoing than the criminal-legal system does. Howard Zehr's Changing Lenses (1990) crystallised the shift — from justice as deserved pain to justice as repair of harm.

Crime is a violation of people and relationships. It creates obligations to make things right. Justice involves the victim, the offender, and the community in a search for solutions which promote repair, reconciliation, and reassurance. Howard Zehr, Changing Lenses (1990)

The two lenses are not two moods or two degrees of severity — they are two starting questions, and the starting question decides everything that follows. Begin with the broken rule and the whole apparatus of blame, defence, and deserved pain unfolds automatically. Begin with the harmed person and a different apparatus — of listening, need, and obligation — unfolds instead. The diagram below sets the two question-sets side by side so you can feel the fork.

Zehr's three pillars

Zehr distils the restorative lens into three load-bearing commitments. They are not stages to march through but a stance to hold — each one a corrective to a reflex the retributive lens trains into us.

  • Harms and needs. Start with those who were harmed and what they need — safety, answers, acknowledgement, restitution, vindication — not with the rule that was broken.
  • Obligations. The person who caused harm has an obligation to understand and, so far as possible, repair it. Accountability here means facing the harm, not receiving punishment.
  • Engagement. Those with a stake — harmed, responsible, and community — are given meaningful roles in the process, rather than having it done to or for them by professionals.
Crime, redefined
In the restorative frame a wrong is a violation of people and relationships, not merely a breach of a rule. This is the pivot on which the three pillars turn: because the injury is to persons and the bonds between them, the response must attend to harms and needs first, must locate the obligation to repair with the one who caused it, and must give the affected parties real engagement in setting things right. Rules still matter — but they are the map, not the territory; the territory is the harm done to real people.
The three pillars, and the reflex each corrects
  • Harms & needs corrects the reflex to lead with the rule. The first move is to name who was hurt and what those people actually need — which is often acknowledgement and safety long before any material restitution.
  • Obligations corrects the reflex to lead with guilt and desert. Guilt closes a question — did they do it, yes or no. Obligation opens one — what does repair now require of them? The second is far harder to discharge and far more useful.
  • Engagement corrects the reflex to hand the matter to professionals. When a magistrate, a principal, or an HR officer resolves things for the parties, the harmed person is spoken about rather than heard, and the one responsible never has to face the person they hurt.

Reintegrative shaming. Criminologist John Braithwaite (Crime, Shame and Reintegration, 1989) distinguishes stigmatising shame, which brands a person as bad and pushes them into criminal identities, from reintegrative shame, which disapproves of the act while affirming the person's worth and welcoming them back. RJ aims for the second.

Case — a wall of graffiti on the community hall

In a small town outside Pune, fifteen-year-old Rohit and two friends spray-paint the freshly whitewashed wall of the neighbourhood samaj mandir — the community hall where weddings, prayer meetings, and the women's savings group all gather. The caretaker, an elderly man named Dattatray, finds it in the morning and is visibly shaken; the savings-group women, who had pooled money for the whitewash, are angry and humiliated in front of the town.

Approach A — the retributive route. The managing committee asks the same three questions the criminal-legal reflex supplies: what rule was broken, who did it, what do they deserve? A property rule was broken; Rohit did it; he deserves to pay. He is named at the next public meeting, barred from the hall for a year, and his father is made to hand over a fine. The rule is vindicated and blame is fixed — but notice what is not produced. Dattatray is never asked what the fright cost him. The savings-group women get money but no acknowledgement of the humiliation. Rohit, shamed publicly and cast out, learns that he is a bad boy rather than that he did a bad thing — Braithwaite's stigmatising shame — and drifts toward the older boys who already wear that label. The wall, incidentally, stays defaced for months because no one's obligation is to repair it; the fine went to the committee's account.

Approach B — the restorative route. A facilitator instead convenes a circle and asks Zehr's three questions. Who was harmed? — Dattatray, who felt unsafe in the place he tends; the women, whose labour and standing were mocked; and, it emerges, Rohit himself, bored and unseen since his mother's death. What do they need? — Dattatray needs to hear that it was not aimed at him; the women need public acknowledgement and their wall restored; Rohit needs a way back that does not brand him. Whose obligation is it to repair? — Rohit's, concretely. He hears, to his surprise, what his hour of bravado did to people he passes every day. The circle agrees an obligation: he re-whitewashes the wall over three weekends alongside two of the savings-group women, who — this is the reintegrative move — end up teaching him to plaster and feeding him lunch. The act is still disapproved; the person is kept. What this route produces that the first did not: a repaired wall, a reassured caretaker, acknowledged women, and a boy who is accountable and still belongs.

Varna
When you first sit with Zehr's three questions — who was hurt, what do they need, whose obligation is it — notice how quickly the instinct to assign blame tries to take over. Practise holding that instinct back; the whole paradigm lives in that pause.
