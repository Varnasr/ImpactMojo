Module 07

The Restorative Paradigm

Restorative Justice (RJ) asks a different question of wrongdoing than the criminal-legal system does. Howard Zehr's Changing Lenses (1990) crystallised the shift — from justice as deserved pain to justice as repair of harm.

Two lenses on the same act

Retributive lensRestorative lens
Crime is a violation of the law and the stateCrime is a violation of people and relationships
Violations create guiltViolations create obligations
Justice = the state determines blame and administers deserved painJustice = those affected search together to identify needs and put things right
Central: what laws were broken? who did it? what do they deserve?Central: who has been hurt? what do they need? whose obligation is it to meet those needs?
Crime is a violation of people and relationships. It creates obligations to make things right. Justice involves the victim, the offender, and the community in a search for solutions which promote repair, reconciliation, and reassurance. Howard Zehr, Changing Lenses (1990)

Zehr's three pillars

  • Harms and needs. Start with those who were harmed and what they need — safety, answers, acknowledgement, restitution, vindication — not with the rule that was broken.
  • Obligations. The person who caused harm has an obligation to understand and, so far as possible, repair it. Accountability here means facing the harm, not receiving punishment.
  • Engagement. Those with a stake — harmed, responsible, and community — are given meaningful roles in the process, rather than having it done to or for them by professionals.

Reintegrative shaming. Criminologist John Braithwaite (Crime, Shame and Reintegration, 1989) distinguishes stigmatising shame, which brands a person as bad and pushes them into criminal identities, from reintegrative shame, which disapproves of the act while affirming the person's worth and welcoming them back. RJ aims for the second.
