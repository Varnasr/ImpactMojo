Module 12: Capstone — Design a Nonviolent Response

Every module so far handed you one instrument — a way of speaking, a way of holding authority, a way of repairing harm. This capstone is where you assemble them. Choose one live conflict or harm from your own practice — a real one, suitably anonymised — and carry it through all three canons until you can hand a sceptic a plan that survives their hardest questions. The test is not recall; it is whether you can design a response that the person harmed, the person who caused harm, and a critical colleague would each recognise as honest.

The capstone is a designed sequence, not a menu: each step constrains the next, and an honest critique often sends you back to re-diagnose before you write a single line of script.

The End-to-End Design Walk

A nonviolent response is designed in a sequence, and each step constrains the next. Work them in order — the discipline is refusing to reach for a technique before you have understood the conflict, and refusing to convene anyone before you have checked that it is safe to. Take your one case down this ladder.

1
Diagnose

Describe the conflict or harm. Map the parties, the needs beneath their positions, the power differences, and any safety risks. Decide honestly whether a nonviolent process is appropriate at all — and say why. (Modules 1, 10)

2
Apply the three lenses

Write an NVC reframe of the key exchange (observation, feeling, need, request + one empathic guess); sketch an NVR plan if an authority relationship is involved (announcement, self-control, two supporters, one reconciliation gesture); and design a restorative process (who is centred, what format, the three questions). (Modules 2–9)

3
Critique your own plan

Name the risks: where could this replicate power, coerce, re-traumatise, or romanticise the community? What safeguards will you build? When would you not proceed? (Module 9)

4
Write the facilitation script

Produce a one-page facilitation script or run-of-show you could actually use in the room, grounded in the Module 11 arc. (Module 11)

Exemplar capstone — a serious peer-harm incident in a residential hostel

The situation. In a residential hostel attached to a rural secondary school, an older student, Ravi, has for weeks been coercing a younger boy, Sameer, into handing over his food and pocket money, backing it with threats and one episode of physical intimidation witnessed by three others. A warden found out when Sameer stopped eating and a friend spoke up. The reflex response — expel Ravi, close the file — would remove one boy, teach the dormitory that the way to be safe is to have a warden who likes you, and leave Sameer carrying the shame with no repair. Here is the same case walked through the four steps.

Step 1 — Diagnose. Parties: Sameer (harmed), Ravi (caused harm), three student witnesses, the warden, and — in the background — both boys’ families and the wider dorm. Needs beneath positions: Sameer needs safety, restitution and to stop being seen as a victim; Ravi, on inquiry, is himself food-insecure and newly humiliated after failing a year. The power map is stark: an age-and-size gap between the boys, and a warden who holds punitive authority over both. Safety risk is real and immediate, so the first move is not a circle — it is separating the boys’ routines and confirming Sameer is eating and safe. Only then is a nonviolent process even on the table.

Step 2 — Apply the three lenses. NVC reframes the warden’s opening to Ravi from “you are a thief and a bully” to an observation-feeling-need-request: “When I heard that Sameer’s dinner went to you three nights this week, I felt alarmed, because every child here needs to be safe and fed. Would you be willing to tell me what has been going on for you?” — plus the empathic guess, “My hunch is things have been hard since the exam results.” NVR governs the warden’s stance: a calm announcement to Ravi that the extortion will not continue and that the adults are now present and watching, held with self-control rather than a shouting match; two supporters recruited (a favourite teacher and an uncle) so authority is relational, not lonely; and a later reconciliation gesture that signals Ravi is not being discarded. Restorative justice shapes the repair itself: Sameer is centred, the format is a small facilitated conference (not a whole-dorm spectacle that would re-expose him), and the three questions structure it — what happened, who was affected and how, and what must now be done to put it right, including returning the money and a concrete plan for Ravi’s own food security.

Step 3 — Critique your own plan. Where could this go wrong? A conference held too soon re-traumatises Sameer and lets the size-and-age power gap reassert itself in the room — so Sameer meets the facilitator alone first and holds a veto over whether he sits with Ravi at all. Restorative language can quietly become pressure on the harmed child to forgive; the script must never ask Sameer to reassure Ravi. The warden’s authority could turn the “announcement” into a threat, so the two supporters are partly there to keep the adult honest. And there is a real romanticising risk in treating a hungry, humiliated boy’s coercion as merely a “misunderstanding”: the harm is named plainly, restitution is non-negotiable, and if Ravi will not acknowledge it the process pauses rather than performs. When I would not proceed: if Sameer does not freely want a meeting, if there is any ongoing threat, or if a family insists on a punitive track — then this becomes a safeguarding referral, not a circle.

Step 4 — Write the facilitation script. The deliverable is a one-page run-of-show: a private safety check-in with Sameer; separate preparation meetings with each boy; the conference opening (facilitator names the purpose and the ground rules, confirms consent in the room); the three questions in order, with the money and food-security plan surfaced as the “put it right” step; an explicit close that assigns follow-up dates and a named adult Sameer can go to; and a written note of what would trigger a pause. It is short enough to hold in one hand and specific enough that a nervous facilitator could actually run it.

The test of a nonviolent design

A good plan is not the one with the most technique in it — it is the one that survives the question “and where could this go wrong?” The strongest capstones we see spend as much ink on their safeguards and their “I would not do this if…” conditions as on the process itself. Designing for the failure case is the mark of a practitioner, not a technician.

Deliverables

Submit three things:

  • A 4–6 page case memo covering all four phases above.
  • One usable facilitation script or NVR announcement letter you could take into the room.
  • An explicit “when I would not do this” section — the conditions under which you would refuse, pause, or refer.
