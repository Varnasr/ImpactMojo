Module 6: NVR in Action

NVR is a toolkit of concrete, sequenced practices. Each mirrors a move from political nonviolent resistance, scaled to the household, classroom or care setting. This module walks the sequence — announce, be present, resist through a basket of measures, keep the relationship warm, and never stand alone — and then works a full case through it.

The NVR sequence

The methods are not a menu to pick from at random. They run as a sequence, and the support network runs underneath every step — the adult announces, sits in, applies a measured basket, and reaches out to reconcile, all while held by supporters throughout.

Key terms

Basket of measures
A small, prioritised set of resistance actions the adult is willing to sustain now, chosen because they can be carried out without aggression or collapse. The “basket” image is deliberate: it holds only what you can carry. Resistance is targeted at one or two behaviours, not the whole list of grievances — the rest wait in another basket for later.
The sit-in
The adult enters the young person’s space at a calm moment, states the behaviour that cannot continue, asks for their suggestions, and then simply stays — present, non-hostile, and un-hurried — without demanding compliance or issuing threats. Its power is presence and duration, not force: it signals “we are here, we are not giving up, and we are not attacking you.”
Reconciliation gestures
Unconditional acts of warmth — a favourite meal, a note, a shared errand — deliberately decoupled from whether the behaviour improved. They are not rewards. Their job is to keep the relationship alive so that resistance is felt as “I am against this behaviour” and never as “I am against you.”
Strike while the iron is cold

NVR inverts the folk wisdom. The heated moment — mid-tantrum, mid-fight — is precisely when the adult should not act, because any response then is reactive and escalates. Omer’s rule is delay over immediacy: postpone the response to a calm hour, then deliver it deliberately. “We’ll return to what happened later” is a complete, powerful sentence. The announcement and the sit-in are cold-iron acts by design — planned, rehearsed with supporters, and carried out when tempers have settled, not in the flare-up.

Vigilant care: three levels of attention

Omer’s answer to “how much monitoring is too much” is vigilant care — care that adjusts its intensity to risk, rather than swinging between neglect and surveillance:

  • Open attention — the default: warm interest, open conversation.
  • Focused attention — when worry signs appear: asking more, checking in, coordinating with others.
  • Direct protection — when danger is clear: acting decisively to protect, without secrecy.

South Asian resonance. The NVR “network of supporters” maps naturally onto the extended family and neighbourhood structures of South Asian life — the khandaan, the mohalla, the grandparents, the teacher. Where individualistic parenting advice tells a lone parent to be consistent, NVR’s insistence that “the adult is not alone” is closer to how care actually works here. The caution (Module 9): the same networks can enforce control and honour-based coercion — supporters must be recruited for presence and de-escalation, never for shaming.

Where NVR is used

  • Parenting — child violence, self-destructive behaviour, entitlement/tyrannical behaviour, and (via Omer & Lebowitz’s SPACE programme) childhood anxiety and OCD, by reducing parental accommodation.
  • Schools — whole-school anti-bullying built on vigilant care and adult presence rather than zero-tolerance exclusion.
  • Residential & care settings — de-escalation cultures that reduce restraint.

Working the sequence

Case — persistent bullying at a Chennai school

Class 8 at a government-aided school in Chennai has a bullying problem centred on one boy, Karthik, who has repeatedly humiliated and shoved a quieter classmate, Aravind, over several weeks. Aravind has started missing school. The class teacher, Mrs Fernandes, must respond.

Approach A — zero-tolerance reaction. Karthik is caught mid-incident, marched to the office, suspended for three days, and warned that the next time means expulsion. The punishment is loud, immediate, and satisfying to the adults. But it is struck while the iron is hot: it escalates the fight, casts Karthik as the enemy, and gives him status among peers as the boy who defied the school. He returns angrier, the bullying goes underground, and Aravind is now marked as the one who “told.”

Approach B — NVR. Mrs Fernandes does not confront Karthik in the corridor. She strikes while the iron is cold. First she builds the network: she brings in a second teacher, the year coordinator, and — with care — Karthik’s parents and a respected uncle from the family, recruited for presence and de-escalation, explicitly not for beating or shaming him.

The announcement. At a calm time, two teachers hand Karthik a short written statement and read it aloud: “We know that Aravind has been pushed and humiliated. We cannot accept this. We will not use it against you as an enemy, and we will not give up until it stops. We would like your help.” No threat, no ultimatum — a declaration.

The sit-in. When the behaviour continues, the two teachers sit down with Karthik during a free period, in the presence of the coordinator: “We are here because the pushing has not stopped. We are not leaving angry, and we are not here to punish you today. We’d like your ideas on how it ends.” Then they wait — present, unhurried, silent if need be. The duration and calm carry the message.

The basket — one or two measures, non-escalating. They choose a single behaviour to resist first: the physical shoving (verbal teasing waits in another basket). The measures are gradual — an adult now shadows the corridor and yard at the transition times when incidents cluster, and each parent is informed, once, in writing, transparently, so the problem is out of secrecy. There is no expulsion threat and no public humiliation; if the shoving recurs, the response is a repeat sit-in, not a bigger punishment.

Reconciliation, decoupled. Separately — and regardless of whether Karthik has “improved” — Mrs Fernandes keeps a warm door open: she asks him to help set up the science corner, greets him normally. The gesture is not a reward; it keeps the relationship alive so the resistance lands as “we are against the shoving,” not “we are against you.” Karthik loses his audience and his enemy at the same time, and the shoving has nowhere to escalate.

Reflect. Think of a persistent behaviour you are currently reacting to hot — in the moment, alone, with escalating consequences. What single behaviour would go in your first basket, who would you recruit into an open support network before you announced anything, and — hardest of all — what reconciliation gesture could you sustain even on the days the behaviour has not improved at all?
