Module 5: NVR & New Authority

Where NVC works between rough equals in a conversation, Non-Violent Resistance (NVR) addresses the authority relationship — parent and child, teacher and pupil, carer and cared-for. Israeli psychologist Haim Omer (Tel Aviv University) took Gandhi's and the civil-rights movement's methods of nonviolent resistance and transposed them, deliberately and explicitly, into the family.

The problem: the collapse of authority

Traditional authority ran on hierarchy, distance and the threat of punishment. As that model rightly lost legitimacy, many parents and carers were left oscillating between two failures: authoritarian escalation (trying to reassert control through force, which escalates) and permissive capitulation (giving in to avoid conflict, which abandons the young person to their worst impulses). Omer's New Authority replaces control with presence and the anchoring function: the adult is not the one who wins, but the one who stays.

I am your parent. I cannot make you change, but I will resist your harmful behaviour with all my strength, and I will not give up on you — and I am not alone. The stance of New Authority, paraphrasing Omer

Old authority and new authority

The shift is not from firm to soft. It is a shift in where the authority is located. Old authority is vertical: it flows down a single line from the adult who stands above the child, and its currency is obedience secured by distance and the threat of escalation. If that line is challenged, the only moves left are to press harder or to let go. New authority is networked: the adult is not a lone figure at the top of a hierarchy but an anchored presence held inside a web of allies — the other parent, the extended family, teachers, neighbours, the wider community. Legitimacy no longer rests on the adult overpowering the child; it rests on the adult being rooted, transparent and supported.

Omer's move: authority stops being a single downward line of control and becomes an anchored, supported network — the adult resists from a rooted position, not from above.
New Authority
Haim Omer's reconstruction of adult authority for an age that has rightly abandoned domination. Its strength is not the power to make the child comply but the adult's capacity to remain present, self-controlled, transparent and supported. Its core formula: “we resist, we do not attack.” The adult opposes the harmful behaviour without becoming its mirror — refusing both to strike and to surrender.

The two pillars: presence and self-control

  • Presence. “I am here, I am your parent, you cannot get rid of me.” Not surveillance or control — persistent, warm, unyielding presence that communicates commitment.
  • Self-control (de-escalation). The single hardest discipline: the adult controls only their own behaviour, never the child's. Omer's maxim — “strike while the iron is cold” — inverts the reflex to respond in the heat of the moment. You resist later, deliberately, from a regulated state.
Anchoring: self-control is not control of the other

The whole method turns on one distinction that is easy to say and hard to live. Control of the other asks: how do I make this young person stop? Every answer to that question — a louder threat, a bigger punishment, a withdrawal of love — hands the child the power to escalate, because it makes the adult's stability depend on the child's compliance. Self-control asks a different question: how do I stay anchored while I resist? The anchoring function is what a mooring does for a boat in a storm — it does not stop the waves, it holds a fixed point through them. A regulated, present adult who refuses to be dragged into the fight becomes something the young person can push against without being able to knock over. That immovability, paradoxically, is felt as safety.

The escalation traps. Omer names two: mutual escalation (matching aggression with aggression) and complementary escalation (matching aggression with capitulation). Both teach the young person that escalation works. NVR is the third path: firm resistance without either.

The Gandhian transposition

Omer is unusually explicit that NVR is Gandhi and the American civil-rights movement brought home. From satyagraha he takes: resistance without aggression; the acceptance of the relationship even while refusing the behaviour; publicity over secrecy; the mobilisation of a wider community; and persistence as a form of strength. The child is never the “enemy” — the behaviour is resisted, the person is held.

Case — a fifteen-year-old’s rage

Rukhsana is raising her fifteen-year-old son, Imran, largely alone in a crowded Karachi flat; his father works in the Gulf. Over a hard year Imran has begun exploding — shoving past her, punching a door, threatening to leave and not come back, once knocking a plate from her hand. Each incident leaves her shaking and the household frightened.

Approach A — matching force / then giving in. In the moment Rukhsana meets the threat with a bigger one: she shouts, confiscates his phone, warns that she will send him to his uncle’s. Imran escalates — that is what a threat invites — and the row peaks in slammed doors. Exhausted and afraid of losing him, she reverses course a day later: the phone comes back, nothing is said, the incident is buried. She has now shown him both escalation traps in a single week: force provokes him, and capitulation rewards him. The lesson he absorbs is that rage is the fastest way to get his mother to move.

Approach B — the new-authority response. Rukhsana does not respond in the heat of it; she strikes while the iron is cold. When she is regulated, she delivers a short, unemotional announcement: “I have decided I will no longer accept being pushed or threatened. I am not going to fight you and I am not going to give up on you. This has to change, and I will keep coming back to it.” She does not demand he agree or reply. She then does the thing old authority forbids — she breaks the secrecy: she tells her sister and Imran’s trusted maths tutor what is happening and asks them to be present, to check in, to make the resistance a matter of the wider circle rather than a private duel. When the next incident comes, she stays physically present and calm, names that it is not acceptable, and refuses either to strike back or to be driven off — later, with an ally alongside, she may sit outside his room in a quiet sit-in that says I am still here. The mechanism: by removing both the fight he can win and the surrender he can extract, and by anchoring herself inside a visible network, she stops being an opponent he can topple and becomes a presence he can rely on. The aggression loses its function.

The reversal

Old authority tried to be strong by being above and alone. New authority is strong by being anchored and accompanied. Transparency and the support network are not softness — they are the load-bearing walls that let an adult resist for as long as it takes without escalating and without giving in.
