Module 5: NVR & New Authority

Where NVC works between rough equals in a conversation, Non-Violent Resistance (NVR) addresses the authority relationship — parent and child, teacher and pupil, carer and cared-for. Israeli psychologist Haim Omer (Tel Aviv University) took Gandhi's and the civil-rights movement's methods of nonviolent resistance and transposed them, deliberately and explicitly, into the family.

The problem: the collapse of authority

Traditional authority ran on hierarchy, distance and the threat of punishment. As that model rightly lost legitimacy, many parents and carers were left oscillating between two failures: authoritarian escalation (trying to reassert control through force, which escalates) and permissive capitulation (giving in to avoid conflict, which abandons the young person to their worst impulses). Omer's New Authority replaces control with presence and the anchoring function: the adult is not the one who wins, but the one who stays.

I am your parent. I cannot make you change, but I will resist your harmful behaviour with all my strength, and I will not give up on you — and I am not alone. The stance of New Authority, paraphrasing Omer

The two pillars: presence and self-control

  • Presence. "I am here, I am your parent, you cannot get rid of me." Not surveillance or control — persistent, warm, unyielding presence that communicates commitment.
  • Self-control (de-escalation). The single hardest discipline: the adult controls only their own behaviour, never the child's. Omer's maxim — "strike while the iron is cold" — inverts the reflex to respond in the heat of the moment. You resist later, deliberately, from a regulated state.

The escalation traps. Omer names two: mutual escalation (matching aggression with aggression) and complementary escalation (matching aggression with capitulation). Both teach the young person that escalation works. NVR is the third path: firm resistance without either.

The Gandhian transposition

Omer is unusually explicit that NVR is Gandhi and the American civil-rights movement brought home. From satyagraha he takes: resistance without aggression; the acceptance of the relationship even while refusing the behaviour; publicity over secrecy; the mobilisation of a wider community; and persistence as a form of strength. The child is never the "enemy" — the behaviour is resisted, the person is held.

Varna
If you are locked in a struggle with a young person right now, notice which escalation trap you keep falling into — matching force, or giving in. "Strike while the iron is cold" is the whole discipline in one line: resist later, from a regulated place.
