Module 2: NVC Foundations

Marshall Rosenberg (1934–2015), a clinical psychologist who trained under Carl Rogers and worked in school desegregation in the 1960s American South, distilled a model of communication he called Nonviolent Communication (NVC), also “Compassionate Communication.” Its wager: most conflict escalates not because needs clash, but because we express them as judgments. The word he chose — nonviolent — is a deliberate nod to Gandhi’s ahimsa: not merely the absence of blows, but a positive quality of the heart that stays connected to the humanity of the person in front of you.

Life-alienating communication

Rosenberg catalogued the habits of speech that “block compassion” and predictably trigger defence and counter-attack:

  • Moralistic judgments — implying wrongness or badness in those who don’t act in harmony with our values (“she’s lazy”, “you’re inconsiderate”). Distinct from value judgments about what we need and care about.
  • Making comparisons — a form of judgment that guarantees misery.
  • Denial of responsibility — language that obscures agency: “I had to”, “you make me feel”, “company policy”, “orders.”
  • Communicating desires as demands — where non-compliance is expected to bring blame or punishment.

Underneath, Rosenberg drew on a stark diagnosis (borrowed from Walter Wink and O.J. Harvey): a “domination culture” trains us in the language of who deserves what — the language of deserving, of good and bad people — which makes punishment and reward feel natural and violence righteous.

Life-alienating communication
Any speech that disconnects us from the natural state of compassion — by locating the cause of our feelings in others’ wrongness rather than in our own unmet needs. Rosenberg named four recurring forms: moralistic judgments, comparisons, denial of responsibility, and demands. Each quietly relocates power and accountability outside the speaker, and each all but guarantees the listener will hear an attack rather than a request.
The core move of NVC: from a jackal stance that judges who is wrong to a giraffe stance that names what happened, what one feels, what one needs, and what one is asking for.

Needs, not strategies

The pivot of NVC is the distinction between needs — universal human requirements for wellbeing (safety, respect, autonomy, connection, rest, meaning) — and strategies, the specific things we do to meet them. Conflict lives at the level of strategies; connection is possible at the level of needs, because needs are shared.

Two questions
Rosenberg framed the whole practice as helping two people answer: (1) What is alive in us? and (2) What would make life more wonderful? Everything technical serves those two questions.

Giraffe & Jackal. In his trainings Rosenberg used hand-puppets: the jackal (the language of judgment, close to the ground) and the giraffe (the land animal with the largest heart, and a long neck for perspective). They are teaching devices, not personality types — everyone speaks both.

Case — from “you’re so irresponsible” to a need

A programme lead in a Bengaluru NGO opens her laptop on Monday and sees that a field colleague has again submitted the beneficiary count two days late. The sentence that arrives, fully formed, is: “You’re so irresponsible.” Watch it move.

Approach A — the jackal sentence, said or swallowed. “You’re so irresponsible” is a moralistic judgment: it diagnoses the person, not the event. If she says it, the colleague hears an attack on his character and defends it (“you have no idea what my week looked like”); if she swallows it, it hardens into a story about who he is. Either way the actual problem — a report she can act on — is now buried under a fight about worth.

Approach B — the internal shift, then the words. Before speaking, she runs the sentence backwards through four questions. What did I actually see? Not “irresponsible” but “the count arrived Wednesday for a Monday deadline, the second time this month” — an observation, checkable, free of evaluation. What am I feeling? Under the flash of anger, anxious, and a little alone with it. What need is under that? Not “he should be better” but her own need for reliability and shared responsibility — she cannot plan the donor update without the numbers. What am I asking? A concrete, doable request, not a demand: “Would you be willing to send me the count by Monday noon, or message me by Friday if that won’t be possible?” The out-loud version becomes: “The count came in on Wednesday again this week—I’m anxious because I can’t build the donor report without it, and I need to be able to rely on the timing. Would Monday noon work, or can we find a deadline that does?” Notice what changed inside before any word did: the cause of her feeling moved from his character to her own unmet need, which is the one thing she can actually speak from without attack — and the one thing he can respond to without defending himself.

Reflect. Catch one judgment you made this week about a colleague or family member — the shorter and more certain the label, the better. Trace it back through the four questions: what did you actually observe, what did you feel, and what need of yours was underneath? And the hardest one: are you willing to voice that need as a request the other person could say no to, without it becoming a demand?
