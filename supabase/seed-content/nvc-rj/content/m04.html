Module 04

Empathy, Self-Empathy & the Uses of Force

OFNR is the expressive half of NVC. The receptive half — empathy — is where the real work lives, and where NVC makes its most demanding and most contested claims.

Empathy as presence, not technique

For Rosenberg, empathy is "a respectful understanding of what others are experiencing" — emptying our mind and listening with our whole being. It is not advising, reassuring, correcting, one-upping, or explaining. Empathic listening guesses at the other's feelings and needs ("Are you frustrated because you were hoping for more warning?") and stays until the person feels fully received — signalled by a release of tension or a natural falling-silent.

Self-empathy and the "cause of anger"

Before we can hear another, we often need to hear ourselves. NVC treats anger not as something to suppress or vent, but as an alarm: "at the core of all anger is a need that is not being fulfilled." Rosenberg's provocative move is that others' actions are the stimulus but never the cause of our anger — the cause is our own judgment (the "enemy image"). The practice: hear the judgment, translate it into the unmet need, and only then speak.

Protective vs punitive force

NVC is not pacifism-at-any-cost. Rosenberg distinguished:

  • The protective use of force — acting to prevent injury when there is no time for dialogue (restraining a child from running into traffic), with the sole intent to protect.
  • The punitive use of force — acting to make the other suffer for what they did, in the belief they "deserve" it. NVC holds that punishment reliably damages goodwill and never produces the internal motivation we actually want.

Two questions before any use of force: (1) What do I want this person to do? and (2) What do I want their reasons to be? Punishment can sometimes achieve the first; it almost always poisons the second.

Taking the critiques seriously

NVC is widely taught and widely criticised. A serious practitioner holds both:

  • The "robot" problem. Rote OFNR ("I hear that you feel… because you need…") can sound formulaic and manipulative. Rosenberg agreed: the form without the intention is "jackal in giraffe clothing."
  • The tone-policing / depoliticising critique. When those in power ask the marginalised to express anger "nonviolently," NVC can become a tool of control that reframes structural injustice as an interpersonal communication problem. The claim that others "never cause" our feelings can, misapplied, sound like victim-blaming.
  • The individualism critique. Focusing on needs and feelings can obscure the material and structural conditions that generate conflict.

Our stance: NVC is a superb tool for the interpersonal layer and a poor substitute for the analysis of power. Use it to humanise a conflict, not to de-politicise it. Module 9 returns to this.
