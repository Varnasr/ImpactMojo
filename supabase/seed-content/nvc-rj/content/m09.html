Module 9: Roots, Power & Critique

Restorative justice did not begin in the 1970s, and it is not automatically benign. This module traces its indigenous and South Asian lineages — and gives the serious critiques the weight they deserve.

Older than the movement

Contemporary RJ rediscovered, and sometimes appropriated, practices that indigenous and traditional societies never abandoned:

  • Māori whānau-centred process, which reshaped New Zealand law.
  • First Nations / Navajo peacemaking (Hózhǫǫjí Naat'áanii) — restoring harmony (hózhǫ) rather than assigning blame.
  • Ubuntu in Southern Africa — “a person is a person through other persons” — an ethic invoked (and contested) in the Truth and Reconciliation Commission.
  • South Asian traditions: the panchayat as a forum of community resolution; India’s Gram Nyayalayas Act (2008) and Lok Adalats (settlement-oriented people’s courts); traditions of sulh (reconciliation) and mediation in Muslim communities.
Solid arrows: living traditions the branded practices drew from. Dashed arrows: the critiques that flow back — power imbalance, co-optation & commercialisation, and the erasure of the very origins that gave these practices their authority.

The khap panchayat problem — a required caution. “Community justice” is not restorative merely because it is communal. The khap panchayat, which has ordered social boycotts and endorsed “honour” killings to enforce caste and gender hierarchy, is the anti-model. Community process can reproduce domination as easily as repair. RJ’s safeguards — voluntariness, centring the harmed, refusal of coercion, protection of the vulnerable — are exactly what distinguishes it from oppressive village “justice.” Never romanticise the local.

Naming the critiques precisely

“It has problems” is not a critique. The objections that matter have names, histories, and specific mechanisms. Three deserve to be held sharply.

Co-optation
The absorption of a transformative practice into the institution it was meant to challenge, so that its language survives but its power drains away. Bolted onto a punitive court or an HR department, a “circle” can become a compliance ritual — the vocabulary of repair doing the work of control. A close cousin is commercialisation: NVC and restorative practice sold as branded trainings and certifications, their indigenous and grassroots lineage quietly detached from the invoice.
The feminist critique of forced empathy & reconciliation
Feminist scholars (Kathleen Daly, Julie Stubbs, and others) argue that empathy and reconciliation, when made the goal of a process rather than a possible outcome, become another burden placed on the harmed — disproportionately on women. A survivor invited to “understand” the person who harmed her, or to sit in dialogue with him for the sake of “closure,” is being asked to perform the emotional labour of repair in the very relation where power was abused. Forced empathy is not empathy; forced reconciliation privatises a public wrong.
The decolonial critique of “invention”
Presenting NVC, NVR, or RJ as modern Western “inventions” erases the Māori, First Nations, African, and South Asian traditions they draw from — and then re-sells those traditions back to the communities that kept them alive, now credentialed by a Western trainer. The decolonial point is not that the branded practices are worthless, but that naming a living indigenous practice as someone else’s discovery is itself an act of power.

The feminist critique: gender-based violence

The most important critique concerns intimate-partner and sexual violence. Feminist scholars warn that a face-to-face “dialogue” between a survivor and an abuser can replicate the power imbalance, pressure survivors to reconcile, privatise a public wrong, and re-traumatise. Yet survivors are often failed by the criminal system too. The frontier is survivor-led design: RJ only where the survivor genuinely chooses it, with rigorous safeguards, skilled facilitation, and no pressure to forgive.

Case — the circle that protected the powerful

A women’s collective in a small town runs a restorative circle after a complaint of sustained harassment by a senior male colleague against a junior woman on staff. The facilitator, trained in NVC, opens by inviting everyone to “speak from needs” and to hear each other with empathy.

Approach A — the naive process that reproduced the imbalance. Both parties are seated as equals in the circle. The senior colleague is articulate, senior, and socially connected; he frames his conduct as a “misunderstanding” and names his own “need for respect and belonging.” The facilitator, committed to even-handed empathy, asks the junior woman whether she can “understand where he is coming from” and work toward reconciliation so the team can heal. She, outnumbered by his allies and reading the room, agrees, says the right words, and forgives. The circle records a warm resolution. Nothing about his power over her hiring, references, or daily work has changed — and she now carries the added labour of having publicly absolved him. The form of repair has laundered the abuse.

Approach B — taking the critique seriously. The facilitator treats the power differential, not the interpersonal friction, as the central fact. She screens first: does the survivor genuinely want a process, and which one? No shared circle is convened unless the survivor chooses it, and never as a route to reconciliation. Accountability sits with the person who caused harm — his obligations (to change conduct, to accept structural consequences, to do the work of understanding her experience) are the agenda, and empathy is asked of him, not demanded of her. “Understanding” is never framed as the survivor’s task, forgiveness is never the goal, and the institution’s power arrangements — supervision, reporting lines, sanction — are on the table alongside any dialogue. The test is not “did we hold a circle?” but “did power actually shift?”

Co-optation, net-widening, and the abolitionist turn

  • Dilution / co-optation. Bolted onto punitive systems, RJ can become a soft add-on that changes nothing — or “net-widening,” drawing more people (especially children) into formal control for minor matters.
  • Transformative Justice (TJ). An abolitionist current (generationFIVE, Mia Mingus, INCITE!, Mariame Kaba) argues that harm is produced by structures, and that genuine accountability must be built in communities outside the carceral state, addressing root causes rather than individual incidents. TJ is RJ’s most demanding interlocutor.
Practitioner stance

Hold the tension: RJ is a real improvement on retribution and can be co-opted, romanticised, or unsafe. The test is never “is it a circle?” but “does this process centre those harmed, refuse coercion, and actually shift power and repair?”

Reflect. Take one restorative or NVC practice you use and trace it back — whose tradition did it come from, and does your version still credit them or quietly claim the practice as its own? Now name a case in your own context where “empathy” or “reconciliation” was asked of the person with less power: if you ran it again taking the critique seriously, what would you refuse to do — even if it left the conflict unresolved?
