Module 9: The Delivery Chain

A well-designed intervention can still fail to reach a single beneficiary, because between the designer’s intent and the change in someone’s life lies a chain of hand-offs — funds, staff, inputs, the service act itself, uptake. Each link is imperfect, and the imperfections multiply. This module treats the delivery chain as a first-class design object: something you map, stress-test, and design for, rather than an implementation detail to be sorted out later by someone else. The uncomfortable lesson of “deliverology” (Barber 2015) is that most of the distance between a good idea and a good outcome is lost here.

Mapping the Chain: Intent to Outcome

Every intervention runs a relay from the designer’s intent to the beneficiary’s outcome. Name each hand-off, because a break at any one of them stops the whole race — and the links are rarely the ones a logframe emphasises.

The seven links from design to outcome
Intent
The design as written — the mechanism and target the theory of change specifies (Module 3).
Funds
Money released, on time, to the right account. In India’s schemes this link alone routinely stalls for months.
Staff
A frontline worker present, trained, and motivated to act — the teacher, ANM, ration dealer, or gram rozgar sahayak.
Inputs
The physical thing in the right place — grain in the fair-price shop, a vaccine at the right temperature, a textbook.
Service act
The intended transaction actually performed — the grain weighed out honestly, the class taught, the wage paid.
Uptake
The beneficiary reached, eligible, and willing to use the service — not deterred by distance, stigma, or a failed authentication.
Outcome
The change the intervention exists to produce — nutrition, learning, income — which only appears if all prior links hold.
The chain is multiplicative, not additive

Delivery is a product of probabilities, not a sum. If five links each work 80% of the time — individually respectable — the end-to-end pass rate is 0.8&sup5; ≈ 33%. Two-thirds of the intended effect vanishes without any single link “failing.” This is why marginal effort is often best spent on the weakest link, not the most visible one, and why designers must know their real per-link odds.

Where Interventions Leak: The Last Mile and the Missing Middle

Leakage clusters in two places. The last mile — the final hand-off to the beneficiary — gets most of the attention: the ration dealer who under-weighs, the ghost beneficiary drawing another’s entitlement. But as much damage is done in the missing middle: the district and block administration where funds sit undisbursed, muster rolls are inflated, and no one owns the outcome.

Ghost beneficiaries and diverted grain. In the classic PDS, subsidised grain leaked through duplicate and fictitious ration cards and diversion into the open market — a last-mile and record-keeping failure at once. In MGNREGA, fake names on muster rolls converted a wage-employment guarantee into a rent for local officials. The design was sound; the chain was porous.

Delayed funds are a design failure, not an accident. MGNREGA guarantees wages within fifteen days, yet chronic delays in fund flow from centre to state to panchayat broke the incentive at the core of the scheme. A worker who cannot count on timely payment stops demanding work — so the missing middle silently reduces uptake, the very last link, without anyone at the last mile doing wrong.

State Capacity as a Binding Design Constraint

The deepest lesson of the delivery chain is that you must design for the delivery system you actually have, not the idealised one your theory of change imagines. A design that assumes capacity which is absent will fail regardless of how sound its internal logic is. State capability — the administrative ability to implement — is a binding constraint, not a variable you can wish away (Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock 2017).

Isomorphic mimicry, revisited
Weak systems adopt the forms of capable ones — elaborate MIS dashboards, social audits on paper — while the underlying function stays broken. A design that looks modern can quietly demand capability the chain does not have.

Design test. For every link in your chain, ask: does a real person, in a real office, with real workload and skills, have both the ability and the incentive to perform this act? If the answer rests on a district officer doing five extra hours of unpaid reconciliation each week, the link is fictional. Simplify the design until the chain fits the system, or invest first in the capability the design needs.

Fidelity versus Adaptation: The Central Tension

Once a design meets a real delivery system, every intervention faces the same knife-edge. Implement it too rigidly and it does not fit local reality; implement it too loosely and the mechanism that made it work dissolves. Managing this tension — deciding which elements are the non-negotiable “active ingredient” and which are free to flex — is a core delivery-chain design decision.

High fidelity

Preserves the active mechanism exactly as tested.

Protects against the model being hollowed out en route to scale.

Risk: rigidity that ignores local constraints — committees that never meet, a protocol staff cannot follow.

Local adaptation

Fits the intervention to language, geography, and the delivery system on the ground.

Builds frontline ownership and durability.

Risk: drift — adapt the active ingredient away and the mechanism no longer fires.

Be rigid about the mechanism, flexible about the form

Name the one or two elements that are the intervention — the thing your theory of change says does the causal work — and defend those with high fidelity. Let everything else adapt. A design that specifies both what must not change and what may change gives the delivery chain room to fit reality without dissolving the mechanism.

Technology to Plug the Leaks — Gains and Side Effects

The last decade’s answer to a leaky chain has been to instrument it: biometric authentication and end-to-end computerisation to close the gaps where ghosts and diversion hide. It works — and it introduces a new failure at the uptake link that designers must weigh.

Case — plugging PDS and MGNREGA leakage

The problem: subsidised grain and guaranteed wages leaked through ghost beneficiaries, over-invoicing, and diverted stock — a chain porous at the staff, service-act, and uptake links.

The intervention. Aadhaar-enabled biometric authentication at the point of service, plus end-to-end computerisation of records and fund flow: the beneficiary’s fingerprint is matched before grain is released or a wage is paid, and the transaction is logged in real time. In Andhra Pradesh, Muralidharan, Niehaus and Sukhtankar (2016) evaluated Smartcard-based biometric payments for MGNREGA and pensions in a large randomised rollout.

The gain. Payments arrived faster and more reliably, and the leakage between what the government disbursed and what beneficiaries received fell sharply — the technology closed the last-mile and missing-middle gaps that audits alone had not. Beneficiaries valued the system enough that few wished to return to the old one.

The side effect. Authentication that fails — a worn fingerprint, no network, a name mismatch — converts a leakage-plugging tool into an exclusion error: a genuine beneficiary denied their entitlement at the counter. Plugging a leak at the service-act link can quietly break the uptake link for the most vulnerable, who are least able to travel back or contest a rejection.

The design trade-off is exclusion versus inclusion error. Tighter authentication reduces leakage (fewer ineligibles paid) but raises exclusion (more eligibles wrongly denied). A humane design plugs the leak and builds a fallback — manual override, offline mode, grievance redress — so the uptake link survives the technology meant to protect it.

Reflect. Take one intervention you know and draw its delivery chain from intent to outcome. Estimate the real success probability of each link and multiply them — what fraction of the intended effect survives? Now name the single weakest link, and ask whether a technology fix there would plug a leak or open a new exclusion.
