Module 2: Diagnosing the Problem

Most interventions are answers. Diagnosis is the discipline of first getting the question right — of naming, precisely, what is broken and why it has not already fixed itself. The costliest error in development design is not a bad solution; it is a good solution aimed at the wrong problem. A borewell for a village whose water crisis is one of distribution, not supply, is faithfully drilled, correctly maintained, and useless. This module builds the diagnostic habits that decide whether everything downstream is aimed true.

Problem-Driven, Not Solution-Driven

Organisations accumulate solutions — a training curriculum, an app, a self-help-group model, a conditional transfer — and then go looking for problems those solutions fit. This is the solution in search of a problem trap, and it is seductive precisely because the solution already works somewhere. Problem-driven design reverses the arrow: it begins with a sharply specified problem and lets the diagnosis dictate the class of solution, even when the answer is one the organisation does not sell.

The tell. If your team can name the intervention before it can state the problem in one falsifiable sentence, you are solution-driven. The test Pritchett, Samji and Hausmann call problem-driven iterative adaptation (Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock 2013) starts the other way round: name the problem, make it locally felt, and let the solution emerge from iteration rather than from the catalogue.

Root-Cause Tools — and Their Limits

The standard diagnostic toolkit — the problem tree, the “5 whys” — is useful for one thing: forcing a team past the first, shallow explanation. Ask why children are malnourished, and “not enough food” gives way, five whys down, to a mother’s time poverty or a caste-segregated ration shop. But these tools have a structural weakness worth naming.

Two tools, one shared flaw
Problem tree
Maps a core problem into its causes (roots) and effects (branches). Good for shared understanding; weak at telling you which root is binding — it treats every cause as co-equal.
5 whys
Chains “why?” to drill from symptom to cause. Good for depth; but a single chain follows one path and quietly ignores the others.
The shared flaw
Both can be run backwards from a pre-chosen answer — a team that wants to build toilets will find, five whys down, a tree whose deepest root is conveniently “no toilets.” The method rationalises the conclusion instead of testing it.

A root-cause exercise is only as honest as the team running it. Used to explore, it opens the problem; used to justify, it launders a decision already taken. The corrective is to force a ranking: not what are the causes, but which single cause, relaxed, would move the outcome most.

Causation flows upward: an intervention aimed at the visible symptoms at the top — more testing, attendance drives — leaves untouched the binding constraint two levels down at the roots.

Binding-Constraints Thinking

That ranking question is the heart of growth diagnostics, developed to explain why economies stall (Hausmann, Rodrik & Velasco 2005). Its logic transfers cleanly to social problems. At any moment, many things are wrong, but they are not equally wrong: one constraint binds, and relaxing it moves the outcome far more than fixing the others. Pour resources into a non-binding constraint and you get almost nothing — more textbooks where the constraint is instruction, more clinics where the constraint is trust.

Find the one constraint that moves the outcome

The diagnostic question is not “what are all the barriers?” — there are always many — but “which barrier, if removed, releases the largest change per rupee?” The signature of a binding constraint: actors are visibly working around it, its shadow price is high, and small relaxations produce outsized responses. A well-designed intervention attacks that constraint and, deliberately, ignores the rest.

Isomorphic Mimicry and Premature Load-Bearing

A distinct diagnostic failure is to copy the form of something that works while missing its function — what Andrews, Pritchett and Woolcock (2017) call isomorphic mimicry, after organisms that evolve the appearance of a stronger species. A ministry adopts the anti-corruption commission, the results framework, the grievance portal — the visible signatures of a capable state — while the underlying function they signal never takes root. The form earns legitimacy; the function is absent.

Premature load-bearing. The related trap is asking a borrowed form to carry weight its function cannot yet bear — devolving budgets to village committees that have never managed money, or mandating outcome-based contracts where no one can measure outcomes. The wall looks finished, so real load is placed on it, and it fails under exactly the pressure it was built to advertise it could take.

Behavioural Diagnosis: What Kind of Problem Is It?

Before choosing an instrument, classify the problem by the behaviour it turns on — because each type demands a different class of intervention, and matching the wrong class is the diagnostic error that dooms the design.

Problem typeThe person…Intervention class it implies
Informationwould act, but does not know the fact, the benefit, or the way.Disclosure, reminders, awareness, simplification.
Incentiveknows, but the payoff to acting is too small, too slow, or too risky.Transfers, subsidies, pricing, insurance, penalties.
Capacityknows and wants to, but lacks the skill, tool, time, or infrastructure.Training, provision, access, service redesign.
Belief / normcould act, but the surrounding social norm or belief makes the act costly or unthinkable.Norm-shifting, community mobilisation, role models, collective commitment.

The same surface problem can be any of these. Low immunisation might be an information problem (parents unaware of the schedule), an incentive problem (the clinic is a half-day’s lost wages away — solved by Banerjee et al.’s lentil incentives in Rajasthan), a capacity problem (no cold chain), or a norm problem (rumour and distrust). Only the diagnosis tells you which lever to pull; guess wrong and the intervention is correct for a problem you do not have.

Case — open defecation and Swachh Bharat

India’s sanitation problem was long diagnosed as an access problem — people defecate in the open because they have no toilet — and the answer followed automatically: build toilets. Earlier campaigns and, at first, the Swachh Bharat Mission poured subsidies into latrine construction and counted units built.

But the binding constraint, research showed, was rarely access. Households with functioning latrines still chose open defecation, driven by beliefs about purity, pollution and caste, and a norm that open defecation was healthier or more manly (Coffey & Spears 2017). This was a belief/norm problem wearing the mask of a capacity problem. A toilet is the right instrument for a capacity constraint and the wrong one for a norm constraint — so latrines were built and then used to store grain.

The contrast. Community-Led Total Sanitation (CLTS) diagnosed the problem as a norm and attacked it as one — no subsidy, no construction target, but a facilitated collective “triggering” of disgust and shared commitment that shifted the norm village by village. Swachh Bharat’s later, more effective phase absorbed exactly this lesson, pairing construction with intensive behaviour-change communication. Same sector, same outcome sought — the diagnosis, not the toilet, determined whether the design could work.

Diagnosis before design

The intervention class is not a choice made after the problem is understood — it is implied by how the problem is understood. Misdiagnose the type and no amount of implementation quality can save you: a norm problem answered with hardware, an incentive problem answered with a poster, a capacity problem answered with a rule. Get the diagnosis right and the design half-writes itself; get it wrong and every later module is compounding a first-order error.

Reflect. Take a problem your organisation is working on. Run the classification: is it, at its binding constraint, an information, incentive, capacity, or belief/norm problem — and be honest about whether your current intervention matches that type or the type you wish it were. Then ask the harder question: could you have run a problem tree backwards from the solution you already had?
