Module 1: The Intervention Mindset

Development practice is crowded with things that sound like solutions — a scheme, a policy, a campaign, a pilot. This course is about a narrower and harder object: the intervention, a deliberately designed package of activities meant to change a specific outcome for specific people, through a mechanism you can name. Getting the object right is the first design decision, because most failures are not failures of effort or funding. They are failures of design that were baked in before anyone reached the field.

Policy, Scheme, Project, Intervention — Say Which You Mean

These words are used interchangeably in proposals and they should not be. A policy is a governing rule or allocation — "every child aged 6–14 has a right to free schooling." A scheme is a standing government programme with a budget line. A project is a time-bound bundle of funded activities. An intervention is the causal heart inside any of these: the specific thing you do, to specific people, expected to work through a specific mechanism.

Four objects, four questions
Policy
Asks what should be true. The Right to Education Act declares an entitlement. It does not, by itself, teach a single child to read.
Scheme
Asks what is funded and administered. Samagra Shiksha is the delivery vehicle — budget, staff, guidelines.
Project
Asks what we will do, by when. A three-year district literacy project with a grant and a logframe.
Intervention
Asks what change, in whom, through what mechanism. Grouping children by learning level rather than grade so instruction meets them where they are. This is the design object.

The discipline this course keeps is to force every proposal down to its intervention: strip away the policy language and the budget lines until you can state, in one sentence, who is changed, how, and why that mechanism should work. If you cannot, there is no design yet — only an intention.

The Design Pipeline

An intervention travels a pipeline, and design decisions at each stage constrain every stage that follows. The whole course is organised around it.

1Diagnose

Understand the problem before choosing a solution. What kind of problem is this — information, incentive, capacity, belief? (Module 2)

2Theorise

Write a falsifiable theory of change: the mechanism and the assumptions it rides on. (Module 3)

3Design

Choose and adapt a model — cash, a bundle, a frontline cadre, a collective, a service redesign. (Modules 4–10)

4Pilot

Test at small scale, honestly. Learn whether the mechanism fires and whether delivery holds.

5Scale

The hard part. Most of what works in a pilot loses its voltage on the way up. (Modules 11–13)

6Institutionalise

Hand the intervention to a system that will run it after the designers leave. Survival, not launch, is the test.

The designer's leverage is front-loaded

Each stage narrows the options of the next. A weak diagnosis cannot be rescued by a clever pilot; an intervention that ignores the delivery system cannot be rescued at scale. The cheapest place to fix a design is at the diagnosis; the most expensive is after national rollout. This is why the course spends its first three modules before naming a single model.

Why Good Intentions Fail

Well-funded, well-meant interventions fail in patterned ways. Naming the patterns is the beginning of designing against them.

Failure modeWhat it looks likeWhere it is designed in
Solution in search of a problemThe tool (an app, a training, a kit) is chosen first; the problem is reverse-engineered to fit it.Diagnosis (Module 2)
Isomorphic mimicryThe intervention copies the form of a successful model without its function — committees that never meet, plans no one uses.Design (Modules 2, 13)

The first failure mode is the most seductive, because it feels like action. Case — two responses to the same problem

The problem: primary-school children in a district can attend for five years and still not read a simple paragraph — the "schooling is not learning" gap documented across South Asia (ASER, Pratham).

Response A — more of the same. Build more classrooms, distribute more textbooks, add contract teachers. Inputs rise; the reading gap barely moves, because the binding constraint was never the number of inputs. This is a solution chosen before the problem was diagnosed.

Response B — redesign the mechanism. Pratham's Teaching at the Right Level regroups children by what they can actually do, not by grade, for a focused daily block. The same teachers and the same hours produce large, replicated reading gains. The intervention changed the mechanism of instruction, not the volume of inputs.

Same problem, same sector, opposite outcomes — and the difference was entirely a design decision made before implementation. Module 8 returns to this case in full.

Three Actors, Three Design Logics

Interventions are delivered by the state, by markets, or by civil society, and each carries a different design logic — different reach, different incentives, different failure modes. A designer's first structural choice is who delivers, because it silently sets what is possible.

State reaches everyone and can compel and fund at scale, but moves at the speed of bureaucracy and is bound by capacity. Markets are efficient and self-scaling where people can pay, and blind where they cannot. Civil society is trusted, close to the last mile, and inventive, but rarely reaches population scale on its own. The most durable interventions in this course — graduation, SHGs, ASHA — are hybrids that route each task to the actor best suited to it.

Reflect. Take one programme you know well. Strip away its policy language and budget until you can state its intervention in a single sentence: who is changed, how, and through what mechanism. Now place it on the design pipeline — which stage was strongest, and which failure mode from the table is it most exposed to?
