Module 12: Pathways to Scale

A pilot that works is a beginning, not a victory. Between a proven model and a changed population sits a gap that swallows most good interventions — not because the evidence was wrong, but because nobody chose, deliberately, which road to scale the intervention would travel. There are only three real roads: through the state, through replication by civil society, or through the market. Each buys a different mix of reach, fidelity, and durability, and each exacts a different price. This module is about choosing the road with your eyes open, and about the harder question hiding underneath it — scaling what, exactly?

Three Roads From Pilot to Population

Scale is not a single act. It is a choice among delivery channels, made long before the last district is reached. Government adoption offers reach and durability but dilutes the model as it passes through the bureaucracy. NGO replication protects fidelity but rarely touches population scale. Market delivery is self-sustaining where people can pay and blind where they cannot. Most designers back into one of these by default; the discipline is to pick on purpose.

Government adoption

Reach: population-scale, potentially universal.

Fidelity: low — the model is diluted by civil-service incentives.

Durability: high — it lives on a budget line after you leave.

Cost per head: lowest, using existing staff and systems.

NGO replication

Reach: bounded by your own fundraising and management span.

Fidelity: high — you control training, supervision, and culture.

Durability: tied to grant cycles; ends when funding does.

Cost per head: high — you pay for the whole delivery machine.

Market / commercial

Reach: self-scaling wherever demand and ability-to-pay exist.

Fidelity: shaped by price competition, not by your design.

Durability: high while the business is viable.

Cost per head: borne by the user — which excludes the poorest.

No road gives you reach, fidelity, and durability at once. Choosing government buys reach and durability at the cost of fidelity; choosing an NGO buys fidelity at the cost of reach; choosing the market buys durability at the cost of the poorest. The pathway is a trade-off, and pretending otherwise is how programmes drift into a channel that cannot carry them.

Scaling What? The Question Beneath the Question

Before choosing a road, decide what actually travels. "Scale the programme" is usually the wrong instruction. What replicates well is often the principle behind the programme, not its exact form — a lesson learned painfully whenever a lovingly detailed model is copied and produces nothing (the isomorphic mimicry of Module 1). Distinguish three verbs and three objects.

You can scale…What travelsWhen it is the right target
The organisationYour team, brand, and delivery machine grow to new places.When the result depends on your specific culture and supervision, and quality cannot be codified.
The modelThe full package — protocol, materials, staffing — is handed to others to run.When the package is well-specified and its parts are the active ingredient.
The principleThe insight (e.g. "teach to the child's level, not the grade") diffuses into how others already work.When the mechanism is robust but the exact form must bend to each system.

Three directions of travel cut across these. Scaling-up moves the intervention vertically into policy and institutions — a budget line, a national guideline. Scaling-out replicates it horizontally to more people and places. Scaling-deep shifts the norms and relationships that hold a problem in place, so change outlasts any single programme (the Waterloo scaling typology, McConnell 2017). A serious scaling plan usually needs all three, not one.

Scale the insight, not the artefact

The interventions that reach millions rarely do so by cloning the pilot. They isolate the one mechanism that carries the effect, strip away the scaffolding the pilot needed, and let host systems rebuild the rest in their own idiom. Ask of every element: is this the active ingredient, or the packaging? Only the active ingredient must travel intact.

The Handoff to Government

The state is the only actor that can reach every child, every farmer, every village — so most durable scale runs through it eventually. But a lean pilot built on hand-picked, highly motivated staff must survive contact with a system that runs on transfers, procurement rules, seniority, and a thousand competing mandates. What survives the handoff is what has been designed to survive it.

What survives contact with the bureaucracy
Incentive fit
Tasks that a transferred, overstretched government teacher or ANM can actually do within existing duties survive; tasks that assume a dedicated, mission-driven cadre do not.
Procurement & staffing reality
If the model needs materials the system cannot procure or posts it cannot sanction, it dies quietly at the first budget cycle.
Simplicity of the protocol
A one-page instruction that a monitoring officer can verify scales; a rich but tacit practice that lived in the trainers’ heads evaporates.
A political owner
Without a secretary or minister whose name is on the result, the reform has no defender when priorities shift.

The embedded-partner model has become the dominant route for evidence organisations in South Asia: rather than delivering at scale themselves, they sit inside government. J-PAL and IPA run state cells that co-design and evaluate reforms; Pratham partners with state education departments to train and mentor government teachers. The NGO supplies rigour and the missing capability; the state supplies reach, staff, and permanence. The intervention scales as the government’s own programme, not the NGO’s (Kremer, Gugerty 2018 J-PAL scaling).

Adaptive Scaling: Iterate, Don’t Freeze

The instinct at scale is to lock the model — write the manual, forbid deviation, protect fidelity. But an intervention that worked in three districts meets genuinely new constraints in the next three hundred, and a frozen blueprint cannot answer them. Problem-Driven Iterative Adaptation (Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock 2017) argues the opposite: treat scaling as continued problem-solving, adapting the model to each context through fast cycles of trying, learning, and adjusting, while holding the core mechanism fixed. Fidelity to the mechanism, flexibility on the form.

Scale is not a single leap but a staircase, and the evidence does not travel with you up the stairs. An effect measured at 5,000 households can shrink, vanish, or reverse at 500,000 — delivery quality falls, the marginal participant is less motivated, general-equilibrium effects kick in. Scaling-readiness frameworks (3ie; J-PAL) therefore insist on re-testing at each step: pilot, then a larger at-scale trial under real delivery conditions, then monitored rollout — not one small RCT stretched to justify a national programme (bridge to Module 13).

Case — government adoption vs NGO replication

The intervention: Teaching at the Right Level (TaRL), the Pratham model that regroups children by learning level for a focused daily block and produces large, replicated reading and arithmetic gains.

Road one — NGO delivery. Pratham runs TaRL directly with its own volunteers and staff. Fidelity is superb and the results are clean, but reach is capped at what Pratham can fundraise and manage, and delivery ends where the grant ends. High fidelity, bounded reach, fragile durability.

Road two — government adoption. In Haryana’s Mission Buniyaad, the model was handed to the state to run through its own government teachers across thousands of schools. Reach multiplied enormously and it sat on the state’s budget. But fidelity had to be defended constantly: government teachers had competing duties, the daily level-based block was the first thing squeezed, and gains held only where mentoring and political ownership held. Population reach and durability, bought with a permanent fight over fidelity.

The lesson. Neither road is "correct." Pratham keeps both alive on purpose — direct delivery to prove and refine the model, government partnership to reach the population — and what it hands the state is the principle (teach to level) plus the minimum protocol to enact it, not its full organisational apparatus. The pathway choice was a design decision, made deliberately, not a default. Module 13 takes up how to keep the mechanism alive once the state owns it.

Reflect. Take an intervention you would like to see reach a whole population. Which of the three roads — government, NGO replication, market — can actually carry it, given who it serves and who can pay? Now name what would travel down that road: the organisation, the model, or just the principle? What is the one active ingredient that must survive intact, and what is packaging you can let each host system rebuild?

“The goal is not to scale a project, but to scale a solution — and often the solution is an idea that others must be free to make their own.”— after Lant Pritchett, Michael Woolcock & Matt Andrews
