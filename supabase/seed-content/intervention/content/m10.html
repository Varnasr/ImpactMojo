Module 10: Incentives, Targeting & Cost-Effectiveness

Three questions decide whether a well-theorised intervention survives contact with the real world. Will every actor in the delivery chain actually want to do their part? Are you reaching the people you meant to reach, and at what cost of trying? And is this the best use of the rupee, judged against the outcome you care about? This module treats incentives, targeting, and cost-effectiveness not as afterthoughts for the evaluation team but as design decisions — each one capable of quietly dismantling a mechanism that looked sound on paper.

Incentive Compatibility Across the Delivery Chain

An intervention is a chain of actors — the frontline worker, the supervising official, the vendor, the beneficiary — and each will act on their own incentives, not on your logframe. A design that asks any link to work against its own interest is a design that leaks. The discipline is to walk the chain and ask, at each node, will this person find it in their interest to do their part? Where the answer is no, no amount of training or exhortation will hold.

Two classic traps
Principal–agent problem
The designer (principal) cannot observe everything the frontline agent does. The agent optimises for what is measured and rewarded — so an ASHA paid per institutional delivery pushes deliveries, and an official rewarded for disbursement speed disburses without checking eligibility.
Multitasking problem
When you reward one task, effort drains from the unrewarded ones. Pay teachers for test scores and they teach to the test; pay for enrolment and quality slides. Strong incentives on a narrow metric distort the wider job (Holmstrom & Milgrom).

Design that fights incentives loses. Before adding a monitoring rule or a penalty, ask whether the underlying incentive can be aligned instead. A vendor paid on delivery of a working borewell, not on drilling a hole, has no reason to walk away; a beneficiary whose cash transfer is unconditional has no reason to game a condition that was never worth enforcing.

Targeting as a Design Trade-Off

Targeting is where equity ambition meets administrative reality. Every targeting rule makes two kinds of mistake at once, and tightening against one loosens the other. There is no rule that eliminates both, so the design question is not "how do we target perfectly?" but "which error is cheaper to tolerate here, and does the cost of targeting exceed what it saves?"

The cost of targeting can exceed the savings

India's debate over a universal Public Distribution System versus a tightly targeted one turns on exactly this. Fine means-testing sounds efficient, but proxy-means tests misclassify large shares of the poor, verification is expensive, and the excluded are precisely those least able to appeal. A broader, near-universal benefit accepts leakage in exchange for near-zero exclusion and low administrative cost — often the better design for a cheap, essential good. Targeting is a trade-off to be tuned to the benefit, not a virtue to be maximised. Module 7 treated the political economy of universal versus targeted; here it is a costed design choice.

Cost-Effectiveness as a Design Discipline

The most common error in comparing interventions is to compare cost per output — cost per training delivered, per kit distributed — when what you care about is cost per outcome. An intervention can be cheap per output and ruinously expensive per unit of the change you actually want. Cost-effectiveness analysis forces the denominator to be the outcome: cost per additional year of schooling, per DALY averted, per LAYS (learning-adjusted year of schooling).

Unit-cost thinking is a design habit, not just an evaluation output. Build the cost per outcome into the theory of change from the start: estimate the effect size the mechanism can plausibly deliver, divide the all-in cost by it, and see whether the number is defensible before you pilot. The sibling Cost Effectiveness 101 flagship walks through building these ratios; the effect sizes they rely on come from the causal designs in the MEL and Causal Inference flagships.

Case — comparing two interventions on cost-effectiveness

A state education department has a fixed budget and one goal: more learning. Two proposals sit on the table, each rigorously evaluated, each reporting a real, positive effect.

Proposal A — merit scholarships. Cash awards to high-scoring girls. All-in cost ₹4,000 per girl reached; the evaluation finds an average gain of 0.05 standard deviations in test scores across those reached. Cost per LAYS is high, because most of the spend flows to girls who would have progressed anyway — and the effect is thin.

Proposal B — targeted remedial instruction. A Teaching-at-the-Right-Level block for the lowest-performing children. All-in cost ₹250 per child; the evaluation finds a gain of 0.15 standard deviations, concentrated on children furthest behind (Banerjee et al. 2017). Cost per LAYS is a fraction of Proposal A’s.

Judged on cost per output — children reached — the two look comparable. Judged on cost per outcome, Proposal B delivers roughly an order of magnitude more learning per rupee, and delivers it to the children with the most to gain. The comparison only becomes visible once the denominator is the outcome, not the activity.

Comparative Cost-Effectiveness — and Its Limits

League tables of cost-effectiveness — GiveWell’s charity rankings, 3ie’s evidence syntheses — are powerful for triage. They can reveal that a neglected intervention buys ten times the outcome per dollar of a fashionable one. But a ratio is a guide, not a verdict, and treating it as one is its own design failure.

What CEA does well

Puts rival interventions on one axis; exposes cheap outputs masquerading as cheap outcomes; forces effect sizes and costs into the open; makes triage under a fixed budget honest.

Where it misleads

Ratios travel badly — deworming’s cost-effectiveness collapses where worm prevalence is low (external validity). It is blind to equity, to who gains, and to outcomes that resist a common metric. Contested effect sizes give falsely precise ratios.

The deworming cost-effectiveness debate is the cautionary tale. The headline ratio was extraordinary, but it rested on one long-run effect estimate and on a prevalence context that does not generalise (Baird et al. 2016; the wider replication dispute). The lesson is not that CEA is useless — it is that a cost-effectiveness ratio is an input to judgement, weighted by external validity and equity, never a substitute for it.

Reflect. Take an intervention you are designing. Walk its delivery chain and name the one actor most likely to find their part against their interest — how would you realign it? Then state your targeting rule and say which error, inclusion or exclusion, you are choosing to tolerate and why. Finally, write your cost per outcome as a single number: is it defensible next to an alternative on the same outcome?

“The question is never simply ‘does it work?’ but ‘does it work, for whom, at what cost, compared to what else we could have done with the money?’”— after the cost-effectiveness tradition in development economics
