Module 3: Theory of Change as a Design Instrument

A theory of change is the most abused document in development. In most files it is a diagram drawn after the design was fixed, to satisfy a donor template — a decorative box-and-arrow chart no one revisits. This module treats it as something far more useful and far more demanding: a falsifiable hypothesis about how change happens, the spine that holds an intervention upright. A good theory of change can be wrong, and knowing exactly how it could be wrong is what turns it from paperwork into a design instrument.

The Theory of Change Is a Hypothesis, Not a Diagram

A theory of change (ToC) states the causal claim inside your intervention: if we do these activities, then these outputs will trigger this mechanism, which will produce this outcome, because a set of assumptions holds. The word that matters is because. Carol Weiss, who gave the field the term, insisted that a theory of change is valuable precisely when it is specific enough to be proven wrong (Weiss 1995). A claim that cannot fail cannot guide design.

The anatomy of a theory of change
Inputs & activities
What you fund and do — train, transfer, build, convene. The part organisations control directly.
Outputs
The immediate, countable products of activity — women trained, kits delivered, meetings held. Necessary, but not yet change.
Mechanism
The behavioural or systemic reason an output becomes an outcome — the causal engine. This is the part most theories leave blank.
Outcome & assumptions
The change you are actually after, and the conditions that must hold for the mechanism to fire. The assumptions are where the theory is most likely to break.

Andrea Vogel's review of the concept for DFID found that the term had drifted to mean almost anything — a results chain, a narrative, a workshop output — and that its power was recoverable only when practitioners wrote the assumptions and the causal pathway explicitly, not just the boxes (Vogel 2012). The rest of this module is about doing exactly that.

Assumptions Are the Weakest Link

Every arrow in a theory of change hides an assumption — a condition that must be true for the arrow to hold. Interventions rarely fail because the activities were not done; they fail because a load-bearing assumption quietly did not hold. The designer’s job is not to list every assumption but to find the killer one: the assumption that is both uncertain and consequential, the single point where the whole theory is most likely to snap.

Rank assumptions on two axes: uncertainty (how confident are we this holds?) and consequence (if it fails, does the intervention still work?). The assumptions in the high-uncertainty, high-consequence corner are your killer assumptions. Design a cheap test for each one before committing the budget — that test is often worth more than the pilot itself.

Design for the Mechanism, Not the Activity

The most common flaw in a theory of change is to mistake the activity for the mechanism — to assume that doing the thing is the change. "We trained 500 women" is an output. Whether training changes anything depends on a mechanism: does new knowledge alter what women actually do, and does that altered behaviour survive contact with markets, households, and time? Activity thinking counts what you deliver; mechanism thinking asks why the delivery should move the outcome at all.

Activity thinking

Success = activities completed.

"We held 40 trainings and delivered 500 toolkits."

Assumes output automatically becomes outcome.

Reports what was done; silent on why it should work.

Fails invisibly — targets met, outcome flat.

Mechanism thinking

Success = the causal engine fires.

"Training raises confidence and skill, so women adopt a higher-value crop and sell it."

Names the behaviour that turns output into outcome.

States what must be true for the arrow to hold.

Fails loudly — you can see which link broke.

Results chains and the dose-response question

An outcome is rarely produced by one activity; it is produced by a causal package — a chain of linked outputs and behaviours that must all hold together. Once you have named the chain, ask the question activity thinking never asks: is the dose enough? Three days of training, a one-time transfer, a single visit — each is a dose, and a dose too small to move the outcome is not a smaller version of success but a designed-in failure. Graduation programmes work partly because they deliver a large, bundled, sustained dose; a thin version of the same package moves nothing.

From Theory of Change to Logframe — Without Losing the Theory

The logical framework (Logframe 101) is the donor’s reporting grid: objectives, indicators, means of verification, assumptions, arranged in rows. It is useful for accountability and dangerous for design, because its tidy rows flatten the causal logic — the mechanism disappears into an "output" cell, and the assumptions get demoted to a narrow final column no one reads. A theory of change is written to be argued with; a logframe is written to be audited against. Build the theory of change first, then compress it into the logframe — never the reverse.

The flattening risk. When a logframe is written first, the mechanism is never stated, so the intervention is managed by counting outputs. Staff hit every target in the grid while the outcome stays flat — and because the grid has no row for "why this should work," no one can see where the theory broke. Keep the theory of change as the living document; let the logframe be its compressed shadow, not its replacement.

Case — a theory of change for an SHG livelihoods programme

The design: organise poor rural women into self-help groups, train them in a higher-value activity (say, vegetable cultivation or dairy), link them to credit, and expect household income to rise — the logic behind large programmes like India’s NRLM.

The tidy theory. Form groups → train women → women adopt the new activity → production rises → income rises → wellbeing improves. Every arrow looks obvious, which is exactly why the theory is dangerous: obviousness hides the assumptions.

The killer assumption. Surface the assumptions behind each arrow and one stands out — between "production rises" and "income rises" sits the silent claim that trained women will find a market willing to buy their produce at a viable price. It is highly uncertain (local markets may be thin, saturated, or controlled by intermediaries) and fatal if false (a shed full of unsold vegetables is not income). This is the market-access assumption, and it is the load-bearing wall of the whole design.

How testing it early changes the design. Instead of training 500 women and discovering the market problem at the end, run a cheap early test: place a small first cohort’s produce and watch what happens to price and offtake. If the market clears, scale the training. If it does not, the design changes before the money is spent — add an aggregation and buyer-linkage component, sequence production to demand, or choose a different activity entirely. Testing the killer assumption first is worth more than a perfectly executed programme built on a false one.

Sibling reference — the counterfactual spine. The MEL flagship treats the same causal claim from the measurement side: a theory of change tells you what to measure and where the causal link is that a counterfactual must isolate. Your theory of change names the mechanism; the counterfactual spine tests whether that mechanism, and not something else, produced the change. Design and evaluation are two readings of one hypothesis — write the theory of change so the evaluator can find the link you are claiming.

Reflect. Take one intervention you know and write its theory of change as an if…then…because sentence, not a diagram. Now list the assumptions hiding in each arrow and rank them by uncertainty × consequence. Which is the killer assumption — and what is the cheapest test you could run before committing the budget?

"A theory of change is only useful if it is specific enough to be wrong. The point is not to defend it, but to find out where it breaks — while the change is still cheap to make."— after Carol Weiss (1995) & Isabel Vogel (2012)
