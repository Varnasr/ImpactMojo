Module 11: Why Things Don’t Scale — The Voltage Effect

Here is the most disorienting fact in development practice: an intervention can produce large, real, well-measured effects in a trial and then produce almost nothing when a government runs it at national scale. The effect did not shrink because the idea was wrong. It shrank because the conditions that generated the effect — the people, the implementers, the small size itself — do not survive multiplication. John List calls this loss of effect the voltage drop, and learning to predict it before you scale is the intellectual core of intervention design (List 2022).

The Voltage Metaphor, Stated Precisely

Electrical voltage falls as current travels along a wire; the further it goes, the weaker it arrives. List borrows the image for the gap between the effect an intervention shows in a pilot and the effect that reaches people once it is scaled. The question a designer must ask is not merely “does it work?” but “will it keep its voltage?” Al-Ubaydli, List and Suskind call this the science of using science — the discipline of judging, in advance, whether a piece of evidence is the kind that will scale or the kind that will collapse (Al-Ubaydli et al. 2017; List 2022).

Voltage drop
The reduction in an intervention’s treatment effect as it moves from a controlled pilot to full-scale delivery. A programme that lifts test scores by 0.30 SD in a trial and 0.05 SD at scale has lost five-sixths of its voltage.
Scaling is not a growth stage; it is a different intervention

When you scale, you change who receives the programme, who delivers it, and the environment it operates in. The thing running at national scale is not a bigger copy of the pilot — it is a new intervention that happens to share a name. The pilot’s evidence is evidence about the pilot, not a promise about the scaled version.

The voltage drop: an intervention’s measured effect dissipates at each step away from the ideal trial — the bars are illustrative, but the direction is the recurring finding of List’s scaling work.

The Five Voltage Drops

List identifies five distinct reasons an effect fails to travel. They are not interchangeable: each has a different early warning sign and a different design-time remedy. A serious scaling decision interrogates all five before committing a budget.

4. SpilloversGeneral-equilibrium effects invisible at pilot size appear at scale: market prices shift, services congest, politics reacts.If everyone got this at once, what would move — wages, prices, queues, incentives? Does the mechanism depend on few people having it?

Drops 2 and 3 are the quiet killers. They rarely show up as failure in the pilot — the pilot looks like a triumph. That is exactly why they mislead: the very features that made the trial succeed (eager participants, gifted implementers, intensive oversight) are the features that will not survive scale.

The Pilot-to-Scale Collapse Mechanism

Why do pilots systematically flatter interventions? Because the machinery of a good pilot selects for success in ways that vanish at scale. Participants who enrol in a trial are motivated volunteers, not the reluctant average citizen. Implementers are often hand-picked, trained by the designers, and watched closely. And the delivery agent is frequently the researchers themselves or a high-capacity NGO — not the line bureaucracy that will actually run the programme.

The principal–agent gap. At scale the designer (principal) hands delivery to a bureaucracy (agent) whose incentives, capacity and attention differ. The agent has other duties, weaker monitoring, and no personal stake in the mechanism firing. The intervention that survives is not the one the pilot tested — it is whatever the agent can be made to deliver reliably.

Case — NGO vs Government Implementation

Case — the Kenya contract teachers

An extra contract teacher, hired locally on a short-term contract and accountable to the community, had raised learning in earlier trials. Kenya tested the same intervention two ways at once: delivered by an NGO, and delivered by the government — the body that would actually run it at scale (Bold et al. 2018).

The result split cleanly. Under NGO delivery the contract teachers raised test scores, replicating the earlier finding. Under government delivery the effect was essentially zero. The intervention had not changed — the implementer had. Political and bureaucratic dynamics (union pressure, delayed contracts, weaker local accountability, contract teachers absorbed into civil-service norms) drained the voltage the moment the state took over.

The design lesson. This is voltage drop #3 — a non-representative situation — made visible because the study deliberately tested delivery by the eventual scaling agent. Duflo, Hanna and Ryan’s camera-monitoring of teacher attendance tells a parallel story: it worked crisply under NGO delivery with tight follow-through, and its record under routine government administration has been far more mixed (Duflo et al. 2012). If you want to know whether an intervention will scale, pilot it under the conditions of scale — the real implementer, not a proxy.

The remedy is not to abandon promising interventions but to pilot honestly: recruit a representative sample, hand delivery to the actual scaling agent, and price the marginal — not the average — unit. An intervention that keeps its voltage under those harsher conditions is one you can scale with confidence (List 2022).

Reflect. Take an intervention you believe should be scaled. Walk it through all five voltage drops using the diagnostic column. Which drop is it most exposed to — and could you redesign the next pilot so that this weakness would show up before, not after, national rollout?
