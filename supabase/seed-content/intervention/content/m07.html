Module 7: Institutions of the Poor — SHGs, Federations & Collective Models

Some of the most durable interventions in South Asia are not services delivered to the poor but institutions built by them. A self-help group of fifteen women who save a few rupees a week is, on paper, a tiny finance arrangement. Designed well and stacked into federations, it becomes the delivery rail for credit, livelihoods, entitlements and voice at the scale of tens of millions of households. This module treats the self-help group not as a microfinance product but as a designed institution — and asks what makes collective institutions of the poor endure rather than capture, corrode or collapse.

The SHG as an Institution, Not a Loan

The self-help group is a small, self-selected affinity group — usually 10–20 women — that meets regularly, pools savings, and lends internally before it ever touches a bank. The financial flows matter, but the design object is the group as an institution: a place where poor women accumulate a first asset (savings), practise rotating leadership, keep records, and learn to act collectively. Credit is what the institution does; it is not what the institution is.

Anatomy of the design
Savings first
The group begins by saving, not borrowing. Members build a shared corpus and a credit history before external money arrives — skin in the game that disciplines lending.
Joint liability & peer screening
Members know each other, so they screen and monitor at a cost no bank could bear. Social collateral substitutes for physical collateral.
SHG–bank linkage
Pioneered by NABARD from 1992, the group is linked to a commercial or cooperative bank that lends to the group as a unit. The group, not the bank, does the last-mile allocation and recovery (NABARD 1992).
Rules the group owns
Meeting norms, interest rates, sanctions for default — set and enforced by members, not imposed from outside. Ownership of the rules is what makes them stick.

This distinction is the whole design lesson of the module. A programme that treats the SHG as a channel for pushing loans will optimise disbursement and hollow out the institution. A programme that treats the SHG as an institution will invest in the slow work — savings, meetings, book-keeping, leadership rotation — and the credit follows as a symptom of institutional health.

From Group to Federation: Building at Population Scale

A single SHG is fragile: fifteen women cannot negotiate with a bank, run a grain bank, or hold a block official to account. The design answer is nesting — groups federate upward into larger bodies that do what no single group can. India's Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana — National Rural Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NRLM), launched 2011, made this the architecture of an anti-poverty programme at national scale.

1SHG

10–20 women. Savings, internal lending, mutual support. The primary institution.

2Village Organisation

A federation of 10–15 SHGs in a village. Manages larger funds, resolves disputes, links to schemes.

3Cluster / Block Federation

Federates village organisations across a cluster or block. Negotiates with banks and the state; runs enterprises.

Each circle is one self-help group of 10–15 women; groups nest into village organisations and then a cluster federation, so bargaining power with banks and the state grows tier by tier while member ownership stays at the base.
Federation is a design choice, not a growth accident

Federating is how a fragile local institution acquires bargaining power, professional staff and financial depth without losing member ownership. But each tier added is a tier that can be captured or can float free of its base. The design question at every level is the same: does this tier serve the groups below it, or does it start serving itself?

Ostrom's Design Principles, Mapped to SHG Federations

Why do some collective institutions of the poor endure for decades while others dissolve the moment the facilitating agency leaves? Elinor Ostrom's study of long-lived commons institutions identified design principles that separate durable collective action from the tragedy of the commons (Ostrom 1990). They map almost directly onto SHG-federation design.

Producer Collectives, FPOs and the Cooperative Line

The same logic — aggregate the poor so they can act at a scale the market respects — runs through producer institutions. A smallholder farmer selling alone is a price-taker at the mercy of the trader. A Farmer Producer Organisation (FPO) that aggregates the produce of thousands captures economies of scale in inputs, processing and marketing. The canonical case is dairy: Amul and the Anand pattern of village dairy cooperatives federated into district unions and a state marketing federation turned millions of one-buffalo households into the owners of a national brand.

Aggregation is the intervention. Amul did not make farmers better at keeping cattle; it changed their position in the value chain — from selling raw milk to a middleman to collectively owning the processing and the brand. FPOs promoted under NABARD and NRLM chase the same design: land titling and producer aggregation raise smallholder incomes precisely by shifting bargaining power, not by adding inputs (Deininger & Liu 2013).

Failure Modes: When Collective Institutions Corrode

Collective institutions of the poor fail in patterned ways, and every failure is the flip side of a design principle ignored.

Elite capture. The better-off or dominant-caste members steer credit and posts to themselves. Boundaries and collective-choice rules exist on paper but not in practice.

Free-riding. Members enjoy the group's benefits without contributing to its upkeep. Absent monitoring and graduated sanctions, the diligent subsidise the idle until they quit.

Weak governance. Frozen leadership, unaudited books, ghost meetings — the institutional form without the function, an SHG that exists only for the loan.

Dependence on the promoter. The group runs only while the NGO or mission staffer is present and dissolves when they leave — community-driven development as a participatory fig leaf, not a self-sustaining institution.

Case — JEEViKA and Kudumbashree federation design

Bihar's JEEViKA (the state rural livelihoods mission, later folded into NRLM) and Kerala's Kudumbashree are the two most studied SHG-federation systems in India, and both illustrate the design principles above at work.

Kudumbashree (Kerala, 1998). A three-tier structure — neighbourhood groups, area development societies, community development societies — deliberately fused with local government. The federation is not just a finance body; it holds a formal relationship with the panchayat, so the institution of the poor sits inside the institution of the state. Boundaries are sharp (poverty-targeted membership), leadership rotates, and disputes are resolved locally — Ostrom's nested-enterprise and conflict-resolution principles built into the architecture.

JEEViKA (Bihar, 2007). Randomised evidence found that JEEViKA's SHGs raised savings and formal-sector borrowing and cut reliance on high-cost moneylenders — but the durable gains came where village organisations and cluster federations gave the groups real bargaining power, not where SHGs existed in name only. The Andhra Pradesh SERP model that preceded it showed the same: the federation, not the individual group, is what holds the institution up after the facilitating agency withdraws.

The design lesson: the SHG delivers when it is built as a nested, member-owned institution and captured or hollow when it is treated as a disbursement channel. Same tool, opposite outcomes — decided by design, before a rupee moved.

The honest test of community-driven development. Ask not "is there a group?" but "would this institution survive the promoter leaving tomorrow?" If the savings, the meetings, the book-keeping and the rule-enforcement are member-owned, it is an institution. If they run only on the facilitator's presence, it is scaffolding — and scaffolding is not a building.

Reflect. Take one SHG or producer collective you know. Score it against Ostrom's seven design principles in the table — which are genuinely present, and which exist only on paper? Then ask the promoter-dependence question: would it survive if the NGO or mission staff withdrew this month? What single design change would most raise its odds of enduring?
