Module 13: Designing for Adaptation & Learning

Most of this course has treated design as something you finish before implementation begins — diagnose, theorise, choose a model, then deliver. But some interventions face problems that cannot be fully specified in advance, in contexts that vary faster than any blueprint can anticipate. For these, the design decision is not what to build but how to keep learning — how to build an intervention that generates the evidence to improve itself while it runs. This module is about designing the learning loop into the intervention, not bolting evaluation on at the end.

Blueprint vs Process Design

David Korten’s 1980 study of rural development in Asia drew a distinction that has organised the field ever since. A blueprint approach fixes the design up front, writes it into a manual, and rolls it out uniformly — the plan is the master, and deviation is failure. A learning process approach treats the early intervention as a question, not an answer: it starts small, fits the programme to the local reality, and lets the design emerge from what the fit teaches. Korten’s insight was that the successful programmes he studied — unlike the blueprints that failed — had all been through a learning process first (Korten 1980).

Korten’s three stages of the learning process
Learning to be effective
The programme is small and often fails, but errors are embraced as information. The question is whether the mechanism fires at all in this context.
Learning to be efficient
Once it works, strip out what is not essential. Reduce the cost and the staff intensity so the model can survive beyond a hand-picked team.
Learning to expand
Only now does the organisation build the systems and capacities to scale — because it finally knows what it is scaling.

The choice between the two is not ideological — it is a match to the problem. When the problem is well understood and the context is uniform, a blueprint is cheaper, faster, and fairer. When the problem is complex and the context varies, a blueprint locks in the wrong answer at scale.

Blueprint design

Fits well-understood problems in uniform contexts — a vaccination cold chain, a cash transfer with fixed eligibility.

Standardises: one manual, one protocol, executed the same everywhere.

Optimises for fidelity, cost control, and equity of treatment.

Fails when the context varies or the mechanism is not yet known — it scales the wrong design confidently.

Process design

Fits complex problems in variable contexts — governance reform, behaviour change, frontline service quality.

Standardises the learning method, not the activity; the local design is allowed to differ.

Optimises for fit, discovery, and speed of correction.

Fails when abused as an excuse for no plan, no measurement, and no accountability.

Adaptive Management and PDIA

The Problem-Driven Iterative Adaptation approach gives the learning process a discipline (Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock 2017). Rather than importing a best-practice solution and forcing local reality to comply — the isomorphic mimicry of Module 1 — PDIA starts from a locally-felt problem and builds the solution through many small, monitored iterations. Each step is a cheap experiment: try something, watch closely, keep what works, drop what does not, and let the next step be shaped by the last. The authors call this crawling the design space — you do not know the route in advance, so you feel your way toward it, one short, reversible move at a time.

Positive deviance is the adaptive designer’s favourite raw material. In almost any programme, a few sites, workers, or households already get better results than the rest with the same resources. Instead of importing an outside model, find these deviants, learn what they do differently, and design the next iteration around their practice. It is discovery from inside the system rather than prescription from outside it.

Iterations must be small, fast, and monitored

Adaptation is not the freedom to change anything at any time. Its power comes from a tight loop: a change small enough that its effect can be attributed, fast enough that you learn before the window closes, and monitored closely enough that you actually see the effect. An unmonitored change is not adaptation — it is drift. The learning loop, not the flexibility, is the design.

Developmental Evaluation and Rapid-Cycle Testing

The evaluation traditions of the MEL and Causal Inference flagships answer a summative question — did it work? — and answer it rigorously, at the end. Michael Quinn Patton’s developmental evaluation answers a different question in real time: what should we change next? (Patton 2011). The evaluator sits inside the design team, feeds back observations as the intervention unfolds, and helps the programme adapt while it is still adapting. It is not a softer evaluation — it is evaluation repurposed from verdict to steering wheel.

QuestionSummative evaluationDevelopmental evaluation
WhenAt the end (or midline and endline)Continuously, in fast cycles

The practical form this takes is rapid-cycle testing: pose a design question, run a small test, read a fast indicator, decide, repeat — often in weeks rather than the years a summative trial needs. The two are complements, not rivals. Developmental evaluation shapes the intervention until it stabilises; summative evaluation then tests the stabilised model for the rigorous attribution that scaling decisions require.

Building the Learning Loop Into the Design

An adaptive intervention is one whose own operation produces the data that improves it. This is a design property, not a reporting afterthought: the indicators, the cadence, and the decision rights all have to be built in from the start. The test of a learning loop is simple — every piece of data collected must be attached to a decision someone can actually make.

Learning-loop componentDesign decision it forces
Decision-focused indicatorsWhich few measures would actually change what a frontline worker or manager does next week? Drop indicators no decision depends on.
Feedback cadenceHow fast must data return to be useful — daily, weekly, monthly? The cadence must beat the pace of the decision it feeds.
Who acts on whatFor each signal, name the actor with the authority and the incentive to respond. A loop with no one at the acting end is just monitoring.
Safe-to-fail spaceWhere is deviation permitted and reversible, and where must fidelity hold? Adaptation needs a bounded arena, not the whole programme.
Case — an adaptive, iterated programme in South Asia

The problem: children in early grades across a state were enrolled but not learning to read, and past blueprint fixes — more textbooks, more training days — had left the gap untouched.

The process design. Rather than roll out a fixed manual, Pratham’s Teaching at the Right Level work moved through Korten’s stages: early years learning whether grouping children by level fired at all, then stripping the model down until ordinary government teachers could run it, and only then building the systems to expand. Each state adaptation was a fresh iteration, not a copy.

The learning loop. A one-page assessment that a teacher administers in minutes doubles as the intervention’s core indicator: it tells the teacher which group each child belongs in today (the frontline decision) and, aggregated, tells managers whether the mechanism is firing (the steering decision). The same act of measuring both delivers the intervention and generates the data that improves it — a loop built into the design, not reported upward after the fact.

The hand-off. Once the model stabilised, summative trials tested it for rigorous attribution — the question the MEL flagship equips you to answer. Developmental evaluation shaped the design; summative evaluation validated it for scale.

Data Systems for Delivery, Not Just Reporting

Modules 6 and 9 warned that a management information system built only to fill the funder’s dashboard becomes a tax on frontline time that returns nothing to the people paying it. The adaptive lens sharpens the point: an MIS is part of the intervention’s design, and it should be built for the frontline decision first and the upward report second. If the ASHA, the teacher, or the block officer cannot see something in the data that changes what they do this week, the system is extracting information rather than creating it.

The failure signature is familiar across South Asian programmes: dozens of registers and app screens flow upward to a district dashboard, while the worker who collected every number gets nothing back that helps them serve the next household. Data that only travels up is a cost centre. Data that also travels back down — fast, legible, and tied to a decision the worker owns — is what turns an MIS into a learning loop.

Hand-off to the MEL flagship

This module designs the learning system inside an intervention — which signals, how fast, to whom. It deliberately stops short of measurement rigour. For sampling, indicator validity, attribution, and the machinery of summative and impact evaluation, the ImpactMojo MEL and Causal Inference flagships are the companion courses. Adaptive design decides what to learn and when to act; those courses decide whether you can believe the number.

Reflect. Take one intervention you know. Is it better served by a blueprint or a learning-process design — and is it actually being run that way? Now trace one learning loop inside it: name a single decision-focused indicator, its feedback cadence, and the specific person who is supposed to act on it. If any of the three is missing, the loop is broken — which one, and what would it take to close it?
