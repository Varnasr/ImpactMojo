Module 8: Getting the Level Right — Service-Delivery Redesign

Some of the most powerful interventions in development add nothing new. They spend no more money, hire no new cadre, build no new building. Instead they redesign the mechanism of an existing service — how a classroom is grouped, how safe water reaches a jerrycan, how a default is set — so that the same inputs produce a far larger change in outcome. This module is about that family: service-delivery redesign, where the leverage lies not in more, but in a better-shaped delivery platform.

Redesign the Mechanism, Not the Inputs

Module 1 drew the line between adding inputs and redesigning the mechanism. This module lives on the second side of that line. The canonical case is Teaching at the Right Level (TaRL), developed by Pratham and evaluated with J-PAL across India. The problem it attacks is the "schooling is not learning" gap: children sit in class for years, sorted by age and grade, while instruction sails over the heads of those who cannot yet read a paragraph or subtract two-digit numbers.

What TaRL actually changes
The old mechanism
Group children by age and grade; teach the prescribed curriculum for that grade to all of them at once, regardless of what each can do.
The redesigned mechanism
Assess each child, regroup by current learning level, and run a focused daily block of activities pitched exactly at that level — then move children up as they master each stage.
What did not change
The same teachers, roughly the same hours, the same building and budget. No new inputs were added; the delivery platform was re-shaped.

Across randomised evaluations the reading and arithmetic gains were large and replicated in multiple forms — from Pratham volunteers to in-school "learning camps" to government-teacher delivery (Banerjee et al. 2007, 2017). The intervention worked because it removed a binding constraint — mismatch between instruction and level — that no volume of extra textbooks could touch.

Add inputs

More teachers, textbooks, classrooms, tablets. Cost rises linearly. Works only if the missing input was the binding constraint — often it is not.

Redesign mechanism

Same inputs, re-routed. Regroup by level, reorder the queue, reset the default. Cost is near-flat; the gain comes from removing the true constraint.

The "Cheap, Robust, Scalable" Archetype

A second family of redesigns wins not by cleverness of targeting but by being almost free per unit of outcome, working across many contexts, and being trivial to deliver. Two examples anchor the literature.

Mass deworming. Treating whole schools for intestinal worms — rather than screening and treating individuals — cut absenteeism and raised school participation at a cost of pennies per child, with spillover benefits to untreated children nearby (Miguel & Kremer 2004). The redesign was the delivery rule: treat everyone through the school platform, skip the expensive diagnosis.

Chlorine dispensers. Placing a free chlorine dispenser at the water source — where people already collect water — sharply raised safe-water use versus selling bottled chlorine, by removing price, travel, and memory frictions all at once (Kremer et al.). Again, the intervention was a redesign of where and how the service met people, not a new product.

What deworming, dispensers, and TaRL share is that they push cost per unit of outcome toward zero by re-shaping delivery rather than buying more. This is why they scale: a mechanism that is cheap, robust to context, and simple to administer survives the journey from pilot to system that kills more elaborate designs (Module 11).

Nudges and Choice Architecture — Real Reach, Real Limits

The lightest redesigns change nothing about the service except the choice environment around it: the default option, a timely reminder, a small friction removed or added. Set immunisation or savings as the default; send an SMS reminder before a clinic visit; place the healthy or safe option first. These cost almost nothing and can move behaviour at genuine population scale.

Know the ceiling. Nudges shift behaviour at the margin — effect sizes are usually modest, and they cannot fix a structural or supply-side failure. A reminder to visit a clinic is worthless if the clinic has no nurse or no drugs; a savings default cannot conjure income that is not there. Choice architecture works when the barrier is attention, friction, or inertia — and fails when the barrier is a missing service. Diagnose the constraint (Module 2) before reaching for a nudge.

Platforms vs Point Solutions

The deepest lesson of this module is the difference between bolting a fix onto a service and redesigning the platform through which the service is delivered. A point solution — one more app, one more training, one more kit — leaves the delivery mechanism untouched and usually disappoints. A platform redesign changes the mechanism itself: the classroom becomes level-grouped, the clinic queue is reordered, the water point becomes the treatment point.

Shape is not the only thing that must be right; so is the level at which the mechanism is set and steered. Subsidiarity — the principle that a problem should be handled at the lowest level that can actually see it and act on it — is what stops a good redesign from being pushed to the wrong rung of the system. Set the decision too high and a single central rule cannot see local variation; set it too low and the unit has neither the reach nor the resources to solve the problem. Getting the level right means matching each problem to the rung that holds both the information and the authority to fix it.

Case — TaRL scaling in an Indian state (Haryana / Mission Buniyaad)

The proof-of-concept was clear from Pratham's evaluations; the open question was whether the redesigned mechanism could survive inside a government school system, delivered by regular teachers rather than trusted volunteers.

The redesign at scale. In state partnerships — Haryana, and later the Delhi-region Mission Buniyaad programme — government teachers were trained to assess children, regroup them by learning level, and run a dedicated daily remedial block, with Pratham providing mentoring and a simple assessment rhythm. The platform, not the workforce, was re-engineered.

What made or broke it. Where the daily level-grouped block genuinely happened, learning rose. Where it was diluted back into ordinary grade-based teaching — because the timetable, the official curriculum pressure, or supervision did not protect the block — the gains faded. The mechanism was cheap and robust only when the delivery platform actually adopted it; a nominal rollout that kept the old grouping was isomorphic mimicry (Module 1).

The lesson for scaling: a mechanism redesign is not self-executing. It scales only if the receiving system re-shapes its routine — the timetable, the grouping, the assessment — around the new mechanism, not just around its name.

Reflect. Take a service you know — a school, a clinic, a ration shop. Is the improvement you would propose a point solution that adds an input, or a platform redesign that re-routes the same inputs? What is the binding constraint, and would a nudge, a delivery-rule change, or a full regrouping actually remove it?

"The question is rarely whether to do more, but whether to do the same thing in a different shape — and whether the system will keep that shape once you leave."— after Banerjee, Duflo & the TaRL evidence
