Module 14: Design Studio — Your Intervention, End to End

Every module so far handed you one instrument — a diagnosis, a theory of change, a model, a delivery map, a scale test. This capstone is where you assemble them. Pick one problem you care about — a real one, in a district or community you know — and carry it through the whole pipeline until you can hand a critic a design memo that survives their hardest questions. The test of learning here is not recall; it is whether you can design an intervention that a hostile reviewer, a sceptical funder, and the people it is meant to serve would each recognise as honest.

The End-to-End Design Walk

An intervention is designed in a sequence, and each step constrains the next. Work them in order — the discipline is refusing to name a model before you have diagnosed the problem, and refusing to talk scale before you know the mechanism fires. Take your one problem down this ladder.

1
Diagnose the problem

What kind of problem is this — information, incentive, capacity, belief, or coordination? Name the binding constraint, not the symptom. (Module 2)

2
Write a falsifiable theory of change

State the mechanism in one sentence and list the assumptions it rides on. Mark the one assumption that, if wrong, sinks the whole design. (Module 3)

3
Choose and adapt a model

Cash, a bundle, a frontline cadre, a collective, a service redesign — select the model whose logic matches your mechanism, then adapt it to local reality rather than copying its form. (Modules 4–8)

4
Map the delivery chain

Trace every link from funder to last mile. Find the weakest link before the field does. (Module 9)

5
Check incentives, targeting, cost-effectiveness

Are the frontline incentives compatible with the goal? Does the benefit reach the intended group? Is the cost per outcome defensible against the next-best use of the money? (Module 10)

6
Plan the scale pathway and voltage checks

Name who scales it — state, market, or NGO federation — and where you expect voltage to drop as motivated pilot staff give way to an ordinary system. (Modules 11–12)

7
Build in learning loops

Decide in advance what evidence would make you change course, and how it reaches you fast enough to act. A design with no learning loop cannot be corrected. (Module 13)

Design is a chain of commitments, not a menu

You cannot skip a rung and patch it later cheaply. A weak diagnosis is not rescued by a clever model; a broken delivery chain is not rescued by a bigger budget; a missing learning loop is not rescued at scale. Each step you finish honestly makes the next one narrower and safer. The memo at the end of this module is simply this ladder, written down so someone else can attack it.

The Design Critique Rubric

Before you defend a design, attack it. Each failure mode this course named has a single diagnostic question a good critic asks first. Run your own intervention through every row — if you cannot answer a question with evidence, that is where your design is thin.

Failure modeThe critic’s diagnostic question
Solution in search of a problemWhat is the binding constraint, and would this design survive if the problem turned out to be a different one?
Isomorphic mimicryDoes this reproduce the function of the model you copied, or only its visible form — the committee, the plan, the app?
Broken delivery chainWhich single link is weakest, and what happens to the outcome if it leaks by 30 percent?
Pilot-to-scale collapseWhat in your pilot depended on hand-picked staff or motivated volunteers that an ordinary system will not have?
Elite captureWho is powerful enough near the point of delivery to divert the benefit, and what stops them?
Incentive incompatibilityWhat is the frontline worker actually rewarded and punished for — and does that pull toward or against the goal?
No learning loopWhat evidence would tell you this is failing, how fast would it reach you, and are you committed to act on it?

The Ethics of Intervention

An intervention is an exercise of power over other people’s lives, usually people with less power than the designer. Competence at design does not confer the right to intervene; that right is earned through restraint, consent, and humility about whose problem is being solved.

Six obligations you carry into the field
Do no harm
Ask what the intervention could break — local markets, social trust, existing coping strategies — not only what it might build.
Dignity and agency
Treat participants as authors of their own lives, not as units to be moved. A design that works by removing choice has usually solved the wrong problem.
Informed consent
People should understand what is being done, by whom, and why — in their language, before they are enrolled, not buried in a form.
The right to exit
Participation must be refusable without penalty. An intervention no one can leave is a form of coercion, however good its intentions.
Guard against paternalism
“We know what is good for them” is the sentence that precedes most avoidable harm. Design with people, not merely for them.
The politics of helping
Whose problem is being solved — the community’s, the funder’s, or the designer’s? Who decided it was a problem, and who was in the room?

Power and humility. The people you design for have survived without you and will remain after you leave. They understand their own constraints better than any diagnostic can. If your design assumes they are ignorant, lazy, or irrational, the fault is almost always in the design, not the people. Across South Asia the interventions that endured — SHG federations, the ASHA cadre, community-managed water — succeeded because they routed decisions through the community rather than around it. Ask before every rollout: would the intended participants, if they had full power to choose, choose this?

The Deliverable — A One-Intervention Design Memo

Your capstone output is a single memo a practitioner could hand to a funder or a government partner. Nine fields, each answerable in a few sentences. If a field is empty, that step of the design is not finished. Fill it for your one problem.

#FieldThe one thing it must state
1ProblemThe binding constraint, for whom, backed by one piece of evidence — not the symptom.
2Theory of changeThe mechanism in one sentence, and the assumption that would sink it if wrong.
3ModelWhich model you chose and the local adaptation that keeps its function, not just its form.
4TargetingWho is in, who is out, and how you keep the benefit from the already-powerful.
5Delivery chainThe links from funder to last mile, and the weakest one named honestly.
6Cost-effectivenessCost per outcome, and why it beats the next-best use of the same money.
7Scale pathwayWho scales it and where you expect voltage to drop.
8Learning planThe evidence that would make you change course, and how fast it reaches you.
9Biggest riskThe single thing most likely to make this fail — stated plainly, not buried.

Field 9 is the one that matters most. A memo that names its biggest risk plainly is more trustworthy than one that hides it. A designer who cannot state how their intervention might fail has not finished thinking — and a funder who never hears the risk cannot help you manage it. Write the risk you are most tempted to leave out.

Studio task. Take the one problem you chose at the start. Walk it down all seven steps, run it through every row of the critique rubric, and write the nine-field memo. Then give the memo to someone who disagrees with you and ask them to find the row where your evidence is thinnest. Rewrite that row. That is the whole discipline of this course in one afternoon.

“The people who design an intervention are rarely the people who must live inside it. Humility is not a virtue added at the end — it is a design constraint from the first line.”— adapted from the practice of community-led development in South Asia
