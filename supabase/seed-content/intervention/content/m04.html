Module 4: Cash — Transfers, Targeting & Delivery Rails

Cash is the most-studied intervention in development, and the most misunderstood. Give a poor household money and you have made a design choice with a dozen downstream decisions folded into it: attach conditions or not, target the few or reach the many, and — the part designers forget — build the plumbing that actually moves rupees to a bank account in a village. This module treats cash not as charity but as engineering: the transfer, its targeting, and the delivery rails that decide whether the money arrives at all.

Conditional Cash Transfers — The Design That Launched a Field

Modern cash design begins in Mexico. In 1997, Progresa (later Oportunidades, then Prospera) paid poor mothers a stipend on condition that their children attended school and turned up for health check-ups. The novelty was not the money; it was that the programme was built with a randomised evaluation inside it, so the world could finally see whether cash moved outcomes. It did — enrolment, nutrition, and health use all rose — and the design was copied across Latin America and beyond (Fiszbein & Schady 2009).

What conditionality buys, and what it costs
Buys
Political legitimacy (voters accept transfers tied to “responsible” behaviour), and a nudge on outcomes the state cares about — schooling, immunisation, antenatal visits — that pure income might not reach.
Costs
An expensive monitoring apparatus to verify compliance, and exclusion of the very poorest when they cannot meet the condition — no nearby school, no clinic, a disabled child. The condition can punish the supply-side gap it did not cause.

India runs its own conditional cash line. Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY) pays a woman a cash incentive to deliver her baby in a health facility rather than at home — conditional cash aimed squarely at reducing maternal and newborn mortality. It drove a large shift toward institutional delivery, and also exposed the classic limit: paying women to reach clinics does little where the clinic is empty of staff or supplies.

Unconditional Cash — “Just Give Money”

The opposite design strips the conditions away. Unconditional cash transfers (UCTs) trust the household to know its own binding constraint. The strongest evidence comes from GiveDirectly, which sends large lump sums to poor households in East Africa and measures the result: recipients raised assets, consumption, and psychological well-being, with no rise in spending on alcohol or tobacco — the fear that reliably haunts the debate and reliably fails to appear (Haushofer & Shapiro 2016).

Cash is the benchmark, not the default

Because cash is cheap to deliver and hard to misspend on the recipient’s behalf, it has become the yardstick every in-kind programme must now beat. The honest design question is no longer “does cash work?” but “does your training, kit, or service beat simply handing people the money it costs?” Often the in-kind programme cannot — and that comparison is itself a design discipline.

India’s Rails — DBT and the JAM Trinity

India’s contribution to cash design is not the transfer but the rail. Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) moves money straight into a beneficiary’s bank account, bypassing the chain of intermediaries that historically skimmed it. It rides on the JAM trinity: Jan Dhan (near-universal no-frills bank accounts), Aadhaar (a unique biometric ID that de-duplicates beneficiary lists), and Mobile (the channel and the confirmation). Plumbing, not policy, is the innovation.

Delivery rails are design, not logistics. PM-KISAN deposits an income-support instalment directly to farmers’ accounts three times a year; PAHAL (DBT-LPG) pays the cooking-gas subsidy into accounts instead of discounting the cylinder at the point of sale. The same policy — “subsidise cooking fuel” — delivered on a different rail produces wildly different leakage. The rail is where the intervention succeeds or leaks.

Case — PAHAL / DBT-LPG: how a rail cut leakage

Before reform, cooking-gas subsidy was delivered by selling the cylinder cheap. That in-kind discount invited two leaks: ghost connections (subsidised cylinders booked against people who did not exist) and diversion (subsidised domestic cylinders resold into the commercial market at a profit).

The redesign. Under PAHAL, the consumer pays the full market price for the cylinder and the subsidy is transferred separately into an Aadhaar-linked bank account. A ghost cannot hold a bank account and a diverted cylinder earns no arbitrage, because everyone now pays the same market price. The rail did the work: de-duplication removed non-existent beneficiaries and the direct deposit removed the resale margin.

The caution. The same biometric rail that removes ghosts can also remove real people — a failed fingerprint match, an un-seeded account, a migrant far from her home branch. A rail that plugs leakage by tightening the gate must be watched for exclusion error: the genuine poor wrongly shut out. Efficiency and inclusion pull against each other, and the designer owns both.

The Delivery Pipeline — Where Value Leaks

Every rupee budgeted passes through a narrowing pipe before it becomes value in a recipient’s hand. At each joint the pipe loses a little: administrative overhead, leakage to ghosts and middlemen, and — the loss designers most often ignore — the recipient’s own cost of reaching the money: the bus fare to a distant branch, the wage forgone queuing, the biometric that will not match. The transfer on paper is not the transfer received.

A cash pipeline narrows at every stage — the rail’s job is to keep the funnel wide, and the last drop lost to the recipient’s own travel, time, and failed matches is the one measured least.

The value actually received is the gross transfer minus what the pipe swallows on the way — both the system’s leakage and the recipient’s private cost of collection:

\[ V_{\text{received}} \;=\; T_{\text{gross}} \;-\; L_{\text{leakage}} \;-\; C_{\text{access}} \]
\(V_{\text{received}}\) is the value the household truly gains; \(T_{\text{gross}}\) the transfer promised on paper; \(L_{\text{leakage}}\) the value siphoned by admin cost, ghosts, and diversion before it arrives; and \(C_{\text{access}}\) the recipient’s own cost of collection — travel, forgone wages, and time lost to failed matches or un-seeded accounts. A good rail shrinks both \(L\) and \(C\); a rail that cuts \(L\) by tightening the gate can quietly raise \(C\).

Targeting — Who Gets the Money

Every transfer needs a rule for who is in. There are four families of rule, and the choice is a trade-off among accuracy, administrative cost, stigma, and political durability — not a search for a single best method.

The two errors trade off, and they are not symmetric. Tighter targeting cuts inclusion error (leakage to the non-poor) but raises exclusion error (the genuine poor wrongly left out) — and for the poorest, being wrongly excluded is far more damaging than a rich neighbour being wrongly included. A design that optimises only for “savings” is quietly choosing the more harmful error.

When Cash Works — and When It Cannot

Cash relaxes the constraint of money. It does nothing for constraints that are not about money. This is the single most important boundary condition in the module.

Cash is not a cure for a supply-side gap. Paying a family to send its daughter to school does nothing if there is no functioning school, no teacher, no clinic within reach. Conditional cash for institutional delivery cannot manufacture a staffed labour room. Diagnose the binding constraint first (Module 2): if it is demand or liquidity, cash is powerful; if it is supply, cash can even do harm by loading demand onto a system that cannot meet it.

Reflect. Take a cash or subsidy programme you know. State its three design choices out loud: conditional or unconditional, which targeting method, and which delivery rail. Now ask — is the constraint it addresses really about money, or is there a supply-side gap that no amount of cash will close? Which of the two targeting errors is it optimising against, and is that the error that does the most harm here?

“The poor are experts on their own lives; the design question is whether the plumbing lets the money reach them, and whether money was ever the missing thing.”— after Fiszbein & Schady, and Banerjee & Duflo
