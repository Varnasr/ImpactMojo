Module 6: Human Infrastructure — Frontline Workers & the Last Mile

Between a well-designed intervention and the household it is meant to reach stands a person: a woman who walks to a hamlet with a register and a bag of supplies, who is neither a bureaucrat nor a volunteer but something engineered in between. The community health worker, the anganwadi worker, the shasthya shebika — these are not incidental helpers. They are designed human infrastructure, and the choices a designer makes about how to select, pay, supervise, and equip them decide whether the last mile is crossed or merely mapped.

The Frontline Cadre as a Design Object

When the mechanism of an intervention is a person doing a task in a place the system cannot easily see, the cadre itself becomes the design. South Asia has built three of the largest such cadres on earth, each a distinct answer to the same question: how do you put a trusted, trained agent within walking distance of every poor household?

Three cadres, one design problem
ASHA (India, NRHM 2005–)
The Accredited Social Health Activist — a local woman selected by her own village, paid mostly by task-based incentives, bridging households to the public health system. Roughly one million now serve across India.
Anganwadi worker (India, ICDS)
Runs the village child-care centre under the Integrated Child Development Services — nutrition, pre-school, growth monitoring — on a fixed monthly honorarium rather than piece rates.
Shasthya shebika (Bangladesh, BRAC)
BRAC’s community health volunteer, who earns a margin by selling essential health commodities — a market incentive layered onto a social mission.

Note that all three are women, local, and part-time. That is not sentiment; it is design. Local selection buys trust and lowers attrition; part-time work keeps the wage bill payable at population scale; a woman can enter homes and speak to mothers where a male official cannot. Every one of these design choices also creates a constraint the rest of the intervention must live with.

Paying the Frontline: Three Incentive Models

How you pay a frontline agent silently rewrites what they do. Economists call this the multitasking problem (Holmstrom & Milgrom): when a worker has many duties but you can only measure and reward some, effort flows to the measured tasks and drains from the rest. Each pay model buys something and distorts something.

Piece-rate / performance pay

Buys: effort on countable outputs — institutional deliveries, immunisations. Distorts: effort skews to paid tasks; unpaid duties (counselling, follow-up) are neglected; incentive to over-report or cream easy cases. ASHA’s task-based schedule is the classic instance.

Fixed salary / honorarium

Buys: predictability, willingness to do unmeasured relational work. Distorts: weak link between effort and reward invites absenteeism and shirking unless supervision is real. The anganwadi honorarium sits here.

Mission / social motivation

Buys: intrinsic commitment, self-selection of the mission-minded. Distorts: unreliable and unscalable alone; crowded out if crude cash incentives are bolted on; burns out when the workload rises without recognition.

There is no clean pay model — only a chosen distortion

Every incentive scheme rewards what it can count and quietly taxes what it cannot. The designer’s job is not to find the undistorting scheme — there is none — but to make the measured tasks the ones that actually matter, and to protect the unmeasured relational work with supervision and mission, not cash alone.

The Delivery Chain: A Cadre Is Only as Strong as Its Weakest Link

J-PAL’s delivery-chain lens treats the cadre not as a hiring decision but as a sequence of links, each of which must hold: selection → training → supervision → supply → retention. A brilliant incentive is worthless if the drug stock is empty; excellent training decays without supervision; motivated recruits leave if the promised honorarium arrives four months late.

LinkDesign questionFailure when it breaks
SelectionWho is chosen, and by whom?Elite capture of the post; workers distrusted by the very households they serve.
TrainingOnce, or continuous? Skills or scripts?Knowledge decay; workers who can recite but cannot counsel.
SupervisionWho checks the work, how often, with what data?Absenteeism and ghost activity; no correction loop.
Supply chainDo the commodities arrive on time?The worker shows up; the vaccine, the ORS, the register does not.
RetentionIs the role worth staying in?Attrition erases the training investment and the household trust with it.

Because the links multiply rather than add, coverage collapses fast. If selection, training, supervision, supply, and retention each work 80% of the time, end-to-end delivery is 0.8⁵ ≈ 33%. Strengthening the strongest link changes nothing; only the weakest link moves the outcome. Diagnose the chain before you redesign the incentive.

Absenteeism and the Limits of Monitoring

The most-cited frontline failure is simply not being there. In the landmark six-country study “Missing in Action” (Chaudhury et al. 2006), on any given day roughly a quarter of teachers and health workers were absent from the facilities they were paid to staff — and absence was highest where supervision and consequences were weakest.

Monitoring can move the number — camera-and-pay experiments in Rajasthan cut teacher absence sharply (Banerjee et al. 2008) — but only where the measured presence is tied to real consequences and the task you monitor is the task that matters. Monitoring fixes attendance; it does not by itself buy effort, and a worker present but disengaged can satisfy the camera while failing the household.

Monitoring is a substitute for weak intrinsic motivation, not a complement to it. Layer heavy surveillance onto a mission-driven cadre and you can crowd out the very commitment that made them cheap to motivate. Match the accountability instrument to why the worker is failing — absence, ignorance, or empty shelves are three different problems.

Digital Tools at the Frontline: Help or Just Burden?

The reflex fix for a struggling cadre is now an app — a dashboard for the supervisor, a data-entry form for the worker. Sometimes it closes a real gap; often it adds a reporting burden that steals time from the household without improving the outcome.

When it helps: it removes a real friction the worker feels — a job aid that guides counselling, an automatic reminder for the next home visit, a stock alert that keeps the supply link full.

When it burdens: it serves the dashboard, not the worker — duplicate registers, poor connectivity, data entered for someone else’s report while the mother waits or is skipped.

The test: does the tool reduce the worker’s effort per useful task, or shift effort toward being measured? Design for the frontline user first, the analyst second.

Case — designing ASHA incentives

The problem. An ASHA earns a fixed incentive per institutional delivery she accompanies but little or nothing for the slow relational work — antenatal counselling, newborn home visits, tracking a child through the full immunisation schedule. Predictably, effort concentrates on the paid, countable task.

The multitasking trap. Deliveries rise on the dashboard; newborn-care visits and immunisation follow-up lag, because they are unpaid and unmeasured. The scheme has bought the task it can count and taxed the tasks that quietly save lives.

Redesign, chain-first. Before touching the incentive, check the links: are payments arriving on time (retention), is there a supervisor reviewing her register (supervision), is the immunisation stock in the sub-centre (supply)? Then rebalance incentives toward a small number of high-value outcomes — a completed immunisation series, a tracked newborn — not just contacts, while protecting counselling with supervision and peer recognition rather than a fee per sentence spoken.

The design lesson. You cannot pay for everything, so decide deliberately what the measured tasks will be — and make them the ones that matter most. The incentive is a steering wheel, not an accelerator.

Reflect. Take one frontline cadre you know. Which pay model does it use, and which unmeasured task is being quietly starved as a result? Now walk its delivery chain — selection, training, supervision, supply, retention — and name the weakest link. Would an app strengthen that link, or just add a report on top of it?
