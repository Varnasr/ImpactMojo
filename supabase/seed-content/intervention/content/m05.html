Module 5: Bundles & Sequencing — The Graduation Approach

Most anti-poverty interventions hand the poor a single instrument — a loan, a grant, a training, a cash transfer — and hope it is the one thing that was missing. The graduation approach begins from the opposite premise: the poorest are held by several constraints at once, and relieving any one alone leaves the others to pull them back. Its answer is a sequenced bundle of complementary supports, delivered in a deliberate order over about two years, designed to push a household past a poverty trap and leave it standing on its own. Pioneered by BRAC in Bangladesh, it is the clearest case in this course of a design where the combination, and the timing, are the intervention.

The Bundle, and Why It Is Sequenced

BRAC’s Targeting the Ultra-Poor programme (launched 2002) assembles five components that are useless in isolation but powerful together. Crucially, they are not dumped on a household at once — they are layered in sequence, each preparing the ground for the next, over roughly 24 months (Bandiera et al. 2017).

Five layers, delivered in order
1. Productive asset transfer
A one-time grant of a livelihood asset — typically livestock (a cow, goats, poultry). Not a loan: an outright gift that seeds an income stream the household could never have saved toward.
2. Consumption support
A small, time-bound stipend for the first months, so the family need not eat or sell the asset to survive before it starts to pay. This buys the asset time to mature.
3. Skills training
Practical instruction in tending the asset — animal care, husbandry, basic enterprise — so the transfer becomes a managed business, not a windfall.
4. Coaching & handholding
Regular home visits by a field officer over the full period: problem-solving, encouragement, and the social recognition that the household is worth investing in.
5. Savings
Enrolment in a savings discipline, so that surplus is banked rather than dissipated — the mechanism by which the gains become permanent after support ends.
Order is a design variable, not an accident

Consumption support must arrive with the asset, or the family eats the cow. Training must land before the asset can be mismanaged. Coaching runs throughout; savings is emphasised as income begins. Reshuffle the sequence and the same five components fail. The graduation model treats timing as part of the design, not the delivery.

Complementarity: The Bundle Beats the Sum of Its Parts

The single-instrument habit asks “which one input works best?” The graduation logic answers that the question is wrong: the inputs are complements, each raising the return to the others. An asset without training is squandered; training without an asset is theory; an asset without consumption support is sold under duress. The point is not to find the strongest ingredient but to remove several binding constraints at once so that none can drag the household back.

Single-instrument thinking

Cash-only, or training-only, or credit-only. Assumes one missing input. Cheap and simple, but leaves the other constraints intact — gains often fade once the transfer stops.

Sequenced bundle

Asset + consumption + skills + coaching + savings, layered over ~24 months. Costlier and heavier to run, but relieves several constraints together — gains persist after support ends.

Complementarity cuts both ways. A bundle is only as strong as its weakest layer: drop the coaching to save money and the asset is mismanaged; drop the consumption support and it is sold. Cost-cutting a bundled intervention component by component is how a graduation programme quietly turns back into an asset giveaway.

The Evidence: A Big Push That Held

Graduation is one of the most rigorously tested interventions in development. Six simultaneous randomised trials — Ethiopia, Ghana, Honduras, India, Pakistan, Peru — found consistent, lasting gains a full year after all support ended, across consumption, assets, food security, income and psychological well-being (Banerjee, Duflo et al., Science 2015). The Bangladesh evaluation tracked households for seven years and showed the poorest shifting occupation out of casual labour into self-employment — a durable change in the kind of economic life available to them (Bandiera et al. 2017).

A big push, not a nudge

The design intuition is a poverty trap: below a threshold of assets and skills, a household cannot accumulate — every shock forces it to sell what little it has. A small nudge slides back. The bundle is engineered as a single large push over the threshold, after which the household’s own savings and enterprise carry it forward. That is why the gains last: the intervention changed the household’s starting point, not just its current income.

From Lean NGO to Government Scale

Graduation was built by a disciplined NGO running a tight, high-touch protocol. Its promise — and its peril — is what happens when governments adopt it. In India, the approach was absorbed into state rural livelihoods missions (SRLMs) under the National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM), layering graduation onto the existing self-help-group architecture to reach the “poorest of the poor” that SHGs alone often miss.

When a bundled intervention enters a bureaucracy, the layers most likely to erode are the invisible, labour-intensive ones — coaching and sequencing — precisely the ones that made it work. The asset transfer survives (it is procurable and countable); the handholding fades. Modules 11–12 return to this as the central scaling problem: preserving the function of a bundle, not just its form.

Case — graduation into an Indian SRLM

A state rural livelihoods mission decides to reach ultra-poor households its SHGs have never enrolled — landless, no reliable earner, outside the group system. It adopts the graduation bundle: a goat or poultry asset, a few months’ consumption stipend, husbandry training, visits from a community resource person, and enrolment in an SHG savings group.

What travels well. The asset transfer and savings link ride on infrastructure the mission already has — procurement, bank linkage, the SHG network. Targeting the poorest into existing groups extends the mission’s reach downward.

What strains. Coaching intensity collapses first: one resource person covering hundreds of households cannot replicate BRAC’s visit frequency. Sequencing slips when asset delivery and stipend arrive out of order because of procurement calendars. Where fidelity to the sequence is protected, results approach the trial gains; where it is not, the bundle decays into an asset giveaway with fading effects.

The design lesson. Adoption by a mission is not free replication — it is a re-design problem. The question is which layers can be delegated to a bureaucracy and which must be protected as non-negotiable, a judgement Modules 11–13 make explicit.

Reflect. Take a single-instrument programme you know — a cash transfer, a training, a microloan. What are the other binding constraints it leaves untouched, and which two or three complementary components would you sequence around it to turn a nudge into a push? Then ask the harder question: which of those components would a government system quietly drop first?
