Fundamentals

Who sits close to power
and who sits at the rim

The Indian Wheel of Power & Powerlessness, made clickable. Twelve axes of difference, three bands each, thirty-six places a person can stand. Open any band and you get the Indian evidence behind its placement — the survey, the law or the judgment, with the source and the year named.

And, on every axis, an honest note on what that evidence does not settle.

The wheel is not ours. It was built in 2022–23 by The Listeners Collective, Bengaluru, adapting Sylvia Duckworth’s Wheel of Power/Privilege. What ImpactMojo adds is the evidence layer. Full credits ↓

How to read it

The centre is power. Everything else is distance from it.

Each wedge is one axis along which Indian society sorts people. Each ring is a position on that axis. The closer to the hub, the more of the room’s power that group tends to hold — in law, in assets, in who gets counted.

Inner band — closer to powerThe group whose position is the unmarked default: the one the law was drafted around, the one the survey category was designed for.
Middle band — in betweenReal advantages and real ceilings at once. This is where most people live, and where the wheel is hardest to draw.
Outer band — pushed to the marginSystematically worse outcomes, weaker legal standing, or — often — no official count at all.

What this wheel is not

It is not a score. There is no total, no ranking of who has it worst. Adding up bands would turn a map of structures into a competition between people, which is the opposite of what it is for.

It is not additive. A Dalit woman’s position is not “woman, plus caste”. Kimberlé Crenshaw’s point in naming intersectionality, and Gopal Guru’s in writing that Dalit women talk differently, is that the axes compound into something the rings cannot show separately.

It is not fixed. Placement shifts by state, by decade and by room. A Christian in Mizoram, a Muslim in Lakshadweep and a Hindi speaker in Chennai each sit somewhere the national average does not predict.

The map

Thirty-six bands, each with its evidence

Click or tap any band. On a keyboard: tab into the wheel, then use the arrow keys — left and right move between axes, up and down move between bands — and Enter to open one. Every selection has its own link you can share.

Marking is a reflection exercise, kept in your browser and sent nowhere. It produces no score.

Method

How a band earns its place

A placement on this page had to be backed by at least one of three things: a national statistical source (Census, NFHS, PLFS, NSS, NCRB, AISHE, UDISE+, the Time Use Survey), a statute or constitutional provision, or a reported judgment. Where a figure could not be sourced to a named publication and year, it is not on the page.

That rule left visible holes, and we have left them visible rather than filling them with plausible numbers:

  • Body and appearance is the thinnest axis here. No Indian official survey collects skin tone, body size or appearance-based discrimination. Its placement rests on market and case-law evidence, and the panel says so.
  • Sexual orientation has no population data at all — not in the Census, not in NFHS, not in PLFS. Every figure in that wedge is about law rather than people.
  • Disability is measured at 2.2% of the population against a global estimate near 16%. The outer band there is largely a population the state has not counted, and the undercount is the disadvantage.
  • Caste beyond SC/ST has not been enumerated nationally since 1931, so the OBC band rests on state surveys and sample data rather than a census.

Two figures are drawn from the NITI Aayog National MPI baseline round (NFHS-4, 2015–16) rather than the 2023 Progress Review, because the baseline is where the social-group breakdown is published. They are labelled that way in the panels.

Corrections are welcome and wanted — if a figure here is stale, misattributed or misread, tell us or open an issue on GitHub.

The idea underneath it

Kimberlé Crenshaw named intersectionality in 1989; Patricia Hill Collins gave it the “matrix of domination”. Every wheel of this kind is a diagram of that argument.

Crenshaw 1989 · Collins 1990

The Indian lineage

The claim that Indian power has to be read through caste and gender together long predates the diagram: B.R. Ambedkar (Annihilation of Caste, 1936), Savitribai and Jotirao Phule, Uma Chakravarti on brahmanical patriarchy, Sharmila Rege on Dalit feminist standpoint, and Gopal Guru’s “Dalit Women Talk Differently” (1995).

What ImpactMojo added

The interactive map, and the evidence layer: 113 sourced statistics, statutes and judgments attached to the 36 bands, each with a named publication and year, plus a per-axis note on what the evidence does not settle.

Using this in a classroom or workshop

The wheel works best as a conversation opener, not a handout. A common sequence: let people mark themselves privately, then discuss one axis where their placement surprised them, then look at the evidence panel together and argue with it. The disagreements are the point.
