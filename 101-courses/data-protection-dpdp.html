Orientation
What this course covers
India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is now the law of the land, and the DPDP Rules, 2025 put it into operation. For NGOs that hold beneficiary lists, survivor records and health data, this is not just a compliance headache — it is a duty of care.

  • Your obligations — security, breach reporting and retention limits
  • Act now — a practical checklist to get your organisation ready

Why data protection matters for NGOs

Why It Matters
When a list leaks, people get hurt
  • A GBV survivor register is shared on WhatsApp — an abuser locates a woman in a shelter
  • An HIV or TB programme's beneficiary sheet leaks — clients face stigma, blackmail, lost jobs
  • A microfinance repayment list is sold — borrowers are targeted by predatory lenders and scammers
  • A minority-rights membership database is exposed — activists and members face harassment
  • Children's photos from a school programme circulate — used with no consent, impossible to recall
The data an NGO holds is often the most sensitive there is. A leak is not an IT incident — it is a safeguarding failure.

Duty of Care
Trust is your operating licence
Trust is how communities engage you
People share their status, income and location because they trust you to protect them. Mishandle it once and word travels — enrolment drops, and the hardest-to-reach withdraw first.
“Do no harm” is already your standard
Humanitarian codes — Sphere, the IASC and protection-mainstreaming guidance — have long required safeguarding personal information. DPDP now gives that ethical duty the force of Indian law.

Your Data Footprint
You hold more personal data than you think
  • Identity — names, ID numbers, photographs, family details
  • Contact & location — phone numbers, village, GPS from field apps
  • Financial — bank accounts for direct benefit transfer, loan and repayment records
  • Health — HIV or TB status, disability, maternal and child records
  • Sensitive circumstance — caste, religion, GBV and trafficking case notes
All of this is “digital personal data” the moment it lands in a spreadsheet, an M&E app, a Google Form or a CRM.

Common Weak Points
Where NGO data usually leaks
  • Shared drives & open sheets — “anyone with the link” sharing set once and forgotten
  • WhatsApp groups — beneficiary sheets forwarded to volunteers and donors
  • Personal laptops & USB sticks — unencrypted, lost or stolen in the field
  • Ex-staff & volunteers — access never revoked after they leave
  • Donor & vendor sharing — raw data sent for reports with no agreement in place

The Shift
From ‘our data’ to ‘their data’
“The right to privacy is protected as an intrinsic part of the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21.”
— Supreme Court of India, Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, 2017
The DPDP Act is the statute that the Puttaswamy judgment called for. Its core reframing: the person in your database is not a case number you own — they are a Data Principal with enforceable rights over their own information.

Meet the DPDP Act, 2023

The Law
What the DPDP Act is
The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (Act No. 22 of 2023) received Presidential assent on 11 August 2023. It is India's first standalone law governing how personal data is collected, used, stored and shared.
  • Covers digital personal data — data about an identifiable individual, in digital form or later digitised
  • A rights-and-obligations model — rights for individuals, duties for those who process their data
  • Consent at its core — processing generally needs free, informed consent or a defined legitimate use
  • Enforced by a regulator — the Data Protection Board of India, with financial penalties

Scope
Who and where it applies
Applies to
Processing of digital personal data within India. Also processing outside India where it is connected to offering goods or services to people in India — so an overseas grant portal handling Indian beneficiaries is covered.
Does not apply to
Purely personal or household use, and data an individual has made publicly available themselves. Some research, archiving and statistical uses may be exempt, subject to standards.

The Rules
The DPDP Rules, 2025 — in force, in phases
The Act needed rules to become operational. The DPDP Rules, 2025 were notified on 13 November 2025, with a phased rollout so organisations can prepare.
  • From Nov 2025 — the Data Protection Board of India is established and begins functioning
  • By Nov 2026 — the Consent Manager registration framework becomes operational
  • By mid-2027 — the core obligations bite: notice & consent, security safeguards, breach reporting, retention limits and Data Principal rights
You have a runway, not a reprieve. Building good practice now is far cheaper than retrofitting under deadline.

Who's Who
The people the Act names (1 of 2)
Data Principal
The individual the personal data is about — your beneficiary, participant or staff member. For a child, it includes the parent or lawful guardian.
Data Fiduciary
Whoever decides the purpose and means of processing — usually your NGO. The word “fiduciary” signals a relationship of trust, not merely a data holder.
Data Processor
Anyone who processes data on the Fiduciary's behalf under a contract — a survey-app vendor, a cloud host, an SMS gateway.

Who's Who
The people the Act names (2 of 2)
Significant Data Fiduciary
A Fiduciary the government designates as higher-risk by data volume, sensitivity or impact. It carries extra duties — a Data Protection Officer, audits and impact assessments.
Consent Manager
A Board-registered intermediary through which a Data Principal can give, review, manage and withdraw consent across Fiduciaries from a single place.
Data Protection Board of India
The regulator. It investigates breaches, hears complaints and imposes penalties. It is not a court, but its orders are binding and appealable.

Part Three
Consent & notice
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series16www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoData Protection & the DPDP Act 101www.impactmojo.in
Consent
What counts as valid consent
Consent must be a clear affirmative action — and under the Act it must be:
  • Free — not coerced by withholding an unrelated service
  • Specific — tied to a stated purpose, not a blanket “we may use your data”
  • Informed — the person knows what data, why, and their rights
  • Unconditional & unambiguous — a genuine yes, not a pre-ticked box or silence
Consent is limited to what is necessary for the stated purpose. Asking for more “just in case” is not lawful.
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series17www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoData Protection & the DPDP Act 101www.impactmojo.in
Notice
The itemised notice you must give
Before or when you seek consent, you must give the Data Principal a clear notice — available in English and the languages of the Eighth Schedule — that states:
  • What personal data you will collect
  • Why — the specific purpose of processing
  • How they can exercise their rights and withdraw consent
  • How to complain to the Data Protection Board
Plain language beats legalese. A notice a rural participant cannot understand does not make consent “informed.”

Withdrawal
Withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it
The rule
A Data Principal can withdraw consent at any time. The effort to withdraw must be comparable to the effort it took to give it — no buried forms or endless queues.
What follows
On withdrawal you must stop processing within a reasonable time, and ensure your Processors do too — unless another law requires you to keep the data.

Children
Children's data needs a parent's yes
A child under the Act is anyone under 18 years. Their data carries the strictest rules.
  • Verifiable parental consent is required before processing a child's data
  • No behavioural tracking or targeted advertising directed at children
  • No processing likely to harm a child's well-being
This reshapes child-sponsorship, school and youth programmes. A minor's photos, stories and progress records need a guardian's verified consent — not just the child's.

Legitimate Uses
When you can process without fresh consent
The Act allows certain “legitimate uses” where separate consent is not required — but the other duties, such as security and purpose limits, still apply. These include:
  • Data voluntarily given for a purpose the person clearly had in mind
  • State provision of benefits, subsidies, services or certificates the person seeks
  • Medical emergencies and threats to life or public health
  • Disaster and public-order situations, and legal obligations
Do not over-read the exemptions. For routine NGO programme data, valid consent remains your safest and default basis.

Part Four
Rights — and duties — of the Data Principal
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series22www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoData Protection & the DPDP Act 101www.impactmojo.in
Rights
What a Data Principal can demand
  • Right to information — a summary of what data of theirs you process and whom you have shared it with
  • Right to correction & completion — fix inaccurate or incomplete records
  • Right to erasure — delete data no longer needed for its purpose or after consent is withdrawn
  • Right to grievance redressal — a response from you before escalating to the Board
  • Right to nominate — name someone to exercise their rights on death or incapacity
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series23www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoData Protection & the DPDP Act 101www.impactmojo.in
Grievances
Every Fiduciary needs a way to be complained to
Publish a contact
Give Data Principals an easy way to raise a grievance — a named person or office and their contact details, on your website and on consent forms.
Respond in time
Grievances must be answered within the period the Rules prescribe. Only after your mechanism fails may a person escalate to the Data Protection Board.
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series24www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoData Protection & the DPDP Act 101www.impactmojo.in
Nomination
Rights that outlive the individual
A quietly important right
Nomination
A Data Principal can nominate another person to exercise their rights if they die or become incapacitated.
For NGOs running long-term health, disability or legacy programmes this matters: build a nomination field into enrolment so families are not locked out of a deceased member's records.
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series25www.impactmojo.in

Duties
Data Principals have duties too
The Act is not one-sided. A Data Principal must also act in good faith:
  • No false or frivolous complaints to a Fiduciary or the Board
  • No impersonation — do not furnish another person's identity as your own where the law requires it
  • No suppression of material information in documents or applications
Breaching these duties can attract a penalty of up to ₹10,000. Worth knowing — but your organisation's obligations carry far higher stakes.

Obligations of the Data Fiduciary
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series27www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoData Protection & the DPDP Act 101www.impactmojo.in
Your Duties
The core duties of every Data Fiduciary
  • Lawful basis — process only on valid consent or a legitimate use
  • Purpose limitation — use data only for the purpose it was collected for
  • Data accuracy — keep data correct and complete where it affects decisions about a person
  • Security safeguards — protect data with reasonable technical and organisational measures
  • Retention limits — erase data once its purpose is served
  • Accountability — stay responsible even when a Processor holds the data
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series28www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoData Protection & the DPDP Act 101www.impactmojo.in
Security
Reasonable security safeguards, in practice
The Act does not name specific tools, but the Rules expect concrete measures. For an NGO that means, at minimum:
  • Access control — role-based access; only staff who need the data can see it
  • Encryption — on laptops, backups and data in transit
  • Logging & monitoring — know who accessed what, to detect and investigate a breach
  • Backups — so data can be restored after loss or ransomware
Failure here carries the highest penalty — up to ₹250 crore. Security is the obligation the Board treats most seriously.
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series29www.impactmojo.in

Breaches
When data leaks, you must tell people
On becoming aware of a personal data breach, a Data Fiduciary must:
  • Notify the Data Protection Board without delay, with a fuller report to follow
  • Notify every affected Data Principal — in plain language, promptly
The notice to affected people must describe the breach, the data involved, what they can do to protect themselves, and how to reach you.
“Breach” is broad — it covers unauthorised access, disclosure, loss or destruction. A lost unencrypted laptop counts.

Retention
Keep it only as long as you need it
Delete by default
Once the purpose is met and no law requires retention, erase the data — and have your Processors do the same. Data you no longer hold cannot leak.
Balance real obligations
Donor-grant audit trails, FCRA filings and statutory records have their own retention periods. Map which is which rather than keeping everything forever.

Vendors
You are on the hook for your vendors
You may engage a Data Processor — a survey app, cloud host or call centre — only under a valid contract. And accountability does not transfer:
  • You remain the Fiduciary — answerable to the Data Principal for what your Processor does
  • Bind them in writing — purpose limits, security, breach reporting to you, deletion on exit
  • Check where data lives — know your vendor's storage and any sub-processors
“The vendor lost it” is not a defence. A handshake deal with an app developer leaves your NGO carrying the penalty.

SDFs
If you are designated a Significant Data Fiduciary
The government can designate large or high-risk Fiduciaries as Significant Data Fiduciaries. Few small NGOs will be — but large networks and data-heavy programmes might. An SDF must additionally:
  • Appoint a Data Protection Officer based in India, answerable to the governing body
  • Appoint an independent data auditor and undergo periodic audits
  • Conduct Data Protection Impact Assessments for high-risk processing
Even if you are not an SDF, these are good practice. A DPO-like focal point and a simple impact assessment strengthen any organisation.

What your NGO should do now

Step 1
Start with a data map
You cannot protect what you have not mapped. Walk every programme and answer:
  • What personal data do we collect, and how sensitive is it?
  • Why — what is the specific purpose?
  • Where does it live — forms, apps, sheets, drives, vendors?
  • Who can access it, and whom have we shared it with?
  • How long do we keep it, and when do we delete it?

Step 2
Fix consent at the front line
For every participant
One purpose-specific consent per programme, in the person's language, with a real option to say no and an easy way to withdraw later.
For children under 18
Add verified guardian consent for any minor's photos, stories or records. No behavioural targeting of children, ever.

Step 3
Prepare for the bad day
Assume a breach will happen and rehearse the response before it does:
  • Who to call — a named incident lead and a decision-maker on speed dial
  • What to say — draft breach notices for the Board and affected people in advance
  • How to contain — revoke access, reset credentials, preserve logs
Culture beats policy. Ban beneficiary lists on WhatsApp, lock down “anyone with the link” sheets, and revoke access the day someone leaves.

In Summary
Key takeaways
  • The law is real and in force — the DPDP Act 2023 plus the 2025 Rules; core duties bite by mid-2027
  • You are a Data Fiduciary — the beneficiaries in your files are Data Principals with enforceable rights
  • Consent must be free, specific, informed and withdrawable — and children under 18 need a guardian's yes
  • Secure, minimise, delete — and be ready to report a breach to the Board and to affected people
  • Start now — a data map, better consent forms, a retention policy and staff training cost little and protect the people you serve
Data protection is safeguarding. For an NGO, guarding someone's data is guarding the person.
