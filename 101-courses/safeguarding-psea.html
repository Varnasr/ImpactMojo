Orientation
Safeguarding & PSEA — what this course covers
Every organisation that delivers aid, runs programmes, or works with communities holds power over the people it serves. Safeguarding is how we make sure that power is never used to harm, exploit or abuse them — especially sexually.
  • What we mean — safeguarding, PSEA, sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment, and the power imbalance at the centre of it all
  • Why it matters — the sector’s reckoning after Oxfam Haiti, donor rules, “do no harm”, and why abuse is so under-reported
  • The standards — the IASC six core principles, the UN red lines, the Core Humanitarian Standard
  • The red lines & your conduct — the bright-line rules that apply to every staff member and volunteer
  • Reporting & survivor-centred response — safe channels, confidentiality, referral, protection from retaliation
  • Building a culture — risk assessment, safe recruitment, focal points and community complaint mechanisms
A note on this topic. This is sensitive material. Throughout, we keep the survivor at the centre — their safety, dignity and choices come first.

What we mean

Definitions
Safeguarding: the responsibility, not just a policy
Safeguarding is the set of measures an organisation takes to ensure its staff, operations and programmes do no harm to the people they work with — and that any harm that does occur is reported and responded to.
Safeguarding
Preventing and responding to harm caused — through action or inaction — to those an organisation comes into contact with: affected populations, communities, and staff. It covers sexual exploitation and abuse, sexual harassment, and wider forms of harm and abuse of power.
Not a paper exercise. A signed policy protects no one. Safeguarding lives in recruitment, training, reporting channels, and everyday behaviour — or it does not exist at all.

Definitions
PSEA sits inside safeguarding
PSEA — Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse — is the part of safeguarding focused on sexual harm to the affected population: the community members, beneficiaries and vulnerable people an organisation is there to serve.
Safeguarding (the umbrella)
All harm to all people the organisation touches — children, adults, communities and staff.
PSEA (a core part of it)
Sexual exploitation and abuse of the people we serve, by those with power over them. Increasingly written PSEAH to also cover harassment.
In the humanitarian and development sector the framing is now often PSEAH — Protection from Sexual Exploitation, Abuse and Harassment — folding staff-to-staff harassment into the same commitment.

The Three Terms
Sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment — defined
The sector uses the definitions from the UN Secretary-General’s Bulletin ST/SGB/2003/13. Learn the distinctions — they decide how a report is handled.
Sexual Exploitation
Any actual or attempted abuse of a position of vulnerability, differential power or trust, for sexual purposes — including, but not limited to, profiting monetarily, socially or politically.
Sexual Abuse
The actual or threatened physical intrusion of a sexual nature, whether by force or under unequal or coercive conditions.
Sexual Harassment
Any unwelcome sexual advance, request for sexual favour, or verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature — typically in the workplace, between colleagues (ST/SGB/2008/5).

The Core Idea
The power imbalance is the whole point
An aid worker controls access to food, shelter, cash, a job, a referral, a place on a list. The person in front of them may have almost nothing. That gap in power is what makes “consent” unreliable and exploitation possible.
Why the rules are strict
Where power is deeply unequal, apparent consent cannot make a sexual relationship acceptable.
A displaced widow in a relief camp who agrees to sex with the officer distributing rations is not freely consenting — she is responding to power over her survival. This is why the sector treats such relationships as prohibited or strongly discouraged, not as private matters.
Hold this thought. Nearly every red line in this course flows from one fact: we hold power over the people we serve.

Scope
Who is protected — and who must comply
Who is protected
  • Beneficiaries and affected communities
  • Children (anyone under 18) — always
  • People in vulnerable or dependent situations: refugees, the displaced, disaster survivors, women, people with disabilities
  • Staff, from harassment by colleagues
Who must comply
  • All employees — national and international
  • Volunteers and interns
  • Consultants, contractors and suppliers
  • Partner organisations and their staff
  • Board members and visitors
Everyone, everywhere. The standard does not change with your seniority, your location, or whether you are “on the clock”.

The Reckoning
Oxfam, Haiti, and the sector’s wake-up call
In February 2018, reporting revealed that senior Oxfam staff had used sex workers while responding to the 2010 Haiti earthquake — and that the charity had failed to handle the cases properly. The story became the sector’s reckoning.
  • The failure — abuse of a position of power amid extreme vulnerability, and mishandled internal response
  • The regulator — in 2019 the Charity Commission for England & Wales found Oxfam GB had mismanaged its safeguarding response
  • The shift — safeguarding moved from the margins to a board-level, donor-level priority across the whole sector
“We must learn and change.”
— Oxfam, in the aftermath of the Haiti scandal, 2019

The Reckoning
What the scandal changed
The response reshaped how organisations recruit, train and are held to account. Oxfam alone created a Director of Safeguarding role and made safeguarding training mandatory for its staff worldwide; the sector built shared infrastructure.
  • Shared reference checking — the inter-agency Misconduct Disclosure Scheme, to stop perpetrators moving quietly between agencies
  • A UK Parliamentary inquiry and a global safeguarding summit put the sector under scrutiny
  • Donor conditions tightened — safeguarding policies and reporting became a condition of funding
  • Dedicated capacity — safeguarding leads, focal points and budgets, not add-on duties
The lesson. The harm was real, but the deeper failure was the system — weak vetting, no safe reporting, and a culture that protected the institution over the survivor.

The Mandate
Donors now require it
Safeguarding is no longer optional good practice — it is a contractual condition. Major donors require partners to have policies, reporting mechanisms and the ability to act on allegations.
  • Bilateral donors — the UK FCDO, USAID, EU and others make PSEAH capacity a due-diligence and grant requirement
  • UN agencies — require implementing partners to be assessed against a PSEA capacity standard before funds flow
  • Pooled funds — humanitarian country funds screen partners for safeguarding systems
Practical stake. For a South Asian NGO, weak safeguarding is not just an ethical failing — it can disqualify you from funding and partnerships.

The Principle
“Do no harm” — the ethical floor
Humanitarian and development work rests on a first duty: intervention must not leave people worse off. Sexual exploitation by aid workers is the most severe possible breach of that duty — harm inflicted by the very people sent to help.
Do no harm
The people we serve came to us for protection. Betraying that trust is not a lapse — it is a fundamental violation of the mission.
Abuse also corrodes the wider effort: it destroys community trust, endangers other staff, and can shut down programmes that thousands depend on.

The Hidden Scale
Why abuse is so under-reported
Recorded cases are the tip of the iceberg. The same power imbalance that enables abuse also silences it — so low report numbers signal broken systems, not safe programmes.
  • Fear of losing aid — a complaint may mean losing rations, a job, or a place in the programme
  • Fear of retaliation — against the survivor or their family
  • Shame and stigma — especially heavy for women and girls in many South Asian contexts
  • No safe way to report — channels are unknown, inaccessible, or run through the very people implicated
  • Disbelief — a belief that nothing will be done
Read the silence. Zero reports usually means people do not feel safe to speak — not that nothing is happening.

The Framework
The IASC Six Core Principles
The Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) — the coordination forum of UN and non-UN humanitarian agencies — set out six core principles on sexual exploitation and abuse (2002, revised 2019). They are the sector’s shared baseline; most organisational codes of conduct are built on them.

The Framework
IASC principles 1–3
  • 1 · Grounds for dismissal — sexual exploitation and abuse by humanitarian workers constitute acts of gross misconduct and are grounds for termination of employment
  • 2 · No sexual activity with children — sexual activity with anyone under 18 is prohibited regardless of the local age of majority or consent; mistaken belief about a child’s age is no defence
  • 3 · No exchange for sex — exchange of money, employment, goods or services for sex — including sexual favours or other humiliating, degrading or exploitative behaviour — is prohibited, including the exchange of assistance that is due to beneficiaries
Note. Principle 3 explicitly bans withholding or trading aid that people are already entitled to.
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series17www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoSafeguarding & PSEA 101www.impactmojo.in
The Framework
IASC principles 4–6
  • 4 · No abuse of position — sexual relationships between those delivering assistance and those benefiting from it that involve improper use of rank or position are prohibited; they undermine the credibility and integrity of the work
  • 5 · Duty to report — where a worker has concerns or suspicions about sexual abuse or exploitation by a colleague — in their own agency or another — they must report through established mechanisms
  • 6 · Duty to foster a safe environment — workers must help create and maintain an environment that prevents SEA; managers at all levels carry particular responsibility for the systems that sustain it
Reporting is mandatory. Principle 5 makes staying silent a breach in itself — not a neutral choice.

The Framework
The UN red lines — ST/SGB/2003/13
The UN Secretary-General’s Bulletin turned the principles into binding standards for UN staff and those working with the UN. Its bright lines:
  • SEA is serious misconduct — grounds for disciplinary measures, including summary dismissal
  • Under 18 — never — sexual activity with children is prohibited; mistaken belief about age is not a defence
  • No exchange — money, employment, goods or services for sex is prohibited
  • Relationships with beneficiaries — based on inherently unequal power, they undermine the work’s integrity and are strongly discouraged
  • Managers must act — create an environment that prevents SEA and act on concerns
Source: UN Secretary-General’s Bulletin, ST/SGB/2003/13 (2003).

The Framework
The Core Humanitarian Standard
The Core Humanitarian Standard (CHS) sets out commitments to quality and accountability toward people affected by crisis. Revised in 2024 after consultation with over 4,000 people across 90 countries, it now weaves protection from sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment (PSEAH) throughout its Nine Commitments.
What CHS requires
Safe, accessible complaints handling; staff who behave ethically; and communities who know their rights and can hold responders to account.
The PSEAH Index
Sets out the safeguarding requirements embedded in the CHS, so an organisation can verify whether its policies and practices actually protect people.
Source: Core Humanitarian Standard on Quality and Accountability, 2024 edition; CHS Alliance.

Two Tracks
PSEA and staff harassment — related but distinct
Both are sexual misconduct and both matter — but who is harmed differs, and so do the processes. Do not collapse them.
PSEA (external)Sexual Harassment (internal)
Harm to the affected population — beneficiaries, community membersHarm between staff — colleague to colleague, manager to junior
Rooted in the aid worker’s power over vulnerable peopleRooted in workplace power and hierarchy
Sexual exploitation and sexual abuseUnwelcome sexual conduct in the workplace
Handled via PSEA / community reporting channelsHandled via HR / workplace grievance channels
Why keep them separate. A beneficiary and an employee need different protections, referral routes and safeguards — even under the shared PSEAH banner.

Code of Conduct
Zero tolerance means action, not a slogan
“Zero tolerance” does not mean zero incidents — it means zero tolerance for inaction: every allegation is taken seriously, reported, and acted on, and confirmed misconduct ends employment.
  • Every report is acted on — no allegation is buried, minimised or informally settled
  • Confirmed SEA ends employment — it is gross misconduct
  • No cover-ups — protecting the organisation’s reputation over a survivor is itself a safeguarding failure
  • Applies to everyone — including senior and high-performing staff
Balance. Zero tolerance for abuse sits alongside due process — the accused have a right to a fair investigation, and the survivor to a safe, respectful one.

ImpactMojoSafeguarding & PSEA 101www.impactmojo.in
Red Line
No sexual activity with anyone under 18
Bright line — no exceptions
Sexual activity with a child — anyone under 18 — is prohibited, whatever the local age of consent.
In many South Asian settings a person may look older, be married, or the local age of majority may differ. None of that changes the rule.
  • Mistaken belief about age is no defence — “I thought they were older” does not excuse it
  • Local law does not lower the bar — the sector standard is 18, full stop
  • Consent is irrelevant — a child cannot consent to sexual activity with an aid worker
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series24www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoSafeguarding & PSEA 101www.impactmojo.in
Red Line
No transactional or exchange sex
Trading anything of value — aid, cash, a job, a document, a referral, a place on a list — for sex is prohibited. It is exploitation of need, dressed up as a transaction.
  • Aid is never a currency for sex — including assistance people are already entitled to
  • “Favours” count — speeding up a claim, adding a name, quiet preferential treatment
  • Paying sex workers is prohibited too — this was central to the Oxfam Haiti case; the ban applies even where sex work is legal locally
No grey zone. If a sexual interaction is entangled with your role, your resources or your authority, it is prohibited — regardless of what the other person appears to agree to.
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series25www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoSafeguarding & PSEA 101www.impactmojo.in
Red Line
Relationships with beneficiaries & abuse of authority
Because the power gap between an aid worker and a beneficiary is inherently unequal, sexual relationships between them undermine the integrity of the work and are prohibited or strongly discouraged under most organisational codes.
Relationships with beneficiaries
The dependency makes genuine consent unreliable. Many organisations prohibit such relationships outright; all require they be disclosed and never involve improper use of position.
Abuse of authority
Using your rank, access or control — over aid, jobs, or a subordinate — to obtain sexual advantage is a defining feature of both SEA and workplace harassment.
When in doubt, disclose. Raise it with your safeguarding focal point — do not decide alone that a relationship is “fine”.

Reporting & survivor-centred response
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series27www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoSafeguarding & PSEA 101www.impactmojo.in
Reporting
Safe, accessible reporting channels
People can only report if a channel exists that is safe, known and usable for them. A hotline in English, run through the same office that employs the alleged perpetrator, is not a real channel for a woman in a remote camp.
  • Multiple channels — phone, in-person, suggestion box, trusted focal point, partner organisations
  • Accessible — in local languages, reachable by women, people with disabilities, and those who cannot read
  • Independent — a route that does not pass through the person implicated
  • Known — communities are told how to report, and what will happen when they do
Design with the community. Ask affected people how they would want to report — a channel they did not shape is a channel they will not use.
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series28www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoSafeguarding & PSEA 101www.impactmojo.in
Reporting
Confidentiality & data protection
Mishandled information can put a survivor in danger. Details are shared strictly on a need-to-know basis, and only ever with the survivor’s informed consent — with one exception.
  • Need-to-know only — identifying details go no further than those who must act
  • Informed consent to share — the survivor decides what is shared and with whom, wherever possible
  • Secure records — access-controlled, not gossiped, emailed carelessly or left on a desk
  • The child exception — where a child is at risk, mandatory child-protection reporting and their best interests may require action beyond a simple consent rule
A breach can be lethal. In some communities, exposure of a survivor’s identity risks violence, ostracism, or so-called “honour” retaliation.

Response
The survivor-centred approach
When someone discloses, how you respond matters as much as any investigation. The survivor-centred approach rests on four principles that put the survivor’s rights, dignity and wishes first.
Safety
The survivor’s safety is the first priority in every decision
Confidentiality
Information protected and shared only with consent
Respect
The survivor’s choices and wishes guide the response
Non-discrimination
Fair, equal treatment for everyone who comes forward
Source: IASC / IFRC survivor-centred approach.

Response
How to receive a disclosure
You do not need to investigate — you need to receive the disclosure safely and pass it on. Your first job is to do no further harm.
Do
  • Listen, believe, stay calm
  • Ask what they need to be safe
  • Explain what you must do next, and get consent to refer where possible
  • Report through your safeguarding channel promptly
Do not
  • Interrogate or press for graphic detail
  • Promise to keep it entirely secret
  • Confront the alleged perpetrator yourself
  • Judge, blame, or share the story

Response
Referral pathways — connecting survivors to help
Reporting is not the same as support. A survivor may need care that no aid worker can provide alone — so every programme should map, in advance, where to refer, with the survivor’s consent.
  • Medical — time-sensitive care; some services (including post-rape care) matter most within the first hours and days
  • Psychosocial — counselling and mental-health support
  • Legal — advice on rights and options, without pressure to pursue any route
  • Safety & basic needs — safe shelter and protection where there is ongoing risk
Map it before you need it. Know your local referral network — women’s organisations, One Stop Centres, health facilities — so support is ready the moment someone discloses.

Protection
No retaliation — and safe whistleblowing
People will only come forward if speaking up is safe. Protecting survivors, witnesses and reporters from retaliation is a core obligation — and reporting good-faith concerns is a duty, not disloyalty.
  • Protection from retaliation — no one is punished, demoted or excluded for reporting in good faith
  • Good-faith reports are protected — even if an allegation is not ultimately substantiated
  • The duty to report — staying silent about known abuse is itself a breach (IASC Principle 5)
  • Whistleblower routes — a confidential channel, including outside the normal line manager, for raising concerns
Retaliation is misconduct too. Punishing someone for reporting compounds the original harm and must be treated as a safeguarding violation in its own right.

Building a safeguarding culture

Prevention
Start with a safeguarding risk assessment
Prevention begins by asking where your own work could enable harm — then designing that risk out. Different activities carry very different exposure.
  • Contact with children — schools, child-focused or residential programmes
  • Control over resources — cash transfers, distributions, beneficiary selection, job allocation
  • Acute vulnerability — camps, disaster response, trafficking-prone or conflict-affected areas
  • Isolated or unsupervised work — home visits, remote field sites, night operations
Then mitigate. Two-person rules, transparent selection criteria, community oversight, and clear codes of conduct all reduce the opportunity for abuse.
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series35www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoSafeguarding & PSEA 101www.impactmojo.in
Prevention
Safe recruitment — keeping perpetrators out
Abusers move between organisations, exploiting weak checks and short deployments. Safe recruitment closes that door before someone is ever hired.
  • Values-based screening — interviews and scenarios that probe attitudes to power and safeguarding
  • Rigorous reference checks — verify history directly with past employers, not just named referees
  • Background checks — where lawful and available, including for roles working with children
Misconduct Disclosure Scheme
An inter-agency scheme (facilitated by SCHR, hosted by the CHS Alliance) where employers share, on request, whether a candidate was found to have committed sexual exploitation, abuse or harassment — so perpetrators cannot move between agencies undetected. Adopted by 170+ organisations, it has blocked hundreds of such hires.

Prevention
Induction, focal points & community mechanisms
Policy becomes culture through everyday structures — people trained to know the rules, someone clearly responsible, and communities equipped to hold the organisation to account.
Inside the organisation
  • Safeguarding in induction and regular refreshers
  • A signed, understood code of conduct
  • A named safeguarding focal point staff can approach
With the community
  • Community-based complaint mechanisms shaped with local people
  • Communities informed of their rights and how to report
  • Feedback taken seriously and acted on
Leaders set the tone. Where managers model accountability and act on reports, a speak-up culture follows — where they minimise, silence returns.

Wrap-Up
Key takeaways
  • Power is the heart of it — safeguarding exists because aid workers hold power over vulnerable people; every red line flows from that
  • PSEA protects the community; SH protects staff — related tracks, distinct processes, together as PSEAH
  • Know the bright lines — no sex with under-18s (age mistake is no defence), no exchange of aid for sex, no abuse of position with beneficiaries
  • The standards are shared — IASC Six Core Principles, ST/SGB/2003/13, and the Core Humanitarian Standard
  • Reporting is a duty — respond survivor-centred (safety, confidentiality, respect, non-discrimination), refer for care, and never retaliate
  • Culture prevents harm — risk assessment, safe recruitment, focal points and community mechanisms turn policy into protection
If you remember one thing. When someone discloses harm, keep them at the centre — and when in doubt, report. Silence protects abusers; safeguarding protects people.
