The Basics
What a large language model really does
It predicts text
A model like ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini is trained on vast amounts of text. Given your words, it predicts the next most likely chunk of text, one small piece at a time, until it has produced a full answer.
It is not a database
It does not look facts up in a verified library. It generates fluent, plausible language. Sounding confident and being correct are two very different things.
Rule of thumb. Treat the output as a well-read intern’s first draft — quick and genuinely useful, but always read and checked before it leaves your hands.

The Core Caution
Confident, but not always correct
“The model gives a wrong answer in exactly the same confident tone as a right one.”
— The single most important thing to remember
Why this matters
Fluency is not accuracy
The biggest risk for a busy practitioner is trusting a smooth paragraph that happens to be wrong. Errors that sound plausible — a mis-stated law, an invented statistic, a wrong district figure — are the easiest to miss. Build the habit of verifying before you forward, publish or act.

Set Expectations
What GenAI is not
  • Not a search engine — it does not browse the live web unless a tool explicitly adds that, and even then it can misread sources
  • Not a calculator or a spreadsheet — it can slip on sums; check any number that matters
  • Not a lawyer, doctor or auditor — it can draft and explain, but it cannot take professional responsibility
  • Not conscious and not neutral — it has no understanding and no opinions; it reflects patterns, and biases, in its training data
  • Not a decision-maker — it should never be the thing that decides who gets a benefit, a job or a service

Where It Helps
Where GenAI saves you time
Used for the right tasks, a chatbot can take an hour of drafting down to ten minutes of editing. The best uses have a human expert on both ends — you supply the material, and you check the result.
  • Writing — first drafts of reports, proposals, emails and social posts
  • Reading — summarising long PDFs, evaluations and policy documents
  • Language — translation and plain-language rewrites for communities
  • Data — first-pass coding of open-ended survey answers
  • Planning — brainstorming M&E indicators and workshop agendas
  • Meetings — turning rough notes into clean minutes and action lists

In Practice
Drafting and summarising
Drafting
A programme officer in Bihar pastes her bullet points and asks for a two-page donor update in a formal register. She gets a structured draft in seconds, then edits every figure and claim herself.
Summarising
Facing a 60-page evaluation report before a meeting, she asks for a one-page summary with key findings and open questions — then reads the sections that matter most in full.
Always. The AI drafts the words; you own the facts, the numbers and the tone. Nothing goes out unread.

Language & Access
Translation and plain language
South Asia is deeply multilingual. GenAI can produce fast first-pass translations — English to Hindi, Bangla, Tamil, Marathi and more — and rewrite dense text into plain language for communities.
  • Good for — draft translations, simplifying jargon, adjusting reading level
  • Weaker on — idiom, dialect, caste- and gender-sensitive phrasing, and low-resource languages
  • Tools — general models plus South-Asia-focused options such as Sarvam AI for Indian languages
Never publish an unchecked translation. A fluent-sounding error in a health or legal message can do real harm. Have a native speaker review anything community-facing.

Planning
Brainstorming M&E indicators
Stuck on how to measure a new livelihoods programme? Describe the outcome and ask for a menu of possible indicators to react to — it is faster to edit a list than to face a blank page.
  • Ask for a mix — output, outcome and process indicators, with a means of verification for each
  • Pressure-test — “which of these are hard to collect in a low-resource setting?”
  • Localise — align to your logframe, your donor and frameworks like the SDGs
Judgement stays with you. The model does not know your context, budget or theory of change. Use its list to think faster, not to decide what you will measure.

The Recipe
Anatomy of a good prompt
A vague prompt gets a vague answer. Give the model four things and the quality jumps.
  • Role — who it should act as: “You are an M&E officer at an Indian NGO”
  • Context — the background it needs: the programme, the audience, the constraints
  • Task — exactly what you want done, in one clear instruction
  • Format — the shape of the output: length, structure, tone, language
Memory hook. Role, Context, Task, Format — the more of your knowledge you put in, the more useful the draft that comes back.

Techniques
Give examples, then iterate
Show an example
Paste one paragraph in the style you want and say “match this voice.” A single good example teaches the model faster than a long description.
Treat it as a conversation
You rarely get it right first try. Reply with “shorter,” “more formal,” “add a line on risks” — each turn steers the draft closer.
Iterating is normal, not failure. The skill is in the follow-ups, not the first message.

Techniques
Ask it to show reasoning — and critique itself
  • “Think step by step” — asking for the reasoning, not just the answer, often improves quality and lets you spot where it went wrong
  • “Critique your own answer” — ask it to list weaknesses or missing points, then revise; models are surprisingly good at catching their own gaps
  • “What might be wrong or missing here?” — a quick check before you rely on anything
  • “What would you need to verify?” — turns the model into a checklist for your own review
Self-critique helps but is not proof. A model can confidently “confirm” something false. You still verify facts against a real source.

The risks you MUST manage

Overview
Five risks, one discipline
These tools are powerful precisely because they are fluent — which is also what makes their failures easy to miss. Five risks matter most for development work.
  • Hallucination — confident, fabricated facts and citations
  • Bias — Global-North skew and caste, gender and other stereotypes
  • Privacy — beneficiary data leaking into public tools
  • Confidentiality — unpublished strategy, partner and donor information
  • Over-reliance — skills and judgement quietly eroding
The one discipline. A human verifies, decides and takes responsibility — every time.

Risk 1
Hallucination: it will make things up
Because it generates plausible text rather than retrieving facts, a model will sometimes invent details — and present them with full confidence. This is a known, inherent property of today’s LLMs, not a rare bug.
  • Fake citations — real-sounding authors, journals and page numbers that do not exist
  • Invented statistics — precise-looking figures with no source
  • Wrong specifics — mis-stated laws, dates, scheme names or district data
Non-negotiable. Verify every fact, number, quote and reference against a primary source before you use it. If you cannot verify it, do not publish it.

Risk 2
Bias: whose world is in the data
Models learn from text that over-represents wealthy, English-speaking, Global-North perspectives. That skew shows up in quiet ways.
What it looks like
Solutions that assume Western contexts; caste and gender stereotypes in examples; erasing regional and minority realities; smoother, more “standard” English framed as better.
What to do
Name your context explicitly. Ask “whose perspective is missing?” Have community members and local experts review anything sensitive. Never let it profile a group.

Risk 3 · The Law
Privacy and India’s DPDP Act, 2023
Public AI chatbots may store and use what you type. Pasting a beneficiary’s personal data into one can be a data breach — and India now has a law that names the duty.
Data Principal
The individual the personal data is about — your beneficiary, staff member or respondent.
Data Fiduciary
The entity that decides why and how the data is processed — that is your organisation, and the duty is yours.
The rule. Never paste names, phone numbers, IDs, health, caste or location data of real people into a public tool. Anonymise first, or use an approved private system. Children’s data needs verifiable parental consent.

The Law
Why the DPDP Act should shape your practice
India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act was passed in 2023; the DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025, with obligations phasing in through 2026–2027. It sets duties for anyone handling Indians’ personal data.
  • Purpose & consent — collect only what you need, for a stated purpose, with consent
  • Security safeguards — a Data Fiduciary must protect the data it holds
  • The Board — a Data Protection Board of India can investigate and penalise breaches
The point for you. Financial penalties can run up to ₹250 crore for failing to safeguard data. Even setting the law aside, feeding a beneficiary’s details to a public tool breaks the trust your work depends on.

Risk 4
Confidentiality: not just personal data
Privacy is about people; confidentiality is about information. Much of what an NGO holds is sensitive even when no individual is named.
  • Unpublished strategy — proposals, budgets, internal reviews
  • Partner & donor information — shared under an expectation of privacy
  • Security-sensitive detail — locations of shelters, activists or at-risk groups
  • Legal & HR matters — disputes, investigations, staff records
Assume it may be stored. Before pasting, ask: “Would I be comfortable if this text left our organisation?” If not, do not paste it.

Risk 5
Over-reliance and deskilling
The subtlest risk is not a wrong answer — it is depending on the tool so much that your own judgement fades, or your team stops thinking critically.
Warning signs
Forwarding drafts unread; accepting the first answer; junior staff who no longer learn to write or analyse for themselves; “the AI said so” used as evidence.
Stay sharp
Use it to accelerate work you understand, not to replace understanding. Keep writing and analysing without it too. Treat every output as a draft to improve.

The Policy
A one-page AI use policy
You do not need a legal document — you need five clear rules everyone can remember.
  • Protect data — no real personal or confidential data in public tools; anonymise first
  • Human in the loop — a person reviews and approves everything before it is used
  • Verify before use — check facts, numbers and citations against primary sources
  • Disclose assistance — be honest when AI helped produce a document
  • No decisions about people — never let a tool decide who gets a benefit, job or service
Name an owner. One person keeps the policy current, answers questions and reviews new tools before the team adopts them.

Avoid These
Common mistakes to avoid
  • Pasting beneficiary data into a public chatbot to “save time”
  • Copying output unchecked — especially statistics and citations
  • Hiding AI use from colleagues, partners or donors
  • Letting it judge people — ranking applicants or flagging “risky” beneficiaries
  • Assuming it knows your context — it does not know your programme, district or community
  • No named owner — so no one keeps the practice safe as tools change

Takeaways
Key takeaways
  • It predicts, it does not know — fluent output can be confidently wrong
  • Best for drafting and summarising — with a human expert on both ends
  • Prompt with role, context, task, format — then iterate and ask it to self-check
  • Verify everything — facts, numbers and citations, against real sources
  • Protect people’s data — anonymise first; honour the DPDP Act and the trust behind it
  • AI drafts, a human decides — and never about who gets a benefit or a service
Start small, stay honest. Use it on low-risk work, keep a person in the loop, and let it sharpen your judgement — not replace it.
