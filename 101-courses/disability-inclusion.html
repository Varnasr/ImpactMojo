What this course gives you
Persons with disabilities are the world’s largest minority — and among the most excluded from development programmes. This course equips South Asian development staff to design and deliver work that includes them, as a matter of rights, not charity.
  • See disability differently — as the interaction between an impairment and the barriers society builds.
  • Know the rules — the UN CRPD and India’s RPWD Act 2016, and what they require of programmes.
  • Act on the twin-track approach — mainstream inclusion into everything, and support disability-specific empowerment.
  • Count everyone — use the Washington Group Questions so your data does not leave people invisible.
One principle runs through it all: “Nothing about us without us.” Persons with disabilities lead the decisions that affect their lives.

Core Idea
Disability is an interaction, not a trait
A person using a wheelchair is not ‘disabled’ by their legs. They are disabled by the flight of stairs with no ramp, the bus with no space, the form no one will read aloud. Change the environment and the disability shrinks.

Disability (restricted participation)
Disability results from the interaction between persons with impairments and attitudinal and environmental barriers that hinder their full and effective participation on an equal basis with others.

The Treaty
The UN CRPD — the global rulebook
What it is
The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities — the first comprehensive human-rights treaty of the 21st century. It does not create new rights; it clarifies how existing rights apply to persons with disabilities.
Key dates
Adopted by the UN General Assembly on 13 December 2006; entered into force 3 May 2008. India ratified it in 2007 — which is why India rewrote its national law as the RPWD Act 2016.
Ratifying states must respect, protect and fulfil these rights — and report to the UN CRPD Committee on progress.
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series9www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoDisability Inclusion 101www.impactmojo.in
Principles
Eight principles that guide every action
  • Dignity & autonomy — respect for inherent worth and the freedom to make one’s own choices.
  • Non-discrimination — and full, effective participation in society.
  • Respect for difference — disability as part of human diversity.
  • Equality of opportunity and accessibility.
  • Equality between men and women.
  • Respect for the evolving capacities of children with disabilities and their right to identity.
Denying a reasonable accommodation is itself a form of discrimination under the Convention (Article 2).

The Ethos
Nothing about us without us
Nothing about us without us.
— The disability rights movement; the guiding call of the CRPD negotiations
Persons with disabilities and their representative organisations drafted the CRPD alongside governments — an unprecedented negotiation. The principle now binds implementation (Article 4.3): states must closely consult and actively involve persons with disabilities, through their organisations, in decisions that affect them.
For your programme: no needs assessment, design, or evaluation about persons with disabilities without persons with disabilities at the table — not merely consulted at the end.

The Law
India’s RPWD Act 2016
What changed
The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 replaced the 1995 Act and brought Indian law in line with the CRPD. It came into force on 19 April 2017.
What it guarantees
Non-discrimination, equal recognition before the law, accessibility duties, reservation in jobs and education, free education for children with benchmark disabilities aged 6–18, and grievance mechanisms.
Companion laws exist across the region — e.g. Nepal’s Act Relating to Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2017), Pakistan’s ICT Rights of Persons with Disability Act (2020), and Bangladesh’s Rights and Protection of Persons with Disabilities Act (2013).

The Law
21 disabilities — and the ‘benchmark’ threshold
The RPWD Act specifies 21 categories of disability, a broader list than the old law. It spans physical, sensory, intellectual, psychosocial, neurological and blood disorders.
  • Physical & sensory — blindness, low vision, deaf, hard of hearing, locomotor disability, leprosy-cured, dwarfism, muscular dystrophy, cerebral palsy, acid-attack survivors.
  • Intellectual & developmental — intellectual disability, specific learning disabilities, autism spectrum disorder, speech & language disability.
  • Neurological & psychosocial — mental illness, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, chronic neurological conditions.
  • Blood disorders — thalassaemia, haemophilia, sickle-cell disease; plus multiple disabilities.
‘Benchmark disability’ = certified at 40% or more of a specified disability. Reservations and some entitlements attach to this threshold.

The Method
The twin-track approach
Track 1 — Mainstream
Make every programme accessible and inclusive: the school, the clinic, the loan scheme, the water point. Inclusion is the default, not a separate project.
Track 2 — Disability-specific
Targeted support that closes the gap: assistive devices, sign-language interpretation, self-help groups of persons with disabilities, OPD strengthening, skills bridging.
You need both. Mainstreaming alone leaves the furthest-behind out; specific projects alone create parallel silos. The most-cited approach across UN and NGO practice runs both tracks together.

Barriers
Four kinds of barriers — most are ours to remove
BarrierLooks like
AttitudinalPity, low expectations, shame, believing a person ‘can’t’; families hiding a child
Environmental / physicalSteps and no ramp, narrow doors, inaccessible toilets, no tactile paving, print-only notices
Institutional / policyRules, forms and eligibility criteria that exclude; budgets with no line for access
CommunicationNo sign language, no captions, no Braille or Easy Read, jargon-heavy materials
Attitudinal barriers are often the deepest — they shape the other three. Fix the assumption (“they can’t participate”) and the ramp, the form and the interpreter follow.

Barriers
Attitudes do the most damage
Low expectations become self-fulfilling: a child kept home ‘for safety’ never learns; a jobseeker assumed unproductive is never interviewed. Practitioners carry these assumptions too.
Reframe
From “What’s wrong with this person?” to “What in our programme is blocking them?”
The first question searches the person for a deficit. The second searches your design for a fixable barrier — and it usually finds one.
Contact changes minds. Persons with disabilities working as staff, trainers and leaders — not just beneficiaries — shifts attitudes faster than any awareness poster.

Intersectionality
Disability rarely travels alone
Disability compounds with gender, poverty, caste, religion, age and location. A Dalit woman with a disability in a remote village faces stacked, interacting barriers — not one disadvantage but several reinforcing each other.
  • Poverty — disability and poverty drive each other: extra costs of living, lost household income, out-of-pocket health spending.
  • Caste & ethnicity — discrimination layers onto disability, shrinking access to services and land.
  • Location — rural and remote areas have the fewest accessible services and assistive devices.
Design for the intersection. A ‘disability’ lens that ignores gender and caste will still miss the most excluded.

  • Reproductive rights denied — from forced sterilisation to exclusion from maternal health services.
The CRPD (Article 6) and RPWD Act both single out women and girls for specific measures — because a gender-blind disability programme reproduces the gap.

Accessible & inclusive programming
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series21www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoDisability Inclusion 101www.impactmojo.in
Design
Universal design — build it in, not on
Universal design
Designing products, environments, programmes and services to be usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, without the need for adaptation — CRPD, Article 2.
  • Ramp + steps at every entrance helps wheelchair users, older people, porters and parents with prams.
  • Captioned videos help Deaf viewers — and anyone in a noisy or silent room.
  • Plain-language forms help people with intellectual disabilities — and everyone else.
Cheaper up front. Designing access into a new building, form or app from day one costs a fraction of retrofitting it later.
CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · ImpactMojo 101 Series22www.impactmojo.in
ImpactMojoDisability Inclusion 101www.impactmojo.in
Design
Reasonable accommodation — the individual adjustment
Where universal design sets the accessible default, reasonable accommodation is the specific change one person needs to take part on an equal basis — provided unless it is a genuine, disproportionate burden.
Universal design
Systemic & upfront — benefits everyone (a ramp, captions, a screen-reader-friendly site)
Reasonable accommodation
Individual & on-request — a sign interpreter, flexible hours, a ground-floor desk, extra exam time
Under the CRPD and RPWD Act, refusing a reasonable accommodation is discrimination — not a favour withheld.

Communication
Make information reach everyone
  • Sign language — interpreters at events; recognise regional sign languages (e.g. Indian Sign Language).
  • Easy Read & plain language — short sentences, one idea per line, supporting pictures for people with intellectual disabilities and low literacy.
  • Captions & transcripts for all video and audio.
  • Braille, large print & audio versions of key documents; screen-reader-friendly digital files (real text, tagged PDFs, alt text).
  • Multiple formats by default — never rely on a single channel to reach the whole community.
Test with users. Ask persons with disabilities to check your materials — the fastest way to find what doesn’t work.

In Practice
Disaster & humanitarian response
In disasters, persons with disabilities are disproportionately left behind — warnings they can’t hear, shelters they can’t enter, evacuation that forgets them. Inclusion is life-or-death, and it is a standard, not an add-on.
  • Accessible early warning — audio, visual and SMS; reach people who are Deaf, blind or isolated.
  • Accessible shelters, WASH and distribution — ramps, priority queues, replacing lost assistive devices and medication.
  • Identify & involve persons with disabilities and OPDs in preparedness and response planning.
Anchored in the IASC Guidelines on Inclusion of Persons with Disabilities in Humanitarian Action (2019) and the Sendai Framework’s call for inclusive, all-of-society disaster risk reduction.

Data that counts everyone

The Problem
If you don’t count them, you can’t include them
Most routine programme data does not identify who has a disability — so persons with disabilities are invisible in enrolment, coverage and outcome figures. What is not measured is not managed, funded, or fixed.
  • Wrong questions — asking “Are you disabled?” triggers stigma and undercounts; many say no.
  • No disaggregation — totals hide whether persons with disabilities are being reached.
  • Missed from sampling — people in institutions or kept at home never enter the frame.
India’s recorded 2.21% versus the global ~16% is the clearest symptom: a data problem masquerading as a low prevalence.

The Tool
The Washington Group Short Set
Six short questions, about a minute to administer. They ask about functional difficulty — not whether someone ‘is disabled’ — across six universal domains, with a graded scale (no difficulty → some → a lot → cannot do at all).
Seeing
even wearing glasses
Hearing
even with a hearing aid
Walking
or climbing steps
Remembering
or concentrating
Self-care
washing, dressing
Communicating
being understood
Recommended by the UN for SDG disability disaggregation. Add the WG-SS to your baselines, MIS and surveys — it is free and open.

The Commitment
Disaggregate — and ‘leave no one behind’
… data disaggregated by… disability… in national contexts.
— SDG Target 17.18, on increasing availability of disaggregated data
The 2030 Agenda’s promise to leave no one behind is measurable only if data is broken down by disability. Persons with disabilities are named explicitly across the SDGs — in education, work, inequality, cities and data.
Practical rule: every indicator you report by sex and age, also report by disability status (using WG questions). A total is not enough.

Whose Data
Involve OPDs in the data itself
  • Design questions with OPDs — so tools are respectful, accurate and locally worded.
  • Recruit persons with disabilities as enumerators — builds trust and improves disclosure.
  • Share findings back in accessible formats — data is a tool for communities, not just donors.
  • Use the data to act — set targets, track reach, and adjust the programme.
“Nothing about us without us” applies to statistics too — people counted should help decide how, and why.

Partnership
Partner with OPDs — and know what they are
OPD — Organisation of Persons with Disabilities
An organisation led, directed and governed by persons with disabilities themselves (a majority of members and leadership). Distinct from an NGO working for them.
  • Partner early — involve OPDs in design, not just delivery; budget for their time and expertise.
  • Fund and strengthen — core support, capacity, and a real seat in governance.
  • Reach diversity — connect with women’s, Deaf, blind, intellectual- and psychosocial-disability groups, not one umbrella body alone.
The CRPD (Article 4.3) makes consulting OPDs an obligation — they are the primary experts on their own inclusion.

Workplace
Accessible recruitment & workplaces
  • Reach — advertise through OPDs and disability job portals; state that accommodations are available.
  • Accessible process — accessible application forms, venues and interviews; offer format choices; assess the job, not the impairment.
  • Onboard & accommodate — provide the adjustments people need; ask, don’t assume.
  • Retain & promote — accessible offices, inclusive culture, and paths to leadership — not entry-level ceilings.
Employing persons with disabilities is the strongest inclusion signal an organisation can send — internally and to the communities it serves.

Money
Budgeting for inclusion — usually low-cost
The most persistent myth is that inclusion is expensive. Most accommodations cost little or nothing; the largest returns come from designing access in from the start rather than retrofitting.
Zero–low
cost of many accommodations: flexible hours, a moved desk, a plain-language form, seating priority
One line
add an accessibility & accommodation line to every project budget
Design-in
accessible-by-default is far cheaper than retrofitting later
Budget for interpreters, accessible venues, transport support, assistive devices and accessible materials — small sums that unlock participation.

Events
Accessible meetings & events — a checklist
  • Venue — step-free access, accessible toilet, room to move; ask access needs on the invite.
  • Communication — sign-language interpreters, captions, a microphone always used, materials in advance and in accessible formats.
  • Facilitation — describe visuals aloud, plain language, breaks, no reliance on colour or small print.
  • Logistics — accessible transport, quiet space, and support for a personal assistant or guide to attend.
Ask, then provide. A single question — “What do you need to take part fully?” — on every registration form does most of the work.

Takeaways
Key takeaways
  • Disability is an interaction — impairment meeting barriers. Change the environment, not the person.
  • It is a rights issue — the CRPD (2006) and India’s RPWD Act 2016 make inclusion a legal duty, not charity.
  • Run both tracks — mainstream inclusion into everything and support disability-specific empowerment.
  • Remove the four barriers — attitudinal, physical, institutional and communication — and design for intersections of gender, poverty and caste.
  • Count everyone — use the Washington Group Questions and disaggregate, so no one is left behind.
  • Nothing about us without us — partner with OPDs and employ persons with disabilities in every stage.
Start now, small. Add one WG question set, one budget line, one accessible-event checklist, and one OPD partnership — then build from there.
