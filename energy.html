What this shows — and what it can't

This explorer sits on the supply and resource side of India's energy account: what each state could produce from renewables, what it has installed, and its coal reserves. Read together they tell the transition story — some states are rich in potential but have built little; some have almost no fossil base and are going straight to solar and wind. What this report does not break down by state is the demand side — electricity consumption, per-capita use and village electrification are published as national figures only, so you won't find "per-capita power by state" here. The national headline numbers (like the jump from 748 to 1,106 kWh per person) are shown as India-wide figures, honestly labelled.

Making sense of the numbers

What the measures mean. Renewable-energy potential is the maximum capacity a state could install across wind, solar, small hydro, biomass and bagasse cogeneration (plus large hydro), as estimated by MNRE — a ceiling, not a plan. Installed renewable capacity is what is actually connected to the grid today (solar, wind, bio-power, small hydro, waste-to-energy). Coal reserves are the geological estimate of coal in the ground (proved + indicated + inferred), not what is being mined. Comparing the first two shows how much of a state's clean-energy headroom has been used; the third shows the fossil base the transition has to move away from.

Reading it responsibly. A big-potential state that has built little (Rajasthan, Maharashtra) is not "behind" in any moral sense — potential reflects land, wind and sun, while build-out reflects investment, grid access and policy. Coal-rich states (Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh) face a genuinely harder transition because coal is also jobs and state revenue. Read a state against the national total, and read the three measures together rather than any one alone.

The national shift to watch. India's total installed power capacity rose from about 2.9 lakh MW (31.03.2014) to 5.21 lakh MW (31.03.2024), and per-capita electricity use from 748 to 1,106 kWh — the transition is happening on top of a system that is still growing fast, which is why coal production also kept climbing (to a provisional 997.83 MT in 2023-24). Growth and greening are happening at once.
