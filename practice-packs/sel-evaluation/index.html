SEL Evaluation: Design & Instruments

A 3-hour Practice Pack that takes you from an SEL programme you care about to a defensible, drafted evaluation design — covering the design choice, instrument selection, data collection plan, and reporting outline.

📚 4 modules ⏱ ~3 hours 🎯 Practitioner level 🇮🇳 India-context
Your Capstone

What you'll have when you finish

A one-page SEL Evaluation Design Brief for your real programme — a clear research question, sample frame, selected instruments with justification, data collection plan and budget sketch, mixed-methods integration approach, and a one-page reporting outline. Defensible to a funder. Achievable on a real budget.

Module 1 · ~25 min

What kind of evaluation do you actually need?

The number-one reason SEL evaluations disappoint is that the design choice happens implicitly. The team picks the method they've used before. The funder asks for "impact." Someone says "mixed-methods" without specifying what is mixed with what. Six months later, the data answers a question nobody quite asked.

Before any instrument selection, before any sampling, walk through three dimensions explicitly.

Dimension 1 — What is the question, really?

Three options, each implying a different design:

  • Outcome evaluation — "Did it work? What was the effect?" Needs a comparison group and measurable outcomes. Tells you whether to scale.
  • Process tracing — "Why did it happen? What was the mechanism?" Needs detailed, time-ordered evidence. Tells you how to redesign.
  • Theory-based evaluation — combines both. Tests the causal links inside a theory of change. Most demanding; most informative.

For SEL specifically, the most common mismatch is wanting to know why the programme works (a process question) but writing the ToR for an outcome evaluation. The resulting effect-size estimate cannot tell you why.

Dimension 2 — What evidence will be persuasive?

  • Quantitative — for representativeness, scale, effect sizes. Needs adequate sample. For SEL: hard because most validated quant instruments are CASEL-aligned and US-developed.
  • Qualitative — for depth, mechanism, lived experience. Needs careful case selection. For SEL: especially powerful with children and teachers.
  • Mixed-methods — most common for SEL because both kinds of evidence are usually needed. Consistently under-budgeted: clients pay for the quant strand and the qual strand but not for the integration time.

Dimension 3 — Snapshot or change?

  • Cross-sectional — one point in time. Cannot support change claims.
  • Pre-post — minimum credible change design. Needs baseline before the intervention.
  • Longitudinal — same units across multiple rounds. Strongest for causality, hardest operationally.
  • Retrospective — relies on recall. Cheaper, weaker, use only when no alternative.

SEL effects are real but small in any single year (CASEL Durlak meta-analysis ~11 percentile points, cumulative). A one-year cross-sectional study is structurally tilted toward null findings.

Worked example

NGO: Sangati programme runs in 40 Mumbai government schools, three years in. Funder wants evidence for renewal. Budget: ₹15 lakh, 9 months.

Dimension 1: Outcome ("did it improve SEL competencies?") + process ("which programme features explain success?"). Pick a primary — outcome — and a secondary — process.

Dimension 2: Mixed-methods. Quantitative competency measurement primary; qualitative case studies secondary. Budget 30% for integration time, not just the two strands.

Dimension 3: Quasi-experimental matched comparison. No baseline data — but matched control schools provide a counterfactual. Honest about the limits this places on causal claims.

Exercise 1 (15 min)

For your real SEL programme, write one sentence per dimension. If you cannot write a clean sentence, you do not yet have a defensible design — keep iterating.

  2. The evidence will be primarily ____________ (quant / qual / mixed) with the rationale that ____________
  3. The time dimension is ____________ (cross-sectional / pre-post / longitudinal / retrospective) because ____________

Module 2 · ~30 min

Choosing your measurement approach

Instrument selection is where most SEL evaluations quietly go wrong. Three persistent failure modes:

  1. Imported instruments used without cultural adaptation — Indian children interpret items differently than the US/UK populations the instruments were validated on.
  2. Programme-developed instruments that measure exposure to the programme rather than the underlying competency.
  3. Self-report scales used as the sole measure with younger children, where social-desirability bias dominates.

The good news: the field has matured. There are now real options across frameworks.

Available instruments by framework

FrameworkSample instrumentsIndia status
CASELSECA (Social-Emotional Composite Assessment), DESSA, SSIS-SELTranslated; partial India validation (Tandon 2020); item-level cultural-validity concerns persist
SEE LearningEmbedded reflection rubrics; observational checklists from the Dalai Lama Centre toolkitUsed in Indian pilot schools; not yet rigorously validated
WHO Life Skills / NCERT AEPNCERT AEP self-rating; SCERT well-being modules; ACER India SEL skills assessmentMost aligned with government schools. ACER India scales are the strongest validated option
Direct assessmentHead-Toes-Knees-Shoulders (self-regulation); Dimensional Change Card SortImplementable; bypasses self-report bias; narrower constructs
Behavioural observationCLASS (Classroom Assessment Scoring System); structured peer-interaction logsHighest validity; highest cost; needs trained observers
Adult reportTeacher rating scales; parent report (PHQ-A adapted; SDQ India)Operationally easy; rater-bias concerns; useful in mixed-methods designs

How to choose

  • Government-school context? Start with NCERT AEP and ACER India scales — they fit the curricular ground the schools already work on.
  • Younger children (Grade 5 or below)? De-emphasise self-report. Prioritise behavioural observation or adult report.
  • Budget under ₹10L? One quant instrument + qualitative case studies. Skip behavioural observation; it breaks the budget.
  • Multi-stakeholder triangulation desired? Pair child self-report with teacher report and brief parent report.
Worked example

Sangati's Mumbai eval (Class 4–6, government schools, ₹15L budget): primary instrument = ACER India SEL skills assessment (200 children, pre-post). Secondary = teacher report on classroom climate (CLASS subscales, 40 classrooms). Qualitative = 30 child interviews + 20 teacher interviews. Behavioural observation deliberately skipped — would have added ₹6L the budget cannot absorb.

Note on instrument adaptation

Indian cultural validation is not the same as translation. Translation handles language; adaptation handles whether the underlying construct (e.g., "self-regulation") means the same thing in a Mumbai government school as in a Boston suburb. ACER India and Tandon et al. (2020) document specific items that do not transfer well. Budget at least one cognitive-pretesting round per instrument.

Exercise 2 (20 min)

Select 2–3 instruments and justify

  1. From the table above, pick a primary quant instrument and a secondary triangulation source.
  2. For each, write one sentence on why it fits your context (school type, child age, budget).
  3. For each, write one sentence on its limits (what it under-measures, what bias it carries).

Module 3 · ~30 min

Designing the data collection (without breaking the budget)

Three operational realities define what Indian SEL data collection looks like:

  1. School-year calendar — June–April is workable; May, October (festivals), and exam weeks are not. You have roughly 5–6 months of clean fieldwork per academic year.
  2. Consent architecture — School management committee (SMC) approval, parent informed consent, child assent. All three. Plan a 6-week consent window minimum.
  3. Field team composition — for child-facing SEL data collection, you need women enumerators, ideally local-language native, trained in child-rapport protocols. This is a real constraint, not a checkbox.

Sample size — without illusions

The "how many" question is rarely as scientific as the power calculation suggests. For SEL specifically:

  • Single-arm pre-post: 200–300 children minimum to detect moderate effect (~0.3 SD). Below 100, power is too weak to interpret.
  • Two-arm quasi-experimental: 400–600 children minimum across treatment and control. Cluster effects (children within schools) reduce effective sample by 30–50%.
  • RCT at the school level: ~12+ schools per arm for credible inference. Below this, group-level comparisons are unstable.
  • Qualitative depth: 25–40 interviews + 4–8 FGDs typically reaches thematic saturation for a single-context SEL question. More is not better; more often dilutes.

Cost realities (₹ per data point, 2026)

  • Survey administration (in-school, supervised, ~40 min): ₹350–₹600 per child including enumerator time, supervision, transit, materials.
  • Qualitative interview (45–60 min, with transcription): ₹2,500–₹4,500 each (₹800/day enumerator + ₹60/min transcription + supervisor time).
  • Focus group (90 min, 6–8 participants, with transcription): ₹6,000–₹10,000 each.
  • Behavioural observation (1 classroom, ~3 hours, 2 trained observers, inter-rater reliability): ₹4,000–₹7,000 per observation session.
  • Teacher/parent report instrument (mail/digital, follow-up): ₹150–₹300 per respondent at scale.
Worked example

₹15L Sangati evaluation: 200 children × ACER quant (₹500 × 2 rounds = ₹2L), 30 child interviews (₹3,000 each = ₹90K), 20 teacher interviews (₹4,000 = ₹80K), 6 FGDs (₹8,000 = ₹48K), CLASS observations on 20 classrooms × 1 round (₹5,000 = ₹1L), instrument adaptation + pilot (₹1.5L), team training (₹1L), project management 15% (~₹2.3L), travel/logistics (₹1.5L), analysis + reporting (₹3L), contingency 8% (₹1.2L). Total ≈ ₹15L.

The integration line item

Mixed-methods studies in India routinely under-budget the work of integrating quant and qual findings into a coherent narrative. Budget at minimum 10–15 days of senior researcher time post-fieldwork specifically for integration meetings, joint coding, and contradictory-finding resolution. Without it, you produce two parallel reports instead of one integrated study.

Exercise 3 (20 min)

Sketch your data collection plan against your budget

  1. Take your instrument selection from Module 2. Multiply each by realistic per-unit cost.
  2. Add adaptation, training, travel, PM (10–15%), analysis (~20%), reporting, contingency (8%).
  3. Compare the total to your actual budget. If over: cut sample size, drop a method, or descope.
  4. Identify your one biggest risk to fieldwork (timing, consent, team, season).

Module 4 · ~25 min

Analysis, reporting, and the honest framing

The reporting stage is where SEL evaluations earn or lose trust. Two patterns recur:

  1. Over-claim from underpowered data — small effect, marginal significance, headline as if causal. Funders who've seen this once stop reading the next report.
  2. Under-claim from mixed findings — the report buries the real signal because the team is afraid to commit. Programme teams stop fighting for further funding.

The honest middle is harder and more valuable.

What to expect from SEL analysis

  • Effect sizes will be small. Kraft (2020) calibrates the "typical" effect size for cost-effective school interventions at 0.05–0.20 SD — not the 0.30+ that marketing language suggests. SEL effects sit in this range.
  • Statistical significance is not the same as meaningful change. With 500 children, very small effects become "significant" without being interpretively important.
  • Subgroup analyses are exploratory. If you did not pre-register them, treat as hypothesis-generating, not confirming.
  • Mixed findings are the rule. One competency improves, another doesn't. One school sub-group benefits, another doesn't. Report all of it.

The integration discipline

Mixed-methods studies typically run quant and qual analyses in parallel. The genuine work is at integration:

  • Where do the two strands agree? That's your strongest finding.
  • Where do they disagree? That's the most interesting question — neither strand alone could surface it.
  • Where does one strand fill a hole the other strand opens? Effect estimates without mechanism explanations need the qual to make them usable.

Reporting that funders read

The structure that works:

  1. 1-page executive summary — what we asked, what we found (with honest framing), what we recommend.
  2. 2-page methods note — design, sample, instruments, key limitations.
  3. 3–5 page findings section — organised by research question, not by data type.
  4. 2-page implications — what should the programme do; what should be evaluated next.
  5. Appendices — full results, instruments, sample characteristics, qualitative quotes.

Total: 15–25 pages of main report + appendices. Anything longer goes unread.

The honesty test

Before submitting your report, run the honesty test: hand it to a colleague unfamiliar with the programme and ask them to summarise the headline finding in one sentence. If their sentence overstates the evidence, your framing was too strong. If they cannot find the signal at all, your framing was too weak. Iterate.

Worked example — Sangati exec summary

What we asked: Does the Sangati SEL programme improve social-emotional competencies, classroom climate, and child-reported wellbeing in Mumbai Grade 4–6 government schools?

What we found: Modest positive effects on self-awareness (ACER scales, +0.18 SD, p=0.04) and classroom climate (CLASS subscales, teacher-rated, +0.22 SD, p=0.01). No detectable effect on self-management or peer relationships in this period. Qualitative interviews surfaced a consistent theme of programme fatigue in higher-implementation schools, suggesting the dose-response relationship is non-linear.

What we recommend: Continue Sangati with a revised implementation cadence (4 sessions/week, not 5) and an explicit teacher-load review. Re-evaluate at 24 months with longitudinal design to capture the slower-emerging competencies. Do not scale further without addressing the fatigue finding.

  2. Write three "what we might find" sentences — your range from strongest plausible finding to weakest. Be honest.
  3. Write a "what we recommend" structure — programme implications + next-evaluation suggestions.

Capstone · 30–60 min

Your SEL Evaluation Design Brief

You now have outputs from all four modules. Pull them together into a single one-page brief you can share with your team, funder, or evaluation agency.

The deliverable

A 1–2 page SEL Evaluation Design Brief covering:

  1. Programme + context (3 lines)
  2. Research question (one sentence)
  3. Design choice across all three dimensions
  4. Primary + secondary instruments with adaptation plan
  5. Data collection plan + total budget
  6. Analysis approach + integration strategy
  7. Reporting outline + audience map
  8. Top three limitations
  9. One honesty-test sentence
