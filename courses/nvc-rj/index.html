Nonviolence in Practice

Communication, Resistance & Repair — NVC, NVR and Restorative Justice

Gandhi gave the twentieth century a philosophy of nonviolence. This course teaches the working methods that grew from it — three canonical traditions that turn ahimsa into concrete skill. Nonviolent Communication (Marshall Rosenberg) for the interpersonal moment; Non-Violent Resistance / New Authority (Haim Omer) for holding a firm, non-escalating line in families, care work and institutions; and Restorative Justice (Howard Zehr and the circle traditions) for repairing harm in communities and systems. We teach each canon in full, trace its indigenous and South Asian roots, take its feminist and decolonial critiques seriously, and end in fieldcraft. Sits alongside our Gandhi and Social-Emotional Learning flagships.

How to Read This Course

Three traditions, one commitment: conflict without domination.

Nonviolence is often taught as either high philosophy (Gandhi, King) or as a slogan. This course sits in between — in the craft. Each of the three traditions answers a different practical question, and each operates at a different scale. Read in order, they form a ladder from the interpersonal to the institutional.

  • Nonviolent Communication (NVC) — Marshall Rosenberg, 1960s–70s. The interpersonal unit. How to speak and listen so that a hard conversation stays connected: observations, feelings, needs, requests. Answers: how do I stay in dialogue when I'm angry or afraid?
  • Non-Violent Resistance / New Authority (NVR) — Haim Omer, 1990s–2000s. The authority relationship. How a parent, teacher, carer or manager can firmly resist harmful behaviour without escalating into force or capitulating into helplessness. Answers: how do I hold a line without domination?
  • Restorative Justice (RJ) — Howard Zehr and the circle traditions, 1970s onward (with far older indigenous roots). The community and institutional layer. How a group repairs the harm of wrongdoing by centring those harmed, obligations, and re-integration rather than punishment. Answers: what does accountability look like when the goal is repair, not retribution?

All three descend, directly or indirectly, from the Gandhian insight that means and ends are inseparable — that you cannot build a peaceful outcome through coercive means. But they are practitioner methods, not creeds: this course teaches them at canonical depth and names what each cannot do. Module 9 gives the critiques (power asymmetry, gender-based violence, co-optation) their due; Module 10 is about knowing which tool the situation actually calls for.

Part I · Foundations

The Ground the Three Traditions Stand On

Module 01

The Practice of Nonviolence

Nonviolence is not the absence of conflict. It is a disciplined way of engaging conflict that refuses to dominate, humiliate, or destroy the other party — while still refusing to submit to harm. This module sets the shared foundations that Nonviolent Communication, Non-Violent Resistance, and Restorative Justice all build on.

From ahimsa to method

The Sanskrit ahimsa — literally "non-harm" — is older than Gandhi, running through Jain, Buddhist and Hindu ethical traditions. Gandhi's contribution was to make it active and political: satyagraha ("holding firmly to truth") is not passive non-resistance but a militant, disciplined confrontation with injustice that accepts suffering rather than inflicting it. The three traditions in this course are, in effect, satyagraha scaled down — from the mass movement to the conversation, the household, and the community circle.

Nonviolence is not a garment to be put on and off at will. Its seat is in the heart, and it must be an inseparable part of our very being. M.K. Gandhi, Harijan, 1936

The unifying claim, common to all three, is the Gandhian axiom that means and ends are inseparable: a just outcome cannot be reached through unjust means, because the means become the emerging end. You cannot punish people into genuine accountability, shout people into being heard, or coerce a child into trusting you.

Three levels, three questions

Each tradition operates at a different scale of social life and answers a different practical question. Holding them together is what makes a rounded nonviolent practitioner.

TraditionScaleCore questionSignature move
NVC (Rosenberg)The interpersonal momentHow do I stay connected when I'm angry or afraid?Observation → feeling → need → request
NVR (Omer)The authority relationshipHow do I resist harm firmly without domination?Presence + self-control + supporters
Restorative Justice (Zehr)Community & institutionsWhat does accountability look like as repair, not punishment?Centre the harmed; name obligations; re-integrate
Orientation

Nonviolence is often confused with niceness. It is not. All three traditions are compatible with firmness, anger, and the refusal to comply with harm. What they rule out is the intent to injure — and the fantasy that you can build peace by winning.

Core readings

  • M.K. Gandhi — Non-Violent Resistance (Satyagraha) (selected writings)
  • Gene Sharp — The Politics of Nonviolent Action (1973), Vol. 1
  • Judith Butler — The Force of Non-Violence (2020)
  • ImpactMojo flagship — Gandhi's Political Thought
Part II · Nonviolent Communication

Marshall Rosenberg's Language of Life

Module 02

NVC Foundations

Marshall Rosenberg (1934–2015), a clinical psychologist who trained under Carl Rogers and worked in school desegregation in the 1960s American South, distilled a model of communication he called Nonviolent Communication (NVC), also "Compassionate Communication." Its wager: most conflict escalates not because needs clash, but because we express them as judgments.

Life-alienating communication

Rosenberg catalogued the habits of speech that "block compassion" and predictably trigger defence and counter-attack:

  • Moralistic judgments — implying wrongness or badness in those who don't act in harmony with our values ("she's lazy", "you're inconsiderate"). Distinct from value judgments about what we need and care about.
  • Making comparisons — a form of judgment that guarantees misery.
  • Denial of responsibility — language that obscures agency: "I had to", "you make me feel", "company policy", "orders."
  • Communicating desires as demands — where non-compliance is expected to bring blame or punishment.

Underneath, Rosenberg drew on a stark diagnosis (borrowed from Walter Wink and O.J. Harvey): a "domination culture" trains us in the language of who deserves what — the language of deserving, of good and bad people — which makes punishment and reward feel natural and violence righteous.

Needs, not strategies

The pivot of NVC is the distinction between needs — universal human requirements for wellbeing (safety, respect, autonomy, connection, rest, meaning) — and strategies, the specific things we do to meet them. Conflict lives at the level of strategies; connection is possible at the level of needs, because needs are shared.

Two questions
Rosenberg framed the whole practice as helping two people answer: (1) What is alive in us? and (2) What would make life more wonderful? Everything technical serves those two questions.

Giraffe & Jackal. In his trainings Rosenberg used hand-puppets: the jackal (the language of judgment, close to the ground) and the giraffe (the land animal with the largest heart, and a long neck for perspective). They are teaching devices, not personality types — everyone speaks both.

Core readings

  • Marshall Rosenberg — Nonviolent Communication: A Language of Life (3rd ed., 2015)
  • Marshall Rosenberg — Speak Peace in a World of Conflict (2005)
  • Oren Jay Sofer — Say What You Mean (2018) — NVC + mindfulness
Module 03

The Four Components

The visible technique of NVC is a four-part form — Observation, Feeling, Need, Request (OFNR). It is a scaffold, not a script: the aim is the consciousness behind it, not the phrasing.

OFNR, precisely

ComponentIsIs not (the common error)
ObservationWhat a camera would record — specific, time- and context-bound factsEvaluation ("you're always late") — mixing observation with judgment invites the other to hear criticism
FeelingAn emotion or body sensation (anxious, relieved, tired)A thought disguised as a feeling: "I feel ignored / manipulated / let down" — these are interpretations of what others did
NeedThe universal need alive under the feeling (to be heard, for reliability, for rest)A strategy aimed at one person ("I need you to call me") — that belongs in the request
RequestA present, positive, concrete, doable action, offered as genuinely open to "no"A demand — where a "no" will be met with blame, guilt, or punishment
Observing without evaluating is the highest form of human intelligence. J. Krishnamurti, quoted approvingly by Rosenberg

Request or demand? The test is in the response to "no"

The same sentence — "Would you be willing to take the bins out tonight?" — is a request or a demand depending entirely on what happens when the answer is no. If the "no" is treated as information about the other's needs (and you look for a strategy that works for both), it was a request. If the "no" brings coldness, guilt-tripping or retaliation, it was a demand wearing polite clothing. NVC insists that we cannot make anyone do anything; we can only create the quality of connection in which people give willingly.

Worked example

Jackal: "You never listen to me in these meetings."
Giraffe (OFNR): "In the last two meetings, when I finished a point you moved straight to the agenda (observation). I felt discouraged (feeling), because I really want my contributions to land (need). Would you be willing to tell me back what you heard before we move on? (request)"

Practise this

  • Rosenberg — NVC: A Language of Life, chs. 3–6 (one chapter per component)
  • CNVC "feelings & needs inventories" (Center for Nonviolent Communication)
Module 04

Empathy, Self-Empathy & the Uses of Force

OFNR is the expressive half of NVC. The receptive half — empathy — is where the real work lives, and where NVC makes its most demanding and most contested claims.

Empathy as presence, not technique

For Rosenberg, empathy is "a respectful understanding of what others are experiencing" — emptying our mind and listening with our whole being. It is not advising, reassuring, correcting, one-upping, or explaining. Empathic listening guesses at the other's feelings and needs ("Are you frustrated because you were hoping for more warning?") and stays until the person feels fully received — signalled by a release of tension or a natural falling-silent.

Self-empathy and the "cause of anger"

Before we can hear another, we often need to hear ourselves. NVC treats anger not as something to suppress or vent, but as an alarm: "at the core of all anger is a need that is not being fulfilled." Rosenberg's provocative move is that others' actions are the stimulus but never the cause of our anger — the cause is our own judgment (the "enemy image"). The practice: hear the judgment, translate it into the unmet need, and only then speak.

Protective vs punitive force

NVC is not pacifism-at-any-cost. Rosenberg distinguished:

  • The protective use of force — acting to prevent injury when there is no time for dialogue (restraining a child from running into traffic), with the sole intent to protect.
  • The punitive use of force — acting to make the other suffer for what they did, in the belief they "deserve" it. NVC holds that punishment reliably damages goodwill and never produces the internal motivation we actually want.

Two questions before any use of force: (1) What do I want this person to do? and (2) What do I want their reasons to be? Punishment can sometimes achieve the first; it almost always poisons the second.

Taking the critiques seriously

NVC is widely taught and widely criticised. A serious practitioner holds both:

  • The "robot" problem. Rote OFNR ("I hear that you feel… because you need…") can sound formulaic and manipulative. Rosenberg agreed: the form without the intention is "jackal in giraffe clothing."
  • The tone-policing / depoliticising critique. When those in power ask the marginalised to express anger "nonviolently," NVC can become a tool of control that reframes structural injustice as an interpersonal communication problem. The claim that others "never cause" our feelings can, misapplied, sound like victim-blaming.
  • The individualism critique. Focusing on needs and feelings can obscure the material and structural conditions that generate conflict.

Our stance: NVC is a superb tool for the interpersonal layer and a poor substitute for the analysis of power. Use it to humanise a conflict, not to de-politicise it. Module 9 returns to this.

Core readings

  • Rosenberg — NVC: A Language of Life, chs. 7–11 (empathy, anger, protective force)
  • Rosenberg — The Surprising Purpose of Anger (2005)
  • Critical: essays on NVC and power / tone-policing (see lexicon: "Faux-NVC")
Part III · Non-Violent Resistance

Haim Omer's New Authority

Module 05

NVR & New Authority

Where NVC works between rough equals in a conversation, Non-Violent Resistance (NVR) addresses the authority relationship — parent and child, teacher and pupil, carer and cared-for. Israeli psychologist Haim Omer (Tel Aviv University) took Gandhi's and the civil-rights movement's methods of nonviolent resistance and transposed them, deliberately and explicitly, into the family.

The problem: the collapse of authority

Traditional authority ran on hierarchy, distance and the threat of punishment. As that model rightly lost legitimacy, many parents and carers were left oscillating between two failures: authoritarian escalation (trying to reassert control through force, which escalates) and permissive capitulation (giving in to avoid conflict, which abandons the young person to their worst impulses). Omer's New Authority replaces control with presence and the anchoring function: the adult is not the one who wins, but the one who stays.

I am your parent. I cannot make you change, but I will resist your harmful behaviour with all my strength, and I will not give up on you — and I am not alone. The stance of New Authority, paraphrasing Omer

The two pillars: presence and self-control

  • Presence. "I am here, I am your parent, you cannot get rid of me." Not surveillance or control — persistent, warm, unyielding presence that communicates commitment.
  • Self-control (de-escalation). The single hardest discipline: the adult controls only their own behaviour, never the child's. Omer's maxim — "strike while the iron is cold" — inverts the reflex to respond in the heat of the moment. You resist later, deliberately, from a regulated state.

The escalation traps. Omer names two: mutual escalation (matching aggression with aggression) and complementary escalation (matching aggression with capitulation). Both teach the young person that escalation works. NVR is the third path: firm resistance without either.

The Gandhian transposition

Omer is unusually explicit that NVR is Gandhi and the American civil-rights movement brought home. From satyagraha he takes: resistance without aggression; the acceptance of the relationship even while refusing the behaviour; publicity over secrecy; the mobilisation of a wider community; and persistence as a form of strength. The child is never the "enemy" — the behaviour is resisted, the person is held.

Core readings

  • Haim Omer — Non-Violent Resistance: A New Approach to Violent and Self-Destructive Children (2004)
  • Haim Omer — The New Authority: Family, School, and Community (2010)
Module 06

NVR in Action

NVR is a toolkit of concrete, sequenced practices. Each mirrors a move from political nonviolent resistance, scaled to the household, classroom or care setting.

The core methods

MethodWhat it isPolitical parallel
The AnnouncementA short, calm, often written declaration naming the specific behaviour the adult will now resist — and that they will not use violence or give upThe public manifesto / statement of grievance
The Sit-inThe adult sits in the young person's space, present and calm, saying "we are here because we cannot accept X; we'd like your ideas," and waitsThe occupation / sit-in
Prioritising ("baskets")Choosing a small number of behaviours to resist now; deferring the rest — resistance is targeted, not totalStrategic choice of the point of pressure
Supporters & transparencyRecruiting family, friends, teachers into an open network; breaking the secrecy around the problemThe movement; public witness over secrecy
Reconciliation gesturesUnconditional acts of care and connection, decoupled from behaviour, to keep the relationship warmGandhi's insistence on friendship with the opponent

Vigilant care: three levels of attention

Omer's answer to "how much monitoring is too much" is vigilant care — care that adjusts its intensity to risk, rather than swinging between neglect and surveillance:

  • Open attention — the default: warm interest, open conversation.
  • Focused attention — when worry signs appear: asking more, checking in, coordinating with others.
  • Direct protection — when danger is clear: acting decisively to protect, without secrecy.

South Asian resonance. The NVR "network of supporters" maps naturally onto the extended family and neighbourhood structures of South Asian life — the khandaan, the mohalla, the grandparents, the teacher. Where individualistic parenting advice tells a lone parent to be consistent, NVR's insistence that "the adult is not alone" is closer to how care actually works here. The caution (Module 9): the same networks can enforce control and honour-based coercion — supporters must be recruited for presence and de-escalation, never for shaming.

Where NVR is used

  • Parenting — child violence, self-destructive behaviour, entitlement/tyrannical behaviour, and (via Omer & Lebowitz's SPACE programme) childhood anxiety and OCD, by reducing parental accommodation.
  • Schools — whole-school anti-bullying built on vigilant care and adult presence rather than zero-tolerance exclusion.
  • Residential & care settings — de-escalation cultures that reduce restraint.

Core readings

  • Omer — Non-Violent Resistance (2004), practice chapters
  • Eli Lebowitz & Haim Omer — work on SPACE (parent-based treatment of child anxiety)
  • Omer & others — NVR in schools ("vigilant care")
Part IV · Restorative Justice

Repair Instead of Retribution

Module 07

The Restorative Paradigm

Restorative Justice (RJ) asks a different question of wrongdoing than the criminal-legal system does. Howard Zehr's Changing Lenses (1990) crystallised the shift — from justice as deserved pain to justice as repair of harm.

Two lenses on the same act

Retributive lensRestorative lens
Crime is a violation of the law and the stateCrime is a violation of people and relationships
Violations create guiltViolations create obligations
Justice = the state determines blame and administers deserved painJustice = those affected search together to identify needs and put things right
Central: what laws were broken? who did it? what do they deserve?Central: who has been hurt? what do they need? whose obligation is it to meet those needs?
Crime is a violation of people and relationships. It creates obligations to make things right. Justice involves the victim, the offender, and the community in a search for solutions which promote repair, reconciliation, and reassurance. Howard Zehr, Changing Lenses (1990)

Zehr's three pillars

  • Harms and needs. Start with those who were harmed and what they need — safety, answers, acknowledgement, restitution, vindication — not with the rule that was broken.
  • Obligations. The person who caused harm has an obligation to understand and, so far as possible, repair it. Accountability here means facing the harm, not receiving punishment.
  • Engagement. Those with a stake — harmed, responsible, and community — are given meaningful roles in the process, rather than having it done to or for them by professionals.

Reintegrative shaming. Criminologist John Braithwaite (Crime, Shame and Reintegration, 1989) distinguishes stigmatising shame, which brands a person as bad and pushes them into criminal identities, from reintegrative shame, which disapproves of the act while affirming the person's worth and welcoming them back. RJ aims for the second.

Core readings

  • Howard Zehr — Changing Lenses (1990)
  • Howard Zehr — The Little Book of Restorative Justice (rev. 2015)
  • John Braithwaite — Crime, Shame and Reintegration (1989)
Module 08

Restorative Practices

The paradigm becomes practice through a family of structured encounters. They range from a thirty-second "affective statement" to a formal, scripted conference — a continuum, not a single method.

The core models

  • Victim–Offender Mediation / Dialogue (VOM). A prepared, facilitated meeting between the person harmed and the person responsible — the earliest Western RJ model (Kitchener, Ontario, 1974).
  • Family Group Conferencing (FGC). Originating in Aotearoa New Zealand's Children, Young Persons and Their Families Act 1989, shaped by Māori practice — the extended family (whānau) is convened, given private time to devise a plan, and central to the outcome.
  • Circles (peacemaking / talking circles / sentencing circles). Rooted in First Nations practice in Canada and the US; a keeper holds the space and a talking piece passes so each voice is heard in turn. Documented by Kay Pranis.
  • Restorative Conferences (the "Wagga Wagga" / scripted model). A trained facilitator follows a script built on affective questions ("What happened? Who's been affected? What's needed to make things right?").

The restorative continuum

Ted Wachtel and the IIRP frame practices as a continuum of increasing formality and participation — most restorative work is the informal end, done daily, not the dramatic conference:

Informal ←→ Formal
Affective statements ("I felt worried when…")Affective questionsSmall impromptu conference / circleFormal conference / FGC
Design principle

The heart of every restorative practice is the same: "with," not "to" or "for." High expectations and high support, together — not control (to), not permissiveness (for), and not neglect (neither).

Two settings that matter for practitioners

  • Schools. Restorative practices replace suspension-and-expulsion cultures with circles, restorative conversations and repair. Strong evidence for reduced exclusions and improved climate; weaker where bolted on without whole-school buy-in. Directly relevant to South Asian education programming and the anti-corporal-punishment agenda.
  • Criminal justice. Used as diversion (pre-charge), pre-sentence, in prisons, and at re-entry. Meta-analyses (e.g. Sherman & Strang) find RJ conferencing tends to reduce repeat offending and markedly improves victim satisfaction and post-traumatic recovery compared with court.

Core readings

  • Kay Pranis — The Little Book of Circle Processes (2005)
  • Belinda Hopkins — Just Schools (2004) — restorative approaches in education
  • Sherman & Strang — Restorative Justice: The Evidence (2007)
Module 09

Roots, Power & Critique

Restorative justice did not begin in the 1970s, and it is not automatically benign. This module traces its indigenous and South Asian lineages — and gives the serious critiques the weight they deserve.

Older than the movement

Contemporary RJ rediscovered, and sometimes appropriated, practices that indigenous and traditional societies never abandoned:

  • Māori whānau-centred process, which reshaped New Zealand law.
  • First Nations / Navajo peacemaking (Hózhǫ́ǫ́jí Naat'áanii) — restoring harmony (hózhǫ́) rather than assigning blame.
  • Ubuntu in Southern Africa — "a person is a person through other persons" — an ethic invoked (and contested) in the Truth and Reconciliation Commission.
  • South Asian traditions: the panchayat as a forum of community resolution; India's Gram Nyayalayas Act (2008) and Lok Adalats (settlement-oriented people's courts); traditions of sulh (reconciliation) and mediation in Muslim communities.

The khap panchayat problem — a required caution. "Community justice" is not restorative merely because it is communal. The khap panchayat, which has ordered social boycotts and endorsed "honour" killings to enforce caste and gender hierarchy, is the anti-model. Community process can reproduce domination as easily as repair. RJ's safeguards — voluntariness, centring the harmed, refusal of coercion, protection of the vulnerable — are exactly what distinguishes it from oppressive village "justice." Never romanticise the local.

The feminist critique: gender-based violence

The most important critique concerns intimate-partner and sexual violence. Feminist scholars (e.g. Kathleen Daly, Julie Stubbs) warn that a face-to-face "dialogue" between a survivor and an abuser can replicate the power imbalance, pressure survivors to reconcile, privatise a public wrong, and re-traumatise. Yet survivors are often failed by the criminal system too. The frontier is survivor-led design: RJ only where the survivor genuinely chooses it, with rigorous safeguards, skilled facilitation, and no pressure to forgive.

Co-optation, net-widening, and the abolitionist turn

  • Dilution / co-optation. Bolted onto punitive systems, RJ can become a soft add-on that changes nothing — or "net-widening," drawing more people (especially children) into formal control for minor matters.
  • Transformative Justice (TJ). An abolitionist current (generationFIVE, Mia Mingus, INCITE!, Mariame Kaba) argues that harm is produced by structures, and that genuine accountability must be built in communities outside the carceral state, addressing root causes rather than individual incidents. TJ is RJ's most demanding interlocutor.
Practitioner stance

Hold the tension: RJ is a real improvement on retribution and can be co-opted, romanticised, or unsafe. The test is never "is it a circle?" but "does this process centre those harmed, refuse coercion, and actually shift power and repair?"

Core readings

  • Fania Davis — The Little Book of Race and Restorative Justice (2019)
  • Danielle Sered — Until We Reckon (2019)
  • Kathleen Daly & Julie Stubbs — feminist engagements with RJ (papers)
  • generationFIVE — Toward Transformative Justice (2007)
Part V · Integration & Practice

From Canon to Craft

Module 10

Choosing & Combining the Practices

Three canons, one situation in front of you. The mark of skill is not mastery of any single method but knowing which the moment calls for — and when nonviolent method is not the answer.

A decision heuristic

If the situation is…Reach first for…
A charged conversation between rough equalsNVC — observation/feeling/need/request; empathy first
An adult needing to resist a young person's harmful behaviourNVR — presence, self-control, supporters, announcement
Harm has been done and a community must respondRestorative Justice — centre the harmed; circle/conference
The conflict is structural / about power and resourcesNone of the three alone — organise, advocate, change the conditions (see Gandhi flagship)

They also compose: an NVR supporters' meeting is run with NVC; a restorative circle depends on NVC-style empathic listening; NVC self-empathy is what keeps an NVR parent "striking while the iron is cold."

Three cross-cutting cautions

  • Power and safety first. Where there is ongoing abuse, a live threat, or a severe power imbalance, dialogue can endanger. Safety planning precedes any restorative or communicative process; sometimes the nonviolent act is to leave or to call for protection.
  • Trauma-informed practice. A traumatised nervous system cannot access OFNR or sit in a circle until it is regulated. Grounding, choice, and the option to withdraw are prerequisites, not niceties. (See the SEL flagship on regulation and vicarious trauma.)
  • The practitioner's own regulation. All three methods fail the instant the facilitator is dysregulated. Self-empathy and self-control are not preliminaries — they are the method.

Consent and voluntariness run through everything here. A coerced apology, a mandated circle, a scripted "request" that is really a demand — each turns a nonviolent form into a violent function. If participation is not genuinely free, it is not the practice.

Module 11

Fieldcraft & Facilitation

A practical, transferable sequence for convening a restorative or communicative process — whether you are mediating a team conflict, running a classroom circle, or coaching a parent through an NVR plan.

The facilitation arc

  1. Assess & screen. Is this appropriate? Check for safety, power imbalance, genuine willingness on all sides, and whether those harmed actually want a process. Screen out where dialogue would endanger.
  2. Prepare each party separately. The un-glamorous majority of the work. Meet people one-to-one first: hear them, explain the process, clarify it is voluntary, agree what they hope for. No surprises in the room.
  3. Set the container. Agree ground rules and roles; introduce the talking piece or the script; make the space physically and psychologically safe (seating, timing, an exit option).
  4. Surface what happened and who was affected. Use affective questions and empathic listening. Let those harmed speak to impact; let those responsible listen before they explain.
  5. Move to needs and obligations. What is needed to make things as right as possible? Who will do what? Keep agreements concrete, doable, and owned by the parties — not imposed.
  6. Close and follow up. Mark the transition; record agreements; schedule a check-in. Repair is a process, not an event — reconciliation gestures and follow-through are where it holds or fails.

Facilitator's self-check before you begin: Am I regulated? Am I neutral enough to hold every person's dignity, including the one who caused harm? Have I confused my need for a tidy resolution with their need for repair? Who is not in this room whose voice matters?

These are motor skills, not just ideas — they change only through rehearsal and feedback. Pair this course with role-play, and bring real (anonymised) cases to a facilitated group.

Capstone

Capstone Project

Turn the three canons into a defensible, real-world plan. Choose a live conflict or harm from your own practice (suitably anonymised) and work it through all three lenses.

Design a Nonviolent Response

1
Diagnose

Describe the conflict or harm. Map the parties, the needs beneath their positions, the power differences, and any safety risks. Decide honestly whether a nonviolent process is appropriate — and say why.

2
Apply the three lenses

Write an NVC reframe of the key exchange (OFNR + one empathic guess); sketch an NVR plan if an authority relationship is involved (announcement, self-control, two supporters, one reconciliation gesture); and design a restorative process (who is centred, what format, the three questions).

3
Critique your own plan

Name the risks: where could this replicate power, coerce, re-traumatise, or romanticise the community? What safeguards will you build? When would you not proceed?

4
Facilitation script

Produce a one-page facilitation script or run-of-show you could actually use, grounded in the Module 11 arc.

Deliverables

  • A 4–6 page case memo covering all four phases
  • One usable facilitation script or announcement letter
  • An explicit "when I would not do this" section
