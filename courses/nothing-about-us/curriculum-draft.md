# Nothing About Us Without Us: Disability, Justice & Development
## Flagship curriculum — DRAFT for review

**Status:** reviewed and published. The module bodies now live in Supabase (`course_content`, `course_id = nothing-about-us`, modules 1–9). Built from standard public frameworks (social model, CRPD, RPwD Act 2016, Washington Group, disability justice) — **not** from any third party's proprietary workshop material.

**Post-review revision (South Asia centring):** following review, the published modules were revised to **centre South Asian scholarship, activists, movements, laws and examples** — Anita Ghai, Renu Addlakha, Nandini Ghosh, Nidhi Goyal, Malini Chib, Javed Abidi and others, plus disability law across Bangladesh, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka — with the Western canon retained as *complement*, not primary authority. The outline below is the pre-revision draft; the live modules lead with South Asia throughout.

**Please review for:** accuracy, tone/voice, language choices (identity-first vs person-first), South Asia grounding, and anything that a reviewer with lived experience of disability would flag. This is deliberately reviewable *before* publication, in keeping with the course's own principle — nothing about us without us.

**Shape:** 9 modules. Module 1 is free/public; modules 2–9 sit behind a free sign-in (the standard flagship pattern). Each module ≈ 6–10 min read + a short auto-graded check. The course cross-links to **Disability Inclusion 101** (concepts) and the **Disability-Inclusive MEL Lab** (practice).

A note on language used in this draft: it uses **both** "disabled people" (identity-first, preferred by much of the rights/justice movement and the social model) and "persons with disabilities" (person-first, the CRPD/legal convention), and the course will name this choice explicitly in Module 1 rather than pretend there is one correct answer.

## Module 1 — Models & History: How We Think About Disability *(free/public)*

**Learning objectives**
- Distinguish the medical, social, biopsychosocial, human-rights and disability-justice models — and see how each shapes what we do.
- Trace how disability has been understood over time, including in South Asia.
- Make informed, respectful language choices.

**Content**
- **The models.** *Medical model* — disability as an individual deficit to be cured, rehabilitated or managed by professionals. *Social model* (Mike Oliver, UK) — impairment is a fact of a body; *disability* is what society does to it through barriers. *Biopsychosocial / ICF* (WHO, 2001) — an interaction of body, activity, participation and environment. *Human-rights model* (the CRPD) — disabled people as rights-holders, not objects of charity or medicine. *Disability justice* (Sins Invalid; Patty Berne, Mia Mingus, Stacey Milbern) — a movement *beyond* single-issue legal rights: intersectional, collective, anti-capitalist, centring the most marginalised (disabled people who are also Black, brown, queer, poor). Its ten principles include intersectionality, leadership of the most impacted, anti-capitalism, cross-movement solidarity, interdependence, collective access and collective liberation.
- **A short history.** From confinement and the asylum, through charity and pity, to the Independent Living movement (Ed Roberts and the Berkeley CIL, 1970s), the UK social model, deinstitutionalisation, and disability pride.
- **South Asia.** Traditional framings (karma, fate, charity, pity) and their double edge; colonial-era asylums and "lunacy"/leprosy laws; the post-independence *welfare* approach that treated disability as medical and charitable rather than political; disability in epics, film and everyday life.
- **Language.** Person-first ("person with a disability") vs identity-first ("disabled person") — and why both exist. Terms to retire ("handicapped", "the disabled", slurs, "wheelchair-bound"). The debate over "differently abled" and the Indian government's "*divyangjan*/divyang" ("divine-bodied") — widely criticised by activists as patronising and depoliticising. The principle: **ask people what they prefer.**

**Key readings**
- Mike Oliver, *Understanding Disability* / "The Social Model of Disability."
- Tom Shakespeare, *Disability Rights and Wrongs Revisited* (a critical take on the social model).
- Sins Invalid, *Skin, Tooth, and Bone: The Basis of Movement is Our People* (disability justice primer).
- Anita Ghai, *Rethinking Disability in India*.
- WHO, *International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)*, overview.

**Check (sample)**
1. In the social model, a Deaf student fails an oral-only exam. The "disability" is located in… → *the exam's inaccessible design, not the student.*
2. Which framing goes **beyond** single-issue legal rights to centre the most marginalised and collective liberation? → *Disability justice.*

## Module 2 — The Rights Architecture: CRPD, Law & Entitlements *(sign-in)*

**Learning objectives**
- Explain the CRPD's paradigm shift and its key articles.
- Map India's disability law and the main entitlements.
- Identify where rights break down in practice.

**Content**
- **The CRPD (2006; in force 2008; India ratified 2007).** The first human-rights treaty of the 21st century; shifted disability from welfare to rights. Key articles: Art 5 (equality & non-discrimination), Art 9 (accessibility), Art 12 (equal recognition before the law / legal capacity — the basis for *supported* over *substituted* decision-making), Art 19 (living independently, in the community), Art 24 (inclusive education), Art 27 (work & employment). Reasonable accommodation is defined in Art 2; denying it is discrimination.
- **India's framework.** The Constitution (equality; directive principles). The Persons with Disabilities Act, 1995 → replaced by the **Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act, 2016**, which recognises **21 disabilities**, provides **4% reservation** in government jobs and **5%** in higher education, mandates accessibility, and creates Chief/State Commissioners with penalties for non-compliance. Companion laws: the **National Trust Act, 1999** (autism, cerebral palsy, intellectual disability, multiple disabilities); the **Rehabilitation Council of India Act**; the **Mental Healthcare Act, 2017** (advance directives; effectively decriminalised attempted suicide).
- **Entitlements & schemes.** The **UDID** (Unique Disability ID) card; disability pensions (IGNDPS / state schemes); scholarships; the **ADIP** scheme for aids & appliances; tax benefits and travel concessions; reservation in jobs and education.
- **The implementation gap.** Certification as a gatekeeping barrier; accessibility mandates widely unmet; low awareness; weak enforcement. Rights on paper vs rights in practice.

**Key readings**
- UN CRPD — full text and the Committee's General Comments (esp. GC No. 2 on accessibility, GC No. 1 on legal capacity).
- The RPwD Act, 2016 and RPwD Rules, 2017.
- NCPEDP resources; Human Rights Watch reporting on disability rights in India.

**Check (sample)**
1. India's *domestic* law recognising 21 disabilities and mandating reservation is… → *the RPwD Act, 2016* (the CRPD is the UN treaty).
2. Denying a reasonable accommodation, under the CRPD, is… → *a form of discrimination.*

## Module 3 — Counting Disability & the Politics of Data *(sign-in)*

**Learning objectives**
- Explain why disability is hard — and political — to count.
- Use the Washington Group approach and know its cut-off.
- Read prevalence figures critically.

**Content**
- **Prevalence.** WHO/World Bank estimate ~**16% of the world's population** — about **1.3 billion people** — experience significant disability. India's **Census 2011** recorded just **2.21%**, and NSS 2018 a similar figure — both widely regarded as large undercounts driven by stigma, narrow questions and certification barriers.
- **How to count well.** "Do you have a disability?" undercounts. The **Washington Group Short Set** asks about *functional difficulty* across six domains (seeing, hearing, walking/mobility, cognition, self-care, communication) on a four-point scale, with the disability cut-off at "a lot of difficulty" or "cannot do at all" in ≥1 domain. Companions: the **Child Functioning Module** (with UNICEF) and the WHO **Model Disability Survey**. *Hands-on:* the [Disability-Inclusive MEL Lab](/Labs/disability-inclusive-mel-lab.html).
- **The politics of data.** What gets counted reflects who is in power to define it; medical certification as gatekeeping; the invisibility of women, rural, and psychosocially disabled people; SDG target 17.18's call to disaggregate data by disability.

**Key readings**
- Washington Group on Disability Statistics — question sets and rationale.
- WHO, *Global Report on Health Equity for Persons with Disabilities* (2022); *World Report on Disability* (2011).
- Mitra & Sambamoorthi, on disability prevalence and measurement in India.

**Check (sample)**
1. The Washington Group questions count disability by asking about… → *functional difficulty in everyday activities.*
2. India's Census figure (~2.2%) vs the global (~16%) mainly reflects… → *undercounting (stigma, narrow questions, certification), not lower true prevalence.*

## Module 4 — Barriers & Accessibility *(sign-in)*

**Learning objectives**
- Name the types of barriers disabled people face.
- Distinguish universal design, reasonable accommodation and assistive technology.
- Apply accessibility standards, including digital.

**Content**
- **Types of barrier:** physical/built-environment, attitudinal (the deepest), institutional/policy, communication & information, and financial (the extra cost of disability).
- **Accessibility in law & practice:** CRPD Art 9; India's **Accessible India Campaign (Sugamya Bharat Abhiyan, 2015)** covering the built environment, transport and ICT; the **Harmonised Guidelines and Standards for Universal Accessibility in India (2021)**; BIS standards.
- **Three related but distinct ideas.** *Universal design* (Ronald Mace; 7 principles) — designed to be usable by all from the start (a ramp, captions, plain language). *Reasonable accommodation* — an individual adjustment for a specific person in a specific situation (a sign-language interpreter, extra time). *Assistive technology* — devices and software (wheelchairs, screen readers, hearing aids), a major global access gap.
- **Digital accessibility:** WCAG; screen readers and keyboard navigation; India's GIGW (Guidelines for Indian Government Websites); why "we'll make a special version" is usually the wrong answer.

**Key readings**
- CRPD Art 9 + General Comment No. 2.
- Sugamya Bharat Abhiyan; Harmonised Guidelines 2021.
- The Center for Universal Design — the 7 Principles.
- W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) at a glance.

**Check (sample)**
1. Captioning *all* videos so everyone can use the same materials is… → *universal design* (vs a one-off reasonable accommodation).
2. The barrier disabled people most often name as the hardest is… → *attitudinal.*

## Module 5 — Disability & Development: Sectors *(sign-in)*

**Learning objectives**
- Explain the disability–poverty cycle and "leave no one behind."
- Apply a disability lens to education, work, health, social protection and DRR.

**Content**
- **The disability–poverty cycle.** Barriers to school, work, health and services push disabled people into poverty; poverty raises the risk and deepens the consequences of disability. The SDGs' pledge to "leave no one behind" names disability across Goals 4, 8, 10, 11 and 17.
- **Inclusive education.** The ladder from exclusion → segregation → integration → *inclusion*; RTE and RPwD obligations; Universal Design for Learning; the reality of "enrolled but not included."
- **Work & livelihoods.** Barriers and the RPwD reservation; supported employment; self-help groups and inclusive livelihoods; entrepreneurship; the business-case *and* rights case.
- **Health.** Accessibility of mainstream health services (not just "disability health"); **Community-Based Rehabilitation → Community-Based Inclusive Development (CBR/CBID)**; sexual & reproductive health rights; mental health.
- **Social protection.** Disability pensions and the *extra cost* of disability; disability-inclusive social protection.
- **Humanitarian & DRR.** Disabled people die at higher rates in disasters; disability-inclusive disaster risk reduction; the **IASC Guidelines on the Inclusion of Persons with Disabilities in Humanitarian Action**.

**Key readings**
- World Bank, *Disability Inclusion* overview and *Inclusion Matters*.
- IASC Guidelines (2019) on inclusion in humanitarian action.
- CBM / Light for the World disability-inclusive development resources; WHO CBR/CBID guidelines.

**Check (sample)**
1. A child with a disability is enrolled but seated at the back with no support or accessible materials. This is closest to… → *integration, not inclusion.*
2. In disasters, disabled people typically experience… → *higher mortality and exclusion from response* — hence disability-inclusive DRR.

## Module 6 — Intersectionality *(sign-in)*

**Learning objectives**
- Apply intersectionality to disability.
- Recognise how gender, caste, poverty, age and invisible/psychosocial disability compound exclusion.

**Content**
- **Intersectionality** (Kimberlé Crenshaw) applied to disability — a disabled person's barriers are not the *sum* of separate identities but a distinct, compounded experience.
- **Gender & disability.** Disabled women and girls face compounded discrimination, higher rates of gender-based violence, and violations of reproductive rights (including forced sterilisation and institutional abuse). Disabled women are frequently spoken over in both disability and women's movements.
- **Caste & disability** in South Asia; the layered exclusion of Dalit disabled people.
- **Age.** Children (the Child Functioning Module; inclusive early years) and older persons (the overlap of ageing and disability).
- **Invisible & psychosocial disabilities.** Mental health, chronic illness, neurodiversity — high stigma, low recognition, and the risks of coercion and guardianship.
- **Why disability justice centres this:** its principle of leadership by the *most impacted*.

**Key readings**
- Kimberlé Crenshaw, "Mapping the Margins" (intersectionality).
- Anita Ghai on disabled women in India; Rising Flame (Nidhi Goyal) reports on disabled women and violence.
- Sins Invalid, *Skin, Tooth, and Bone* (intersectional disability justice).

**Check (sample)**
1. "A Deaf Dalit woman's barriers are just Deafness + caste + gender added together." → *False — intersectionality describes a distinct, compounded experience.*
2. A group at particularly high, under-recognised risk of gender-based violence is… → *disabled women and girls.*

## Module 7 — Inclusive Programming & MEL *(sign-in)*

**Learning objectives**
- Put the twin-track approach into a programme cycle.
- Enable meaningful participation of OPDs.
- Budget and measure for inclusion.

**Content**
- **Twin-track in practice** — mainstream every activity *and* run disability-specific action; apply it across the whole programme cycle (assessment → design → delivery → MEL).
- **OPDs — Organisations of Persons with Disabilities.** Meaningful participation vs tokenism; how to resource and centre OPDs; "nothing about us without us" operationalised.
- **Accessible by default:** inclusive recruitment, accessible venues/materials/communications, and the accessibility of *your own* organisation.
- **Inclusive budgeting** — accessibility and accommodation costs are line items, not afterthoughts.
- **MEL** — count with the Washington Group set, disaggregate, and build disability-inclusive and disability-specific indicators. Do this hands-on in the **[Disability-Inclusive MEL Lab](/Labs/disability-inclusive-mel-lab.html)**.
- **Safeguarding & do no harm** — heightened risk, consent, referral pathways.

**Key readings**
- CBM, *Disability Inclusive Development Toolkit*.
- UNPRPD / UN Disability Inclusion Strategy (UNDIS) guidance.
- The Disability-Inclusive MEL Lab (companion tool).

**Check (sample)**
1. Inviting one disabled person to a launch photo but not to any decision is an example of… → *tokenism, not meaningful participation.*
2. Accessibility costs in an inclusive budget should be… → *planned line items, not afterthoughts.*

## Module 8 — Voice, Movements & Culture *(sign-in)*

**Learning objectives**
- Trace the global and Indian disability rights movements.
- Recognise harmful vs authentic representation.

**Content**
- **The global movement:** Independent Living; disability pride; Deaf culture and the neurodiversity movement; and **disability justice** (Sins Invalid; Patty Berne, Mia Mingus) as its more recent, intersectional evolution.
- **The Indian movement:** the campaigns of the 1990s that produced the 1995 Act; **Javed Abidi** and NCPEDP; the National Platform for the Rights of the Disabled (NPRD); Disabled Peoples' International; and self-advocacy by people with intellectual and psychosocial disabilities.
- **Representation & culture:** "**inspiration porn**" (Stella Young) and the "supercrip" trope; disabled people as objects of pity or inspiration rather than full humans; authentic, disabled-led representation; disability arts and crip culture; and what "nothing about us without us" demands of media and researchers.

**Key readings**
- Stella Young, "I'm not your inspiration, thank you very much" (TED).
- Alice Wong (ed.), *Disability Visibility*.
- On the Indian movement: writing on Javed Abidi and the disability rights movement in India.
- Robert McRuer, *Crip Theory* (advanced).

**Check (sample)**
1. A poster using a disabled child only to make non-disabled viewers feel inspired is an example of… → *inspiration porn.*
2. "Nothing about us without us" applied to media means… → *disabled-led, authentic representation and decision-making.*

## Module 9 — Practice, Ethics & Allyship *(sign-in)*

**Learning objectives**
- Apply ethical principles to disability work and research.
- Practise genuine allyship and safeguarding.
- Commit to concrete action.

**Content**
- **Ethics.** Accessible informed consent; **supported decision-making** vs substituted decision-making/guardianship (CRPD Art 12); avoiding extractive research; **emancipatory and participatory research** (Oliver) — research *with* and *by*, not just *on*, disabled people.
- **Allyship.** Hiring disabled people (including in your own team); accessibility as the default, not a favour; sharing power and platforms; knowing when to step back; paying disabled experts for their expertise.
- **Safeguarding.** Heightened risk of violence and abuse; consent, confidentiality, and a ready referral pathway.
- **Action & capstone.** Audit your own programme or organisation for barriers and inclusion; make specific, time-bound commitments; and carry your MEL plan out of the **[Disability-Inclusive MEL Lab](/Labs/disability-inclusive-mel-lab.html)**. The course closes where it began: *nothing about us without us* — as a practice, not a slogan.

**Key readings**
- Mike Oliver on emancipatory research.
- CRPD General Comment No. 1 (Art 12, legal capacity).
- Disability-inclusive safeguarding guidance (e.g. CBM / bond).

**Check (sample)**
1. Research designed and led *with* disabled people, serving their priorities, is… → *emancipatory / participatory research.*
2. Under CRPD Art 12, the preferred alternative to guardianship is… → *supported decision-making.*
